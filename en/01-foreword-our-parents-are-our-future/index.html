    Foreword · Older people are our future selves¶

    You may have picked this up for a parent, or for yourself¶

    The two read completely differently.

    People reading it for a parent are in a hurry to find "what do we do"; they will be disappointed if the book holds nothing they can act on. People reading it for themselves mostly noticed something in their own body — a raised white-cell count on one blood test, breathless on the stairs once, a moment of blackout standing up from a squat.

    This book serves both. The main text talks to "you"; at the end of every chapter there is a section called "If you are reading this for a parent".

    Three numbers, not one¶

    Past sixty, everyone hears the same number: life expectancy. It tells you roughly how many years you have.

    But what actually decides whether the last years go well are two other numbers:

    • Healthy life expectancy — the years without major illness, in which you look after yourself
    • Years of independence — the years in which you can eat, use the toilet, wash and go out on your own

    The last one matters most. It is "freedom" itself: only if you can go out alone does "where to go" even arise; only if you can use the toilet alone does dignity arise.

    So this book is not about living long. It is about pushing the number of independent years back. China has no stable official figure for "years of independence" yet; Chapter 1 sets out where things stand. The number probably differs between north and south, between city and countryside.

    Growing old does not happen in one day¶

    Most people feel they got old all at once. It wasn't that moment.

    The body runs a system of compensation: when one function declines, the rest take on more. That system is tough, and it can cover your weak spots for years. So you don't feel the decline — until one day it can't hold: a fall, a hospital stay, an infection.

    That moment is not the start of aging. It is the day the lid came off.

    Which is why this book wants you to read it now. Not because something will happen to you today, but because on the day it does happen, the notes you reach for should not be someone else's.

    Compression of morbidity: squeezing the hard stretch shorter¶

    Geriatric medicine has a term for it: compression of morbidity.

    It means illness and disability will not disappear; what we can do is postpone them and squeeze them shorter. Turn "the hard years" from ten into three, from lying down into still going out on your own.

    The goal doesn't sound ambitious, but it is far more practical than living to a hundred.

    Older people are our future selves¶

    The line is not this book's invention. It comes from Lucy Pollock: "Our parents and grandparents look at us, and their faces say, 'Here I am, your future.'" (Lucy Pollock, The Book About Getting Older (for people who don't want to talk about it), Penguin Books, 2020, ch. 2, p. 24). Chinese edition: Lucy Pollock, 《变老之前要知道》, translated by 一熙, Guangdong People's Publishing House, "万有引力" imprint; bibliographic record https://book.douban.com/subject/38368536/ ; see also 郭晔旻, 《变老之前要知道的那些事》, China Science Daily, 21 August 2026, https://news.sciencenet.cn/sbhtmlnews/2026/8/390983.shtm

    Pollock is a British geriatrician who worked on geriatric wards for more than thirty years. What she describes is a shift of standpoint.

    "Treat the elders of others as your own" treats older people as other people, who deserve our kindness. Treating yourself as a future older person is admitting that this, too, is a stretch of your own life, that you will live inside it — and that you should therefore start arranging for that self now.

    This book is written from the second standpoint. It was shaped by two things. One is the attitude of Pollock's book — the hard part is not the answer, it is saying it out loud (her own words: fear and prejudice mix into 'a toxic mix that kills conversations', ch. 2, p. 25, our translation). The other is an open-source project, HowToLiveBetter (GitHub: eternity4719/HowToLiveBetter), which tags every suggestion with its cost, its benefit, its evidence level and its original source. We borrowed both: the attitude from the book, the format from the project — but none of its numbers.

    The anxiety business¶

    Aging is the easiest topic of all to be harvested in. The reason is not complicated: many people are afraid, information is unevenly spread, and judgement drops when you are frightened.

    So someone will sell you a medical check-up package for several thousand yuan and say it catches everything early; someone will sell you a "memory-enhancing" supplement and say it prevents dementia; someone will take your property deed and say you can collect rent lying down.

    This book's method against that is crude: every entry carries its source, and anything we could not check we would rather leave out. Where a fear is being used by sellers, we mark it separately (see the table below).

    The things people fear most about aging, and how this book meets them¶

    The table below names those fears one by one. The second column says whether the fear is well placed — some are justified, some point in the wrong direction, and some are being used by sellers.

    What you fear Is the fear well placed? Where to look
    One fall and it's over Justified. A fall really is the turning point of later life, but it is preventable, and the most useful measures together cost less than a few hundred yuan Chapter 5
    Wetting yourself and being found out Justified, but you mostly don't have to put up with it. Most cases can be improved; putting up with it is what actually loses you face Chapter 10
    Forgetting things — is it dementia? Wrong direction. There is a wide gap between normal forgetting and disease, and plenty you can do in between Chapter 9
    Something happens at home alone at night and no one knows Justified. But it can be arranged: one-touch alarms, scheduled check-ins, a living-alone agreement Chapter 15
    Your partner goes first, and the days after Justified, and no one can prepare it for you. But the supports in your daily life can be built in advance Chapter 22
    Getting old just means spending money and suffering Wrong direction. Spending has an order — spend the few hundred first, not the tens of thousands Chapters 12, 16
    You will certainly be put in a care home by your children Mostly not. But if you don't say anything, your children will decide for you. That is the real problem Chapter 20
    Being a burden on your children Justified. Which is why nearly half of this book is about making sure you don't need looking after Part 4
    Being left behind because you can't do things on a phone Justified. But it is learnable, not a matter of ability. Booking appointments, hailing rides, paying, topping up utilities — each can be learned Chapter 18
    Being scammed Justified. Scammers now swap faces, clone voices and pose as customer service, and they call when you are alone at home Chapter 18
    Dying in pain, without dignity Justified. And it can be arranged in advance — many people simply don't know that Chapter 21
    "If you don't get this checked it will be too late" / "this prevents dementia" A fear being used by sellers. Expensive check-up packages and "memory-enhancing" supplements mostly cannot show evidence Chapters 1, 12

    The table is here so that you can first say the fear out loud clearly. Naming a fear is not the same as frightening you: every line pairs the fear with whether it is well placed, and with a chapter that meets it.

    A few of them point in the wrong direction, and that is not your fault. Nobody taught us this thirty years ago.

    One honest thing¶

    Nobody can give you a painless answer about aging. What this book can do is turn most of what is in it from "dropped on you suddenly" into "already thought about, and a little prepared for".

    Acknowledgements: where the ideas came from¶

    • Six "standing committee members" of the "earn money, drink tea" group, whose everyday discussions and example were an inspiration. This book is also dedicated to them and their families.
    • Lucy Pollock, The Book About Getting Older (Penguin Books, 2020): this book's standpoint of "treating yourself as a future older person", and the judgement that "the hard part is saying it out loud", come from her book.
    • The open-source project HowToLiveBetter (GitHub: eternity4719/HowToLiveBetter): this book's format of stating cost, benefit, evidence level and original source for every entry follows that project's practice. Neither its content nor its numbers were carried over.
