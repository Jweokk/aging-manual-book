    Chapter 20 · Growing old together: staggered retirement, all day face to face, and one of you going first¶

    The hardest stretch of a marriage is often not the poverty of your younger years, and not the busyness of middle age. It is the few years when the two of you first stop working, one after the other, and then start failing, one after the other.

    That stretch has no marker, no ceremony, and no one tells you it has already begun.

    20.1 What this chapter solves¶

    Growing old alone and growing old as a couple are two different things.

    When you are one, the questions are: who cooks, who takes you to hospital when you are ill, who hears you if you fall at night. When you are two, the questions are another set entirely: one of you has retired and the other is still working, and the household clock no longer matches; both of you have retired and you face the same person twenty-four hours a day; one body gives way first and the other turns from partner into carer; whose hands the money is in, and how the thing that needs saying gets said; and the fact that one day, one of you will go first.

    The nineteen chapters before this one were mostly about how one person arranges things. This chapter is about two.

    Start with a number. In 2020, China had close to 1.5 亿 (150 million) empty-nest older people, of whom 772 万 (7.72 million) were very old and living alone; among the empty-nest older people living alone, nearly seven in ten were widowed A. That is to say, for most people "two" is not two all the way through; it is two first, and then one. This chapter has to cover both ends.

    This chapter does not urge you to make up and does not urge you to split. It does three things only: it points out the places where two people most easily get stuck; it gives you a few sentences you can use as they are; and it tells you who to look for when you are stuck for good.

    20.2 First, see which stage you are in¶

    The same sentence, said at a different stage, has a completely different effect. Get your position straight first.

    Stage What it looks like The main conflict Which section
    One, staggered retirement One has retired, the other is still working; there may be three to five years between you Time no longer matches, housework tilts quietly, "you've got time anyway" 20.3
    Two, both at home Both retired, both bodies still fine Twenty-four hours face to face, who decides, days that are empty 20.4, 20.5
    Three, one gives way first One of you begins to need care Turning from partner into carer, not being able to hold out 20.6, 20.7
    Four, one alone One of you goes first How to get through the first year, whether to look for someone 20.11

    Four things — money, sex, children, conflict — cannot be avoided at any of the four stages; they sit in 20.8 to 20.10, and in 20.12.

    Staggered retirement is becoming more common now, and there is an institutional reason for it. From 2025-01-01, the statutory retirement age in mainland China began to rise gradually: male employees from 60 to 63; female employees who retired at 55 to 58; female employees who retired at 50 to 55. For male employees and the 55 group, one month is added every four months; for the 50 group, one month every two months; the whole change takes fifteen years. Flexible early retirement is also allowed, up to three years early, but not below the original retirement age A.

    The two of you might have retired in the same year; now you are quite likely to be three to five years apart. Those three to five years are the stretch the next section deals with on its own.

    20.3 One retires first, one is still working: the most awkward few years¶

    This stretch is the easiest to overlook, because it doesn't look like a problem: one of you has finally stopped, the other is still rushing for the morning commute.

    What it actually looks like:

    • The retired one has no one to talk to all day, and the thing they most look forward to is the other coming through the door; the working one has already had a full day by the time they come through that door, and the last thing they want is to be asked straight away, "how was your day?"
    • Housework slides quietly onto the one who is at home. "You've got time anyway" — said once it is a joke; said for a year it is a verdict.
    • The retired one's social circle is shrinking; the working one's is still growing. Your topics stop matching: one talks about the people and the goings-on at the workplace, the other about vegetable prices and the weather.
    • A new sore spot appears over money. One pension, one salary — "who is supporting whom" is not said on ordinary days, but it comes out in a quarrel.
    • Intimacy is out of step too: one is free all day, the other has no strength left at night.

    Not one of these is "the marriage going bad." They are unsynchronized rhythms. The clearest case is the half hour after the door opens: one has a day's worth of words held in, the other has had a day's worth of tiredness and doesn't want to talk. Agree in advance that "the first half hour after coming home is not for discussing anything", and a lot of fires never start.

    Don't treat "at home" as "doing nothing"¶

    • What it costs: one conversation, half an hour (money: none | time: half an hour, and after that each of you keeps a stretch of the day for yourself)
    • In short: Retirement is not a transfer to a full-time housework job. The one at home needs their own daytime, and needs a "clocking off" as well.
    • What you can do: ① Divide housework by "whose time is tighter, who is more tired", not by "who is at home". ② The one at home puts things in their own diary — which days of the week they go out, who they see, what they do; write it down, and don't let "waiting for the other to come home" become the axle of the day. ③ The one still working asks at least once a week, "what did you do today?" — and listens to the answer. Not checking up; acknowledging that the other's daytime exists too.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in the division of household labor and in adjusting to retirement.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — retirees who can still arrange their own days. If someone has been low and unwilling to go out since leaving work, read Chapter 11 first.
    • What you get back: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid of "being treated as someone with nothing to do"
    • How this works in China: You don't need to ask anyone's permission. If you really have nothing to do when you go out, ask at the community office whether there is an activities room, a university of the third age, a volunteer post; many sub-districts have them, they just don't advertise.

    Rehearse the "second retirement" in advance¶

    • What it costs: one long talk, an hour (money: none)
    • In short: The staggered years are in fact the window for preparing "both of us retired". Leave the window unused, and when you are both free the problems all arrive at once.
    • What you can do: ① Two or three years before the one still working retires, have one conversation about "how the days will be arranged the day you stop". ② Each of you writes down three things "I want to do when I've retired", swap the lists, and find one you can do together. ③ Agree in advance: the first three months after retiring are a transition, the other one is allowed to find it hard, and the plan is allowed to change. ④ Go through "where we live after retirement" together (see Chapter 15); don't leave it to the last minute.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in retirement planning.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — couples who can still discuss things. If one of you flatly refuses to talk about "stopping work", change the subject to something small first: "when you've retired, what's the first thing you want to do?"
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid that "when he retires we'll have nothing to say to each other"

    Get the money rules straight first¶

    • What it costs: one conversation, half an hour (money: none)
    • In short: One pension and one salary most easily grow a "this is my money" and a "that is your money".
    • What you can do: ① Decide first where the household's joint spending comes from: the mortgage or the rent, utilities, food, medical bills. ② Each of you keeps a sum that needs no reporting, whatever the amount, as long as you both agree to it. ③ Set a line for large spending: above that figure, talk first, then move. ④ Go through the accounts together once a year — check, don't audit.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in household finance; on the boundary of joint property, see Article 1062 of the Civil Code (substance).
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — couples who can still talk about money. If one of you has stopped dealing with the money entirely and doesn't know what is there, see 20.8 first.
    • What you get back: money + relationships — no longer afraid of "guessing about each other over money"

    20.4 Both retired: twenty-four hours together¶

    With retirement, many couples notice something is off within the first month.

    Before, each of you had your own daytime: he left at half past six, she left at eight, they came in one after the other around seven, and the time they actually overlapped in a day was under four hours. Now the two of you are under the same roof from morning to night — he thinks she mops too often, she thinks his television is too loud; he has just sat down and she asks whether he has booked that check-up; she wants to read in peace and he is walking back and forth beside her.

    What you quarrel about is usually not anything big. It is that neither of you has ever prepared for "twenty-four hours a day facing the same person".

    Three things have been squeezed together: time (the dozen or so hours that work used to take up are now entirely empty), power (who runs the household, who decides — something you have not renegotiated for many years before retirement), and space (the flat is the same flat, and the hours the two of you spend inside it have doubled). Squeezed together, they come out through the smallest gaps.

    Time of your own¶

    • What it costs: two hours a week, nothing spent (money: none | time: two hours each a week, each on your own)
    • In short: Twenty-four hours together is not intimacy, it is compression. A person needs a stretch of time when nobody is looking for them; take that away and the temper comes.
    • What you can do: ① Each of you names one "slot every day when I am not to be disturbed" — his is seven to nine in the morning with the newspaper, hers is three to five in the afternoon out walking — write them down and stick the paper on the fridge. ② In that slot, unless it is urgent, you don't call the other. ③ Each of you has somewhere you can close a door, even if it is one chair on the balcony. ④ Going out does not need reporting to the minute: say where you are going and when you will be back.
    • Where this comes from: C | Giving each other independent time and space is common practice in marriage and family therapy; it is not the conclusion of any one study.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — couples who are suddenly together for long stretches after retirement and have not yet quarreled to the point of separating. If one of you is already bedbound and needs watching at all times, see 20.6.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid that "the more we're together, the more irritating it gets"

    Three agreements that are not up for argument¶

    • What it costs: one conversation, half an hour (money: none | time: half an hour; if you don't finish, do it in two sittings)
    • In short: After retirement, most quarrels are about who decides. Settle three things in advance and you save yourself half the fires.
    • What you can do: ① Money: each of you keeps a monthly sum that needs no reporting, whatever amount you can both accept. ② Housework: divide by "who is good at it, who is not tired", not by "who ought to". ③ Children: money given to the children, and the boundaries of helping with grandchildren — the two of you agree the line first, then say it to the outside; don't have one of you promise first and leave the other to play the bad cop.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in household finance and the division of housework.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — when the two of you can still sit down and talk. If it turns into a quarrel the moment you open your mouths, do the previous item, "time of your own", first, bring the heat down, and then talk.
    • What you get back: money + relationships — no longer afraid of "guessing about each other over money" and "quarreling over the children"

    Each of you needs a "third place"¶

    • What it costs: going out a few times a week (money: depends where; most are free or very cheap)
    • In short: Home is the first place, your children's home is the second, and a person also needs a third — a place that belongs neither to the family nor to the workplace, only to you: an activities room, a park, a university of the third age, a card corner, a volunteer post, the wet market, any of them will do.
    • What you can do: ① Each of you finds one and goes a fixed number of times a week. ② When you go, don't just sit there — know one or two people, say a few words. ③ Don't always go to the same place together — with a third place each, you have something to say when you come home. ④ Each of you should have at least two people you can talk to on your own, and the two of you at least one couple you can have a meal with. Treating the other as your only social contact is where many marriages start to narrow.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice on social connection and quality of life in later years.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who can still get out. If getting out is hard, read Chapter 5 (falls) and Chapter 15 (where to live) first, and settle the going-out problem.
    • What you get back: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid that "the two of us are tied together and the world keeps narrowing"

    Sleeping can be done in separate beds¶

    • What it costs: nothing (money: none)
    • In short: As you age, sleep gets lighter, you get up more often at night, and the snoring gets louder. Force it into one bed and neither of you sleeps well; sleep badly and you quarrel more easily by day.
    • What you can do: ① Try "separate beds, same room" first — two beds, one room, talk a while before sleeping. ② If there isn't room, separate rooms are fine too; keep ten minutes sitting together before bed. ③ If the snoring is loud, if you wake up choking at night, if you are sleepy all day, go to a respiratory department or a sleep clinic and have it checked — don't just call it "sleeping deeply".
    • Where this comes from: C | Separate beds are the realistic choice in many households; snoring with waking and choking needs a doctor — that is the standard clinical judgment.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — when you both agree. If one of you reads separate beds as "he doesn't want me anymore", talk it through before you do it.
    • What you get back: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid of "sleeping badly and having no energy by day"

    20.5 Make the days into "doing something together"¶

    The hardest thing after retirement is not being busy; it is having nothing that has to be done.

    When you were working, the day was arranged for you: what time to get up, where to go, who to see, when you could rest. Retire, and all that scaffolding is pulled away within a day. The day slides automatically toward three things: the television, the phone, the three meals. Three months later you find that the two of you are indeed together every day, but not one thing in the day is "something we did together".

    Raising the quality of life does not come from "spending more time with each other" — being present is not the same as being together. It comes from doing one thing together that moves forward: learning something, walking a route, sorting something out, going somewhere. With progress there is something to talk about; with something to talk about, the day is not empty.

    Fifteen minutes a week: the two of you, meeting¶

    • What it costs: fifteen minutes a week (money: none)
    • In short: Not a heart-to-heart; a check-in. Four questions, and when they are answered you stop. Don't let it turn into a criticism session.
    • What you can do: ① Fix a time, Sunday after supper for instance. ② Take the four questions in turn: What was the happiest thing for you this week? What made you uncomfortable this week? What can I do to help you next week? Is there something you have wanted to say and haven't? ③ Three rules: don't interrupt, don't defend yourself, don't bring up old scores. ④ While one of you is speaking the other only listens, and at the end says one sentence: "I've heard you."
    • Where this comes from: C | Structured weekly communication is a common practice in marriage and family therapy.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — two people who are still willing to talk. If there has been a long cold war and it turns into a quarrel as soon as you open your mouths, cut the time to five minutes first and answer only the first question.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid of "having nothing to say" and "it piles up until it explodes"

    One thing a year that you will remember afterwards¶

    • What it costs: depends what it is (money: anywhere from a few dozen to a few thousand yuan | time: once a year)
    • In short: The year needs a few markers, or it passes and you can't recall a thing.
    • What you can do: ① At the start of the year, fix one concrete thing: a trip to some place, the old photographs made into an album, a walk around the old hometown, learning a dance together, an old piece of furniture repaired. ② Fix the month and write it in the calendar. ③ Don't set "travel the world"; set "go to Suzhou this October". ④ At the end of the year, go through the photographs once, and set the next one.
    • Where this comes from: C | Shared goals and shared memories are the usual glue in a long relationship.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who can still go out and still use their hands. If moving about is hard, change "go to some place" into "make something at home" — sorting an album together counts.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid that "the days leave no trace"

    Learn something new together¶

    • What it costs: a few hundred yuan a term (money: a university of the third age or a community course, usually not expensive | time: once or twice a week)
    • In short: Learning something together from zero, and one of you teaching the other, are two different things. The first makes you companions; the second makes you teacher and pupil, and easily turns into a new hierarchy.
    • What you can do: ① Choose something neither of you can do: phone photography, choir, tai chi, calligraphy, cooking, a smartphone class. ② Sign up together and sit together. ③ At home, look at each other's work; don't mark it. ④ It doesn't matter if you don't get it — you go there to have something to do and someone to talk to.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice at universities of the third age and community courses.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who can still go out, and can hear and see. If hearing is poor, deal with the hearing aid first (see Chapter 3), otherwise the class is a punishment.
    • What you get back: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid of "having nothing in common to talk about"

    20.6 The two of you don't age at the same speed: how the roles change¶

    Most couples do not go down at the same time. The usual pattern is this: one of you has the stroke first, breaks a hip first, gets the diagnosis first, and the other is still in decent shape — so the sentence "I'll look after you" gets said.

    That sentence is sincere. The trouble is that it usually has no end date, no division of labor and no substitute. Three years on, the one giving the care collapses first — poor sleep, high blood pressure, depression, a fall of their own. And then both of you go down together.

    When the roles change, the hardest part is not the physical work; it is the three things that cannot be said out loud: I'm tired. I need someone to help. Sometimes I wish you would get better faster, so that I could have one day off. Many people go a whole lifetime without saying these three sentences to their partner.

    Change "I'll do it" into "let's divide it"¶

    • What it costs: one long conversation, an hour (money: none | time: an hour, and it may take several goes)
    • In short: One person taking it all on looks like love; in fact it means there is no substitute. Care is a long job, and it has to be arranged by "how long can I keep this up", not by "who loves whom more".
    • What you can do: ① List the four things that must be done every day — food, medicine, hygiene, medical visits — write down who does it and on which day, and stick it on the wall. ② Agree on a code phrase for "I need to hand over"; when it is said, the other one, or a child, has to take over, and no one says "just hold on a bit longer". ③ Fix the substitutes in advance: children, relatives, neighbors, paid helpers — who covers which part. ④ The carer has a check-up of their own once a year, with blood pressure, sleep and mood on the list.
    • Where this comes from: C | Dividing care work is common practice; counting the carer into the care plan is a consensus in the eldercare field.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — couples where one has just become disabled and the other can still move about. If both of you are already disabled, see Chapter 17.
    • What you get back: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid of "dragging the other one down with me"

    Get outside help from the start; don't wait until you can't hold out¶

    • What it costs: asking first, which need not cost anything (money: home services are charged by the visit or by the month, and differ a great deal from place to place)
    • In short: Bringing someone in is not "not loving anymore"; it is what lets this go on for years. Wait until the carer collapses and it is usually too late.
    • What you can do: ① Ask the community residents' committee or the sub-district eldercare service window first: is there help with bathing at home, with meals, with cleaning, with going to the hospital. ② Ask whether there is "respite care" — someone to take over for a few days so that you can rest (see Chapter 17). ③ For the disability assessment and the long-term care insurance benefits, ask the local medical insurance bureau (see Chapter 16). ④ Before you hire anyone, be clear about what they do, for how long, at what price, and how you change them — better on paper.
    • Where this comes from: C | Home services and respite services from the community are common practice. WHO lists "services that reduce the burden on carers" as one of the most promising measures for preventing abuse of older people A.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — the one who is still willing to let a stranger through the door. If they flatly refuse, start with the lightest thing, "a cleaner who only does the cleaning".
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid of "one person carrying it until they collapse"

    20.7 The caregiving spouse: don't put yourself in as well¶

    A carer most easily falls into three pits, and all of them have to do with "love".

    The first pit: doing everything yourself. You don't trust outsiders, you think "nobody is as careful as I am". The result is twenty-four hours on call, without a single day off.

    The second pit: refusing to hire anyone. You are afraid a relative will say "only a few years and you're already tired of it", afraid a child will say "you're at home all day anyway". So you force yourself on, until you fall ill too.

    The third pit: treating "a care home" as failure. You feel that sending them there means you did not try hard enough. But some care genuinely cannot be done at home — turning, suctioning, pressure-sore care; done badly, it can kill.

    With care, lasting a long time matters more than doing it perfectly.

    Check three signals first, then talk about how to save yourself¶

    • What it costs: one self-check, ten minutes (money: none)
    • In short: Before a carer collapses there are usually three signals. See one, and it is time to act.
    • What you can do: ① Sleep: two weeks running of trouble falling asleep, waking early, and thinking about the patient the moment you wake. ② Can't get out: a whole month without once leaving the patient for more than two hours. ③ Hardness in the heart: now and then the thought "if only this day didn't exist", and then being frightened by yourself. If you have one of the three, do two things: find someone to take over, and go to the geriatrics or psychology department of a hospital and say it once. ④ Arrange a full check-up for yourself every year — don't only go along to the patient's.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common signs of carer burden and the direction of intervention are standard thinking in the eldercare field.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people currently caring for a spouse. If thoughts of harming yourself or the other person appear, don't wait: find someone at once — call the 12356 psychological assistance hotline, or go straight to a psychiatric department.
    • What you get back: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid that "I'll be the first to go"

    20.8 Money: the joint account, the separate accounts, and the risk of one person handling it all¶

    In old age, money tends to go to one of two extremes: everything mixed together so that neither of you can say what is what; or one of you handling it all while the other has no idea how much there is, where it is, or what the password is.

    With the first, a quarrel has no facts to stand on; with the second, the moment the one handling the money falls ill or dies, the other doesn't even know which card the mortgage comes out of.

    Split the money into three pots, and both of you know about all three¶

    • What it costs: one stocktaking, two hours (money: none)
    • In short: Money has two extremes to fear: everything mixed together so that neither of you can say what is what; or one of you handling it all while the other doesn't know which card the mortgage comes out of.
    • What you can do: ① Three pots: joint spending (mortgage or rent, utilities, food, medical bills, out of one joint account), pocket money each (a sum a month each, no questions about what it is for), emergency money (both of you know it, both of you can take it out; for hospital stays, house repairs, sudden events). ② Make a list: which accounts, which bank, roughly how much, who handles it. ③ Don't write the passwords on the list, but make sure the other one knows where to find them. ④ Go through the accounts together once a year — check, don't audit. ⑤ Set one rule for large transfers: above a certain figure, wait 24 hours before doing it — that is both a chance for the couple to talk and a defense against scams (see Chapter 18).
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in separating household money and keeping it transparent; on joint property during the marriage, see Article 1062 of the Civil Code (substance).
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — while both of you still understand the household's finances. If one of you already has cognitive decline, move to designated guardianship — mainland China's counterpart to a durable power of attorney — and to authorization (see Chapter 19 and Chapter 22).
    • What you get back: money + relationships — no longer afraid of "not knowing where the money is" and "guessing about each other over money"

    20.9 Sex, intimacy and the changes in your body¶

    In old age this subject is wrapped in two layers of silence. One is embarrassment at speaking of it; the other is the assumption that "when you're old there should be none of that".

    The fact is: part of it changes, not all of it. Some people are still having sex in their seventies; some stop in their fifties. Both are normal. What genuinely needs attention is sudden change and pain — those are often not "getting old", they are illness.

    This section does not say how to treat anything. It says only: which situations need a doctor, which department to register with, and what to ask once you are in the room.

    Which changes are not "normal for old age"¶

    • What it costs: one registration (money: the standard outpatient registration fee | time: half a day)
    • In short: Age slows everything down, but it does not make everything hurt, or bleed, or suddenly get worse.
    • What you can do: ① If these appear, get them checked: pain; bleeding (any bleeding in a woman after menopause must be checked); something that was manageable and has clearly got worse in a short time; a sudden big change in mood, sleeplessness, irritability. ② Which department: women, gynecology or geriatrics; men, urology or geriatrics; either of you can start by asking at geriatrics. ③ Once inside, ask three things: "Is this normal aging, or does it need investigating?" "Do I need medication, and will it affect my blood pressure or my heart?" "How often should I be checked?" ④ If the doctor asks about sex, answer honestly — they are not asking out of curiosity, they are judging another illness.
    • Where this comes from: C | Which changes need a doctor and which department to go to rest on standard clinical judgment; this chapter does not deal with specific drugs or doses.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who can still get to a clinic. If getting about is hard, ask the community health center or your contracted family doctor whether they can come to the house or ask on your behalf (see Chapter 17).
    • What you get back: self-reliance + dignity — no longer afraid of "too embarrassed to ask, until it becomes something serious"

    How you start matters more than what you say¶

    • What it costs: one evening (money: none)
    • In short: When this conversation goes wrong it is usually not because of "not being able to"; it is because the way you start sounds like an inspection.
    • What you can do: ① Start with "I": "I've been missing you lately", not "are you still able to". ② Say first that it is not a demand: "I'm not asking for anything, I want to talk about it with you." ③ Pick an evening with no clock running — not after a tiring day, not just after a quarrel, not after drinking. ④ If the other refuses, don't take it as a verdict — you can bring it up another day, or simply hold each other and sleep. ⑤ If you truly cannot talk about it and you are both miserable, go to the geriatrics or psychology department of a hospital and let a third person be in the room.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in intimate communication.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — while you are both still willing to talk. If one of you has avoided it for years and flares up at the mention, read 20.12 first, to rule out control and harm.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid that "this is a thing that can't be said"

    20.10 The boundary with your children: the partner comes first¶

    In old age the order in many families quietly turns upside down: the children become the referee and the partner becomes the defendant. "You have a word with your father about this", "your mother's been buying rubbish again" — the words are said to a child, and what gets hurt is the two of you.

    There is one ordering that saves a great deal of trouble if you settle it early: what belongs to the two of you, the two of you decide first; your children are advisers, not judges.

    Who decides, and the boundaries on money and grandchildren: say it in advance¶

    • What it costs: one family conversation, an hour (money: none)
    • In short: Children who put in money and effort easily take the decisions along with them; money given to children without writing it down will give people something to say later, between siblings and between spouses.
    • What you can do: ① The two of you agree first: where to live, how the money is spent, whether to bring in help — these we decide. Errands, finding a hospital, comparing prices — we can take the children's advice. ② Say one clear sentence to the children: "the two of us will talk this over first, and tell you when we're done." ③ Major medical decisions get written down in advance as an advance directive (see Chapter 22), so that the children are not guessing at the emergency room door. ④ Large gifts of money get a sheet of paper: how much, to whom, a gift or a loan, whether it is to be repaid. ⑤ On looking after grandchildren, be clear about three things: for how long, how many days a week, and under what circumstances it ends.
    • Where this comes from: C | The common practice in family decisions and money across the generations; on spouses supporting and respecting each other, see Articles 1043 and 1059 of the Civil Code (substance).
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who can still express what they want. If they can no longer, move to designated guardianship (Article 33 of the Civil Code) and advance medical decisions.
    • What you get back: dignity + money + relationships — no longer afraid of "being arranged by the children" and "giving the money and still being blamed"

    20.11 One of you goes first: the first year of widowhood¶

    The first year is the most chaotic: many tasks, a mind in a fog, many relatives, many decisions to make. In that stretch you are most easily pushed along by two things — the opinions of relatives, and the urge to "get it all sorted out quickly".

    There is one principle worth writing down and sticking on the fridge: in the first year, make no big irreversible decision. Don't sell the flat, don't move, don't hand out large sums, don't remarry. Wait a year, and much of it will become clear on its own.

    What has to be done in the first three months¶

    • What it costs: several trips out (money: most procedures are free of charge | time: one to three months)
    • In short: These things have deadlines; dragging them out can stop payments and affect inheritance. Do them in order, and don't panic.
    • What you can do: ① Get the death certificate first (hospital, community or police, depending on where the death happened). ② Cancel the household registration. ③ Go to the social insurance office to stop and settle the pension and the medical insurance, and ask clearly about the funeral allowance and survivor benefits (the standards differ from place to place). ④ Bank accounts, property, insurance, vehicles: ask, one by one, how to transfer them or claim. ⑤ Mobile number, messaging and payment accounts, utilities: transfer or close them. ⑥ If you don't know where to start, call 12345 and ask about local procedures, or ask at the community office.
    • Where this comes from: C | A standard checklist of the paperwork that follows a death; the specific procedures and deadlines differ from place to place, and the local office is authoritative.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — anyone who has been widowed. If there is a dispute over the estate, go straight to a lawyer or to legal aid; don't pass messages between relatives.
    • What you get back: money + dignity — no longer afraid of "missing something that had to be done"

    Whether to look for someone, and the money in a remarriage¶

    • What it costs: a year to think it through; advice if you actually do it (money: a lawyer's consultation, from a few hundred yuan)
    • In short: Looking for someone again is not shameful, and it is no longer rare. The hard part is not the feeling; it is the money, the flat and the children.
    • What you can do: ① Ask yourself one question first: do I want someone to talk to, or someone to look after me? The two answers lead different ways. ② If you do go ahead, settle three things before the wedding: each of your property, where you will live, and who will care for whom later. ③ Legal arrangements you can make: a prenuptial property agreement, a will, a residence right for the other person (Book II (Property), Part III, Chapter 14 of the Civil Code), designated guardianship (Article 33, mainland China's counterpart to a durable power of attorney). ④ When the children object, it is usually fear that the flat and the money will disappear — laying the arrangements out on the table works better than arguing. ⑤ For how to actually do it, go to a lawyer or to the local legal aid center (12348); don't go by what relatives say.
    • Where this comes from: A | The text of Article 33 of the Civil Code (designated guardianship) has been checked against the original; on residence rights see Chapter 14. C | The common practice in property arrangements in remarried families.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people who still have full civil capacity. Once cognition has declined, these things can no longer be done.
    • What you get back: relationships + money — no longer afraid that "finding someone else will scramble the family property"

    20.12 When the relationship itself is hurting you: how to recognize it, who to go to¶

    Not all harm happens out in the street. In old age the harm is often at home, and the person causing it may be your partner, or it may be your children.

    A WHO review estimates that about 15.7% of people over 60 have suffered some form of abuse in the past year — roughly one in six A. It also points out that the type of relationship and marital status affect the risk — that is, being "the one you've grown old with" can be a protection, and can also be where the risk sits.

    This section covers two things only: how to recognize it, and who to go to.

    Five signals¶

    • What it costs: nothing (money: none)
    • In short: Abuse of older people is not only being hit. Any one of the five below is worth taking seriously.
    • What you can do: ① Money controlled: the pension card taken away, every cent spent needing permission, being pushed into transfers or signatures. ② Cut off: not allowed to see friends, not allowed to answer the phone, not allowed out. ③ Humiliated: sworn at in front of others, told "why don't you just die", belittled over years. ④ Pushed about: grabbed, slapped, pushed over, even if "no injury was caused". ⑤ Punishment in the name of care: no food, no sleep, no medicine, pain inflicted on purpose. If you see these, secure your safety first, then ask for help.
    • Where this comes from: A | WHO, Abuse of Older People fact sheet; the types of abuse include physical, psychological, financial, sexual abuse and neglect A.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — anyone, at any age, in any marital status. It holds just the same when the person causing it is your partner — "a family matter" is not a reason.
    • What you get back: dignity + safety — no longer afraid that "no one will believe me if I say it"

    Who to go to, and how to keep evidence¶

    • What it costs: no money (money: none)
    • In short: You can't carry this kind of thing on your own. Finding one person who can walk through the door matters more than anything.
    • What you can do: ① If there is immediate danger, call 110. ② The women's rights hotline 12338 (All-China Women's Federation), legal aid 12348. ③ Go to the community residents' committee or the village committee — they have the standing to come to the house, to mediate, and to bring in the civil affairs department. ④ Keep evidence: photographs of injuries, medical records, transfer records, chat records, recordings (record on your own phone; don't break into someone else's home). ⑤ Tell one person you trust: a child, a relative, an old colleague, a neighbor. Don't keep it bottled up alone.
    • Where this comes from: C | The help channels are public information, and which of them are open differs from place to place; where personal safety is involved, the police and the judicial authorities are authoritative.
    • Who this is for: Who this holds for — people still hesitating about whether to speak. Do one thing first: put your ID card, your bank cards and your regular medicines in one bag, and keep it somewhere you can reach.
    • What you get back: safety + dignity — no longer afraid that "there is nowhere left to turn"

    20.13 How this works in China: four places that can give you an answer¶

    Most of this chapter is about what to do inside the household. When you actually need someone's help, there are four places in mainland China that can give you an answer, with no favors to call in.

    1. The community residents' committee, the village committee, the sub-district eldercare service window: help with bathing at home, meals, cleaning, going with you to the hospital, age-friendly home renovation, respite care — start asking here. Policies differ from place to place; one question, "what is there here?", is worth more than three days of searching online.
    2. The community health center, the contracted family doctor: repeat prescriptions for chronic conditions, referrals, home visits, check-ups. Both your blood pressure and your blood sugar ought to have a record here.
    3. The geriatrics and psychology departments of a hospital: mood, sleep, memory, sex — you can ask about all of them here. The advantage of geriatrics is that it looks at you as one person, rather than dividing you up by organ.
    4. Legal aid centers (12348), the women's federation (12338), the civil affairs department's marriage and family counseling: property, remarriage, wills, violence, guardianship — these are the people to go to for such things. Most marriage registration offices now offer marriage and family counseling, free of charge.

    One more place is often forgotten: the trade union or the retired-staff office of your former work unit. If the first two years of retirement sit badly, ask them whether there are activities, whether there is anyone to talk to — better than sitting at home stewing.

    20.14 If you can only do one thing today¶

    Tonight, after supper, say one sentence to the one you have grown old with: "Let's talk for fifteen minutes, you and me."

    Then ask four questions, one round each:

    1. What was the happiest thing for you this week?
    2. What made you uncomfortable this week?
    3. What can I do to help you next week?
    4. Is there something you have wanted to say and haven't?

    There are only three rules: don't interrupt, don't defend yourself, don't bring up old scores.

    When the fifteen minutes are up, stop. Come back to it next week.

    20.15 If you are reading this for your parents¶

    • Don't take sides. When they quarrel, you listen, without judging. The moment you judge, the other one becomes the outsider.
    • Don't be the messenger. "My mother says…" — every time you pass it on, it does damage. Let them say it themselves.
    • Leave them space. After retirement the two of them are squeezed together, and what they lack most is time of their own. When you go home, don't overturn their whole rhythm.
    • Watch for harm. If one of them has long been controlled over money, cut off, humiliated, don't write it off as "the old couple's business". Call the police or go to the community if you have to.
    • Don't decide for them. Where to live, how to be treated — ask first what they want; if they can't say clearly, go to the advance decisions in Chapter 22.

    20.16 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • There are very few nationwide surveys of relationship quality among older couples in China. Most of the "what you can do" in this chapter comes from common practice in marriage and family therapy and in eldercare (grade C), not from large domestic samples. Which of it works particularly well in Chinese households still needs interviews to establish.
    • On sexual health, China has no unified public-information standard aimed at older people, and clinics differ a great deal from place to place. This chapter says only which changes to have checked, which department to go to, and what to ask; the diagnosis and any medication have to be decided by a doctor.
    • On property arrangements in remarriage, courts and registration practice differ from place to place. What this chapter gives you is the legal tools (a property agreement, a will, a residence right, designated guardianship); a particular case needs a lawyer.
