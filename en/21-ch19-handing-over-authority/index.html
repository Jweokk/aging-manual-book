    Chapter 19 · Setting permissions: hand authority over while you are clear-headed¶

    Today you can sign your own admission consent, take out your own money, decide for yourself whether to have that operation. These things look like yours, but they all rest on the same thing — the standing in your own name to say "I decide."

    That standing is not for life. One stroke, one accident, or a memory that slips a little at a time can take it away without notice. Once it has gone, the family faces one question: who decides for you from now on. If you wrote the answer down early, things go the way you wrote them. If you didn't, they go in the order the law sets out.

    19.1 What this chapter solves¶

    Signing, withdrawing money, being admitted to hospital, selling a flat, opening a phone number — in all of these the law recognizes the will of "the person themselves." While your mind is clear, you decide.

    The trouble is that the status of "the person themselves" can disappear without warning: you are here, but you don't recognize your family, can't speak clearly, can't sign. Then there are only two outcomes — if you wrote down early who decides for you, things go the way you wrote; if not, they go in the order the law sets out.

    This chapter is about that gap: while you are still clear-headed, change "who decides for me" from the law's default answer to your own answer.

    All of this is the mainland-China system. The nearest things an English reader will know are a power of attorney (someone acting for you on money and property), an advance directive or living will (your wishes about treatment), and guardianship (someone appointed to decide for you when you cannot). None of them maps exactly onto what follows; each section gives the Chinese instrument and who handles it.

    19.2 Once you are disabled, a lot of it cannot be fixed up afterwards¶

    • What it costs: nothing today; leave it until you have to fix it up and what it costs is time running back and forth and several trips (money: next to nothing | time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: Designated guardianship, a will, a power of attorney — all share one precondition: when you do it, you have to be a "person with full civil capacity", meaning you know what you are signing. Once you are found unable to understand, that route of fixing it up yourself is basically closed, and what is left is the slower, more wounding route of a court appointment.
    • What you can do: Treat "while you are clear-headed" as a point in time: doing it in your sixties, while your health is still decent, is the ordinary course. Leave it until there are signs of dementia and the notary office will check again and again whether you really understand, and plenty of people stall right there.
    • Where this comes from: A | This is the law's hard requirement for "capacity" (Civil Code, Articles 33 and 21), not a notary office making things difficult. But notary offices differ in how strictly they examine an older person's capacity, and there is no nationwide standard: the same older person may be checked repeatedly at one office and get it done in a single visit at another. "Basically cannot be done" describes the trend, not an absolute rule.
    • Who this is for: Adults who can still decide for themselves today. If there is already a clear diagnosis of dementia, this section no longer applies — see 19.10.
    • What you get back: dignity + relationships — no longer afraid that "someone will take the decisions away from me"
    • How this works in China: Ask your local notary office how to do designated guardianship and a notarized will; when fixing it up is hard, go to court (see 19.10). The latest local wording prevails.

    19.3 Designated guardianship: while you are clear-headed, name the person who speaks for you¶

    • What it costs: one or two visits to a notary office (money: a few hundred to a few thousand yuan, varying widely by place | time: half a day to a day | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: Designated guardianship (yiding jianhu) — in plain words: while you are still clear-headed, you and someone you trust (an organization works too) sign a written agreement that on the day I can't understand things or can't sign, you speak for me and decide for me. Article 33 of the Civil Code is what makes this work. This is the mainland-China instrument; the nearest thing readers elsewhere will know is a durable power of attorney, except that here it is a guardianship, and no court is involved in setting it up.
    • What you can do: ① Choose the person first (see 19.4); ② the two of you go to the notary office together and say you want to notarize a "designated guardianship agreement"; ③ put in everything that should be agreed: when it takes effect, what it covers (medical decisions, money, day-to-day care), how it can be changed, how it can be revoked; ④ keep one notarized copy each, and put a photocopy in the family document folder.
    • Where this comes from: A | Article 33 of the Civil Code states: an adult with full civil capacity may, in writing, agree in advance with a close relative or another individual or organization willing to act as guardian that when he loses civil capacity in whole or in part, that person will carry out the duties of guardian. But what it governs is guardianship after you become disabled; it is not an authorization for the other person to touch your money now, and it does not override what you say at the time.
    • Who this is for: People who can still sign for themselves today. Childless couples, remarried couples, parents who have lost their only child, and families with tight relations do this more often, because it turns "whom the law puts first in line" into "whom I choose myself." The guardian can be a person or an organization.
    • What you get back: dignity + relationships — no longer afraid that "when that day comes, no one can speak up for me"
    • How this works in China: Go to your local notary office for the "designated guardianship agreement" notarization. Both sides bring ID cards, household registers and other identity materials; where property disposal is involved, bring the property documents (a deed, for example); bring the text of the agreement you have worked out — after review the notary can help you revise it, or draft it for you. Where to ask and what it costs: call the local notary office for a price first, or look it up on the China Notary Network or the Ministry of Justice government service platform. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.4 Choosing your agent: four things to check¶

    • What it costs: one serious think, one question asked face to face (money: none | time: one long conversation | effort: none | do you have to ask: you have to ask, it is a little awkward — you are asking a favor)
    • In short: What you are picking for designated guardianship is not "the richest" or "the highest-ranking", but the person who "will actually be able to speak up, and speak your meaning." Pick wrong and it is more trouble than picking nobody.
    • What you can do: Four things, looked at side by side —
    • They listen to you; they don't decide for you. Ask them to their face: "If I get confused and I say no treatment, will you do as I say?"
    • They have the strength, and they are there. They can get there in time and can run the paperwork. An eighty-year-old spouse may not be able to carry it.
    • They have nothing to do with your money, or the relationship is out in the open. Ask in advance: on the day they manage money for you, who watches them.
    • Leave a substitute. Agree "if he can't do it, who takes over."
    • Where this comes from: C | These four are common practice, and also the points notary review commonly looks at (is the intent genuine, is the person capable, is there a conflict of interest); but which one matters most varies from person to person, and no research gives a single answer.
    • Who this is for: People who still have someone to choose from. If there is no one you trust at all, you can split the job: money to an institution, personal care to relatives.
    • What you get back: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid of "trusting the wrong person", or "the family falling out over this little bit of property"
    • How this works in China: Talk these four points over with a notary once; where money duties are involved, agree in advance who supervises and how they check. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.5 Advance directives: what Shenzhen writes is "respect", not "must carry out"¶

    • What it costs: one long conversation, plus one notarization or witnessing (money: a few hundred yuan for the notarization | time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to ask: hard to ask — you have to say plainly "don't resuscitate me when the time comes")
    • In short: A living will — an advance directive — is written while you are clear-headed: at the end, do you want a tube, do you want CPR, do you want the ICU. Right now Shenzhen is the only place in the country with a dedicated rule; what Shenzhen writes is: when a medical institution gives treatment at that point, it should respect the patient's expressed wishes — "respect", not "must carry out." It does not say "the hospital must comply"; nor is it euthanasia — it is about whether to have that set of traumatic rescue measures.
    • What you can do: ① Talk those few sentences through with your family and with the doctor you usually see; ② write it down; ③ for a clear basis, ask the local health commission or the palliative care department of a large hospital; in Shenzhen you can ask the directory kept by the Shenzhen living will promotion association. ④ Outside Shenzhen, you can write it following the Beijing Living Will Promotion Association's "My Five Wishes" — the most widely circulated fill-in template in the country, which can be filled in online and printed from the "Choice and Dignity" website (in use since 2009).
    • Where this comes from: A | There is statutory text (Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulations, Article 78). But outside Shenzhen there is no dedicated legislation; a wishes document that is clearly written, witnessed, and can be produced at any time is only a "soft guarantee" — it helps doctors and family know how you think, it is not a hard guarantee.
    • Who this is for: People who can still communicate and decide for themselves. Shenzhen requires: notarized, or witnessed by two or more people not involved in treating you; written, audio or video all count.
    • What you get back: dignity — no longer afraid of "being in pain, strung along by machines, the family falling out over saving me"
    • How this works in China: Talk, write, notarize or have it witnessed; ask the local health commission or the palliative care department of a large hospital. The latest local wording prevails. For matters to do with medical decisions, see Chapter 22.

    19.6 How to make a will that does not get thrown out¶

    • What it costs: self-written costs nothing; a printed will is one to a few hundred yuan; a notarized will is a few hundred yuan, and for people aged 80 and over the service fee is waived the first time (Fagai Jiage [2021] No. 1081) (time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: Wills get thrown out mostly not because you wrote down the wrong person, but because the form was wrong. A few common reasons: the wrong witnesses (letting someone who will inherit a share act as witness, for instance); a missing date or signature; a printed will not signed on every page; being talked into changing it while you sign.
    • What you can do: Pick one form and do it fully — self-written means your own handwriting, your signature, and the year, month and day; a will written down by someone else, or a printed one, needs two or more witnesses, and a printed one also needs the testator and the witnesses to sign every page and note the year, month and day; if that is too much trouble, go to a notary office and have the will notarized.
    • Where this comes from: A | The text of the statutes (Civil Code, Articles 1134–1139). But if you made several wills and they conflict, the last one governs (Article 1142); a will made under fraud or coercion is void (Article 1143); and a will still has to leave a necessary share to an heir who "lacks the ability to work and has no source of income" (Article 1141).
    • Who this is for: People whose minds are clear and who can express themselves. Write the content concretely: which flat, which account, to whom.
    • What you get back: relationships + money — no longer afraid that "what I wrote won't count in the end"
    • How this works in China: Write it yourself, or go to a notary office for a notarized will (free the first time at 80 and over); if you are unsure, ask the notary office or a lawyer. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.7 The family index sheet: one page so the whole family can find things¶

    • What it costs: one pen, half an hour (money: none | time: half an hour | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: What you fear most is not that the things don't exist, but that "only you know where they are." The moment you go into hospital the whole family is turning the place upside down, and the policy, the deed, the bank cards and the notarized papers cannot be found. The fix is crude — write an index sheet.
    • What you can do: Fill in this table, then read it aloud to one family member so that they know where it is. | Category | What | Where it is | Who knows | How to get it in an emergency | | ID and papers | ID card, household register, property deed | | | | | Money | Bank cards, passbooks, insurance policies, funds and securities | | | | | Legal documents | Designated guardianship, will, living will, power of attorney | | | | | Keys and passwords | Safe, spare house key | | passwords: a hint only | | | People and institutions | Attending doctor, community, lawyer, notary | | | |
    • Where this comes from: C | A practical method this book has put together, no data; that "one page saves a few minutes" in an emergency room is common ground.
    • Who this is for: Anyone who keeps important documents at home. Do not write a whole password on the sheet: give the actual password to one person you trust, separately.
    • What you get back: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid that "after I go, the family won't even know where things are"
    • How this works in China: When the time comes, put this sheet and the documents from 19.3, 19.5 and 19.6 into the same folder and keep it in one fixed place. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.8 Digital inheritance: phone numbers, WeChat and Alipay, bank cards, cloud photo albums¶

    • What it costs: one inquiry or one notarization (money: transferring a phone number may need a notarization, a few hundred yuan | time: days to weeks | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: When someone has died, money and accounts are still in the phone, and these have to be looked at separately. Phone number: the number belongs to the state, what you have is a "right of use"; courts and notary offices have accepted that it can be inherited, and once the notarization is done or you have a court document, you go to the carrier to transfer it. The change and balance in WeChat and Alipay: can be withdrawn according to law; the account itself belongs to the platform — most platform agreements state that ownership of the account belongs to the platform and the user has only a right of use, so as a rule it cannot be inherited or transferred.
    • What you can do: ① Phone number — the heirs agree among themselves (or a court rules), do the inheritance notarization or get a mediation document, then go to the carrier's business hall to transfer it; ② WeChat and QQ change — using a WeChat account registered in the heir's own name, go to Me → Services → Wallet → Help Center, type "WeChat Pay property inheritance", and submit the materials as instructed; ③ Alipay — call Alipay's official customer service line 95188, or search "inheritance" in Alipay, say you want to handle "balance / funds inheritance", and submit the materials as instructed; the exact entry point and materials are whatever the page shows at the time; ④ Bank cards — check first, then withdraw; small amounts can be simplified (see Chapter 23); ⑤ Passwords and accounts — do not write whole real passwords on paper; use a password manager with an "emergency contact / emergency access" feature (1Password, Bitwarden and the like), seal the master password and give it to one person you trust; on the list write only "which accounts exist, and which kind of tool they are in." This book has no financial relationship with any software vendor.
    • Where this comes from: A | There is official wording for phone numbers and for WeChat and QQ change (People's Court Daily and the Guangdong Provincial Department of Justice, reposted on the Ministry of Justice's smart legal education platform; Tencent customer service, April 2026). But how cloud photo albums, game accounts, self-media accounts and digital collectibles are handled has no nationwide rule; Article 127 of the Civil Code gives only a principled protection, and the specific rules are whatever each platform's service agreement says, and they change often. For these, ask the platform's customer service; remember "whatever the platform's rules are at the time." Also, the mainstream password managers all offer emergency contact / emergency access (under different names); go by what the product says at the time; this one is common practice (grade C).
    • Who this is for: This one is written more for the family; but what you can do now is list the accounts, the bank cards linked to them, and the platforms you use on that sheet in 19.7.
    • What you get back: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "the money is there while I am, and when I am gone no one knows where it is"
    • How this works in China: Carrier business halls, bank branches, the inheritance channels inside WeChat and Alipay; for notarization, go to a notary office; if you get stuck, call 12345. The latest local and platform wording prevails.

    19.9 What these cost and how many trips they take¶

    • What it costs: the figures below are orders of magnitude, and they differ a lot from place to place (money: tens of yuan to over a thousand | time: several half-days | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: One ledger, so that you have a sense of it and are not frightened by "it has to be notarized."
    • Notarizing a designated guardianship agreement: there is no nationwide price, and places differ widely. Some notary offices negotiate an hourly fee (one published local price list, for example, is 500–1000 yuan an hour, with accompanying items charged separately), others negotiate by the item with a floor of several thousand yuan each. The most accurate answer on how much and how it is charged comes from calling the local notary office.
    • Notarized will: a few hundred yuan; waived for people aged 80 and over the first time.
    • Notarizing a property inheritance: the first rate band is no more than 0.5%, and the total for a single residential property is in principle no more than 10,000 yuan.
    • How many trips: designated guardianship and a notarized will are usually one or two visits; a property inheritance depends on whether there is a dispute, and may take several.
    • Where this comes from: A | Fees are not uniform nationwide; the basis is the Measures for the Administration of Notarization Service Fees and the 2021 joint price guidance from two departments (Fagai Jiage [2021] No. 1081); designated guardianship and the like are mostly market-adjusted prices set by each notary office, so for yours you have to call and ask.
    • Who this is for: Households that are going to spend money on this. For dibao households and people with severe disabilities, basic notarization services are reduced by no less than 50%; ask "can this be reduced" before you start.
    • What you get back: money — no longer afraid of "being marked up back and forth, and running back and forth, just to get one thing done"
    • How this works in China: Call the local notary office first and ask the price and what to bring; if you have doubts about the price, go to the pricing or judicial administration department. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.10 If the person is already disabled, only the court is left¶

    • What it costs: one special-procedure lawsuit, possibly a forensic assessment (money: the assessment is several thousand yuan, varying by place | time: several months | effort: the family running back and forth | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: If designated guardianship was not done in time and the person can no longer understand or sign, then to make "who decides for him" take effect you have to go through the court — a close relative or other interested party, or a relevant organization, applies to the basic-level court where he lives to have him declared a person with no civil capacity or with limited civil capacity, and the court then appoints a guardian.
    • What you can do: ① A close relative or a relevant organization applies to the basic-level court in the place of residence; ② the court hears it under the special procedure, and a capacity assessment is usually needed; ③ after judgment a guardian is appointed; ④ if the situation improves and the cause is gone, you can still apply to revoke the original judgment.
    • Where this comes from: A | The statutes and the interpretation say so (Civil Code, Article 24; Civil Procedure Law, Article 194; Supreme People's Court reply, 2023). But who gets appointed easily turns into a fight among relatives; the court rules on "what is most beneficial to the ward", and will look at the materials you prepared and at who is actually doing the caring.
    • Who this is for: People who are already disabled and made no arrangement in advance, and their families. This section is the reason for the one before it: going through the court is slow, expensive, and wounds the family; the precondition for not going is doing 19.3 while you are clear-headed.
    • What you get back: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid that "now that he is confused, the family is fighting in court over who decides"
    • How this works in China: Go to the filing division of the basic-level court where the person lives and ask about the application materials and how the assessment is done; if you are unsure, ask a lawyer or call 12348. The latest local wording prevails.

    19.11 If you are reading this for your parents¶

    They are unlikely to raise this themselves. You raise it. Don't open with "when you get confused later"; open with yourself: "While we are all clear-headed, I'd like to settle a few things of my own, and I'd like your advice on them."

    Ask about one thing at a time. Today, one question only: "If one day you can't speak for yourself, who would you trust most to speak for you?" The rest waits for next time.

    What you can actually do right now, without his agreement, is three things: put his medicines, his diagnoses and the hospitals he uses on one page; fill in that index sheet in 19.7 for him — even if he won't cooperate, you find out for yourself where things are; and go with him once to a notary office or a hospital, to ask first and persuade later.

    If he won't talk, don't push. Designated guardianship, wills and living wills all have a "soft" version: write down what he thinks first, and ask the institutions that need asking first.

    One last blunt thing, said in a level voice: the earlier these are done, the simpler they are. The later they are done, the more likely it is that the person who could still leave you room to decide has slipped first.

    19.12 The one thing you can do today¶

    Open the notes app on your phone, or take a sheet of paper, and write this line:

    "If one day I cannot speak or sign, I want ______ to decide for me."

    Fill in one name. No paperwork today, just settle on this one person. Once there is a name, the door in 19.3 finally has a way in.

    19.13 Where I am not sure in this chapter¶

    • Notary offices apply different standards when assessing an older person's "capacity"; there is no nationwide document on how strict to be, and in practice the differences are large — which is exactly why this chapter keeps saying "do it early, while you are clear-headed"; whether it can be done and where it gets stuck are questions for the local notary office.
    • The fee for notarizing a designated guardianship agreement is, in many places, a market-adjusted price set by each office, and varies enormously (this chapter gives the published price lists of two notary offices for reference, but they are not a national standard); always call the local office for a price before you go.
    • How cloud photo albums, game accounts, self-media accounts and digital collectibles are handled has no nationwide rule: Article 127 of the Civil Code is only a principled protection, and most of the mainstream platform agreements we looked at say "ownership of the account belongs to the platform; it may not be transferred or inherited." For these you can only ask the platform's customer service, and the platform's rules at the time govern. This one now has a checkable official summary (a People's Daily report reposted by the Guangzhou Municipal Justice Bureau, see 19.14), so it is no longer marked "unverified"; but the conclusion is still — no uniform rule, only "go and ask the platform."
    • unverified How to withdraw a relative's money from WeChat and Alipay, and the entry point and materials, are whatever the platform page shows at the time — these two cannot be pinned to a long-stable official page: WeChat currently goes through a WeChat account registered in the heir's own name, "Me → Services → Wallet → Help Center", typing "WeChat Pay property inheritance" (Tencent customer service published an explanation in 2026-04); Alipay is currently the official customer service line 95188, or searching "inheritance" in the app and explaining the situation (95188 is on the "Contact Us" page of Alipay's service hall). Once the platform changes that page it no longer matches, so before you start, search once in the app or call customer service to confirm.

    The fees and the number of trips given in this chapter are orders of magnitude only; the procedural details of notary offices and courts differ from place to place, so before you do anything, call the local notary office or the court's filing division.
