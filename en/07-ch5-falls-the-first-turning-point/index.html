    Chapter 5 · Falls Are the First Turning Point in Later Life¶

    Three in the morning. You stand up from the toilet. Your vision goes black and your body tips toward the wall. You catch yourself. You don't fall.

    But you know: that instant came close to giving you a different rest of your life. This chapter is written for just before that instant.

    5.1 What this chapter is for¶

    This book runs four accounts. The first is independence. Falls are the watershed of that first account.

    Before a fall you can walk on your own, get to the toilet on your own, go downstairs on your own. After a fall, many people need a cane or someone's arm from then on, and some never get out of bed again. The difference isn't how much that fall hurt. It's how many years you have left to live on your own.

    What follows is not "watch out for slippery floors" talk. It's the moves you can make today, before you fall, and the few people you can go and ask.

    5.2 Why one fall can change your whole world¶

    Falls are the number-one cause of injury-related death among people aged 65 and over in China, and the number-one cause of traumatic fractures and of injury-related clinic visits among older adults ([official wording: Disease Control Bureau of the National Health Commission and Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre, Core Health Education Messages for Preventing Falls in Older Adults (预防老年人跌倒健康教育核心信息), 2021-10-12]).

    Some numbers from China: among community-dwelling adults over 60, about 14.7% fell in the past year; of those who fell, 79.5% were injured (third-party research Chinese Journal of Public Health (《中国公共卫生》), 2021, 21,084 people).

    What ends up changing the person is a hip fracture. In China, falls add more than 1 million new hip fractures among older adults every year. It has a nickname: "the last fracture of your life". Six months after the injury the death rate is about 10%–20%, and at one year 20%–30%; of those who survive, about 35% never walk on their own again, and 25% need long-term care (official wording Beijing Municipal Health Commission, reposting Beijing Shijitan Hospital, 2023).

    The chain links up like this: a fall → a fracture → surgery or bed rest → infection, blood clots, muscle wasting → the longer you lie there the weaker you get → you never get out of bed again. The death is often not the fracture itself. It's the complications of lying down.

    So a fall is not the small matter of "just taking a tumble". It is an accident that cuts a chunk off the years you can look after yourself. And it can be prevented in advance.

    5.3 The stool by the door is the cheapest piece of fall-proof furniture in the home¶

    • What it costs: one or two hundred yuan, done in one go (money: hardly anything | time: one hour | effort: none | asking anyone: not needed)
    • In short: Standing to put on shoes, lifting one foot, is one of the easiest ways to lose your balance. Sit down on a stool to change shoes, and that movement is gone.
    • What you can do: ① get a stool with a back and no wheels; ② mount a vertical grab bar on the wall beside it, so your hand has something to push against as you stand; ③ keep the shoes you wear most at a height you can reach without stretching, not on the bottom shelf.
    • Where this comes from: C | common practice. Falls often happen when you turn, bend, or balance on one foot, but no randomised trial has tested a shoe stool on its own.
    • Who this is for: people who can still walk on their own. If you already use a walker, standing up and turning need their own grab bars.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "falling and never getting up again"
    • How this works in China: Shoe stools, grab bars, and non-slip mats are usually on the government's recommended list for age-friendly home renovation (Minfa [2020] No. 86). Go to your residents' committee or the sub-district office's eldercare service desk and ask: "How do I apply for a home age-friendly renovation, and does a case like mine qualify for a subsidy?"

    5.4 Renovate four things in the bathroom: grab bars, non-slip, a seat for washing, night lights¶

    • What it costs: grab bars and non-slip work run from a few dozen yuan to a few hundred; a commode chair, a shower chair, and night lights are extra; the total per household varies widely (money: some | time: a few days | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Home is where older people fall most, and the bathroom and the entryway are the two corners of the home where falls happen most. They're wet and slippery, and there you also have to turn, crouch, and stand on one foot.
    • What you can do: ① put a grab bar beside the toilet and another in the shower, fixed into the wall, not a suction-cup type; ② make the floor non-slip and get the bath mats out of the house; ③ replace showering with a shower chair you can sit on; ④ install motion-sensor night lights along the whole route from bed to toilet, not just one lamp; ⑤ convert a squat toilet to a seated one, or add a commode chair.
    • Where this comes from: B | the World Health Organization lists "home assessment and modification" as one of the measures to prevent falls in older adults; which part you change and how much it helps depends on the home.
    • Who this is for: most older adults living at home. Grab bars have to go into a load-bearing wall; find a qualified installer and don't just drill expansion bolts in yourself.
    • What you get back: independence and dignity — you stop fearing "falling on a night trip to the toilet, and being seen"
    • How this works in China: The list in Minfa [2020] No. 86 splits into a "basic" category and an "optional" category. Seven basic items are subsidised by the government for older households in difficulty that qualify: non-slip treatment, level-difference treatment, a bedside rail, grab bars, a shower chair, a cane, and an anti-wandering device. The amounts differ by locality. In Guangzhou, qualifying households get up to 3,000 / 1,500 / 1,000 yuan per household depending on the category of the recipient; ask your district civil affairs bureau or community for the local figure. If you're paying yourself, ask the installer three questions first: did the grab bars go into the wall; is the floor non-slip when it's wet; do the night lights run the whole way from bed to toilet.

    5.5 Take a sheet of paper and walk the home spot by spot¶

    • What it costs: one pen, half an hour (money: nothing | time: half an hour | effort: none | asking anyone: not needed)
    • In short: The things that trip you are things you look at every day, so you no longer see them. Put on a pair of spot-the-problem eyes and go room by room.
    • What you can do: go through the checklist below, ticking off each spot as you pass it.
    • Floor: bath mats, thresholds, furniture glides, trailing cords, curled carpet edges, wet patches
    • Lighting: is it bright enough, are the switches somewhere you can reach, is there light the whole way from the bedside to the toilet
    • Passageways: is the hallway cluttered, is there room to turn between the furniture
    • Seating: are the bed and sofa too soft or too low, so that once you sit down you can't get up
    • Reaching things: do the things you use often need you to stretch, stand on a stool, or bend deep to reach
    • Stairs: are the steps a consistent height, is there a handrail, can you see the step edges clearly
    • Bathroom: see the previous item
    • Where this comes from: B | the checklist is drawn from official fall-prevention material and home-hazard risk assessment forms; fixing things one by one lowers risk, but no trial has tested "the checklist itself".
    • Who this is for: everyone. If you already use a walker, measure the hallway width and the turning points separately.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "the house is full of mines and I can't see them"
    • How this works in China: the checklist maps to item 9 of the Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre's Core Health Education Messages for Preventing Falls in Older Adults; in some cities the community will come to your home to do a "home fall-risk screening" (Chongqing and other places have pilots). Ask your community whether they offer this service.

    5.6 Soles need grip, but not softness — grip and a soft sole are two different things¶

    • What it costs: one pair of well-fitting shoes, one or two hundred to a few hundred yuan (money: not much | time: once | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Many people buy their parents "soft-soled shoes", thinking softer means more comfortable. The softer and thicker the sole, the duller the foot's sense of the ground and the slower the brain's response. Grip comes from the tread pattern; steadiness comes from a sole that doesn't collapse.
    • What you can do: ① a sole with clear tread and grooves grips well; ② some thickness and moderate firmness support the foot; ③ an enclosed heel, no backless slippers outdoors; ④ secure the fit with a buckle or laces tied tight; ⑤ the right size, with a little room from your toes to the front; ⑥ try shoes on in the evening, when your feet are most swollen, so the size you pick still fits during the day.
    • Where this comes from: C | the Chinese CDC put "low heel, non-slip, well-fitting" into its core messages; overseas research on shoes and falls is mixed, but what points the same way is "well-fitting, low heel, supportive". Don't believe that "one particular sole is always best".
    • Who this is for: people who can still walk on their own. If your feet are numb, you have diabetic foot, or your foot has changed shape, see a doctor or a foot-and-ankle specialist first, then choose shoes.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "being tripped by a badly fitting shoe"
    • How this works in China: buy shoes according to item 6 of the Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre's Core Health Education Messages for Preventing Falls in Older Adults; don't pay extra for the words "non-slip" or "for the elderly" on the packaging (a manufacturer's claim is not evidence).

    5.7 Three kinds of training, each doing its part: strength holds you up, balance keeps you steady, gait keeps you from tripping¶

    • What it costs: ten-odd minutes a day, done long term (money: nothing | time: daily | effort: a bit tiring | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: In the split second when you're about to fall, your body relies on two things: legs strong enough to hold you, and a body that corrects itself. These are two different abilities, and you train them separately. Being tripped often while walking is a third thing — the step itself is the problem.
    • What you can do:
    • Strength (holds you up): holding on for support, do heel raises (lifting onto your toes), stand-ups from a chair, and straight-leg back lifts, to work the thigh and calf.
    • Balance (keeps you steady): standing on one foot (start holding the wall), heel-to-toe "tightrope" walking, and a gently swaying "roly-poly" motion.
    • Gait (keeps you from tripping): high-knee marching in place, walking on your heels, walking on your toes, and stepping over small obstacles, to fix dragging feet, leaning forward, and unsteady steps. Principle: hold on first and reduce the support later; sit before you stand; get the movement right before adding load. Warm up before you train, and don't push through discomfort.
    • Where this comes from: B | the World Health Organization lists gait, balance, and functional training among measures to prevent falls in older adults, and among them the evidence for tai chi is the most solid; China's core messages also list these movement names.
    • Who this is for: people who can stand, walk, and sit down and stand up on their own. If you're often unsteady, or you've fallen within the past six months, get assessed by a rehabilitation department or a physiotherapist first; don't just start training. Being so afraid of falling that you stop moving at all is the worst option: the less you move, the faster your physical function declines, and the more likely you are to fall.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "the dead loop of being afraid to fall, so not moving at all"
    • How this works in China: these movements are inside the twelve-character fall-prevention motto of the Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre — "exercise, accept your age, adapt the home, treat early" (要锻炼、要服老、要适老、早治疗); communities, community health centres, and universities of the third age often run tai chi classes and walking groups. Where to ask: the family doctor team at your community health centre, or the community eldercare service station.

    5.8 Standing up from a chair without using your hands: how many times in thirty seconds¶

    • What it costs: one chair without armrests, one minute (money: nothing | time: one minute | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Whether you can stand up from a chair using only your legs, without your hands, is the most direct signal of whether your legs have strength. Standing up easily means you still have reserves; needing your hands to push means your legs are raising an alarm.
    • How to do it (do this movement): ① sit in the middle of the chair, no armrests, back straight; ② cross your arms onto the opposite shoulders, held to your chest; ③ feet flat on the floor, back straight; ④ after "go", stand up to fully upright, then sit down, repeating, and count how many times you can get up in thirty seconds; ⑤ if you have to use your hands to stand, stop and score zero. This movement itself is the "sit-to-stand practice" you should do every day.
    • What it means: a screening tool used widely overseas (CDC STEADI) gives "below-average" cut-offs — ages 60–64, men below 14 times and women below 12 count as below average; ages 80–84, men below 10 and women below 9; ages 85–89, both below 8 (official wording the US CDC STEADI chair-stand test form).
    • Where this comes from: B | this is a screening movement used widely overseas, with reference lines by age and sex; but the reference lines come from overseas populations, and lines for Chinese people may not match. Treat it as a comparison, not a diagnosis.
    • Who this is for: people who can sit and stand independently. If you feel dizzy or your heart races after doing it, stop and see a doctor.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "having no idea whether I'm still up to it"
    • How this works in China: the sit-to-stand practice is written into the Chinese CDC's fall-prevention motto ("exercise"), and you can do it at home; whether to measure it and what to do with the result is something to ask the family doctor at your community health centre.

    5.9 Before a fall, your body gives three warnings¶

    • What it costs: no money, just your eyes and your memory (money: nothing | time: ongoing | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Falls rarely come with no warning at all. Dizziness on standing up, tingling soles, blurry vision — these three often come first. Many people take them for "just what getting old is like" and tough it out.
    • What you can do: match yourself against the list and note when each one happens.
    • Dizziness or a blackout on standing up: standing up from sitting or lying makes you dizzy for a few seconds. It's usually a drop in blood pressure on standing (orthostatic hypotension). Rise more slowly, sit a moment before standing, and make a point of telling the doctor about it when you go.
    • Tingling soles, can't feel the ground: the foot's sense of the ground gets worse, making you more likely to trip or step wrong; it's common in people with diabetes or lower-back problems. Have a doctor examine your feet.
    • Blurry vision, can't see well at night: falling eyesight, cataracts, and badly fitting glasses are all in the mix. Get your eyes checked regularly, go out less at night, and take a light with you when you do. Two more that are easiest to overlook: having fallen once within the past six months, and needing to hold onto something after two steps — these two are high-risk signals in themselves.
    • Where this comes from: B | a drop in blood pressure on standing, and worsening eyesight and foot sensation, are all repeatedly confirmed risk factors for falls.
    • Who this is for: anyone who has had any of the above. A single fainting episode, or a single time you can't speak clearly or one side goes weak, is not a "warning" — it's an emergency. Call 120 (China's ambulance number).
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "my body was raising an alarm and I treated it as nothing"
    • How this works in China: rising slowly, putting in night lights, and getting your eyesight checked don't cost much; to get these checked properly you'll need a hospital, and see the clinic checklist in 5.12.

    5.10 These classes of medicine make falls more likely; take the list to the doctor¶

    • What it costs: one clinic visit (money: the price of one registration fee | time: once | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Many common drugs make you dizzy, drowsy, drop your blood pressure, or lower your blood sugar, and the risk of falling goes up. The problem isn't the drugs themselves; it's "which ones you take, whether any are doubled up, and whether the doses are right" — nobody has looked at the whole list for you. Older people often take several drugs at once, and the risks stack up.
    • High-risk drug list (with sources):
    • Sedative-hypnotics such as benzodiazepines and the "Z drugs" (for example estazolam, zolpidem, zopiclone)
    • Antidepressants (especially tricyclics and monoamine oxidase inhibitors)
    • Antipsychotics and antiepileptics
    • Blood-pressure drugs (blood pressure drops on standing)
    • Blood-sugar drugs (low blood sugar brings cold sweats, shaky hands, and blurred vision)
    • Diuretics (losing water quickly brings weakness and unsteadiness on your feet)
    • Opioid painkillers (sedation, muscle relaxation)
    • Aminoglycoside antibiotics (affect the vestibular system and balance)
    • What you can do: ① write out every drug you take, every supplement, and every "folk remedy" someone gave you on one sheet of paper, or take a photo; ② bring it to your next visit and ask the doctor or pharmacist to go through it from the top; ③ when a new drug is prescribed, ask "will this make me more likely to fall?"; ④ before adding a drug, tell the doctor what you already take, to avoid doubling up; ⑤ don't add drugs on your own, and don't stop them on your own.
    • Where this comes from: B | "fall-risk-increasing drugs" is an internationally recognised class, and multiple papers and guidelines agree. Which specific drug to stop, swap, or reduce can only be decided by a doctor. Internationally there are the Beers criteria and STOPP/START tools to screen for inappropriate medication in older adults.
    • Who this is for: people taking sleeping pills, antidepressants, blood-pressure drugs, or blood-sugar drugs, and especially those taking more than three drugs at once. Stopping or switching drugs must be guided by a doctor; doing it yourself can cause something much worse.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "taking more and more drugs, with nobody to help me sort through them"
    • How this works in China: take this list to your community family doctor, to a hospital geriatrics department, or to a pharmacist at a pharmacy for a "medication review"; if you manage your high blood pressure and diabetes at the community level, get the doctor to look at it during a follow-up visit.

    5.11 If someone falls, don't rush to help them up; in these three cases you must call 120¶

    • What it costs: the few seconds it takes to judge (money: nothing | time: a few minutes | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: When you see someone fall, the first instinct is to pull them straight up. That pull can displace a fracture or worsen a brain bleed. Look first, ask first, then decide whether to help them up.
    • What you can do:
    • Assess first: call out twice and see if they respond; check breathing, check for bleeding, whether the arms or legs look crooked, whether they cry out in pain.
    • In these three cases you must call 120, and not help them up yourself: ① unconscious, can't be woken, or vomiting, slurred speech, a drooping corner of the mouth, weakness on one side (possible stroke); ② clear, sharp pain, a limb deformed or shortened, or hip or back pain so bad they can't move (possible fracture); ③ headache or confusion after the head hits the ground, or loss of bladder or bowel control.
    • If they're alert and nothing looks serious: help them slowly into a sitting position and watch for a while; don't yank them up.
    • If you've fallen yourself: first move your fingers and toes to see if you can get up. If you can, roll onto your front first, grab something solid like the edge of the bed or a chair leg, push yourself up slowly, then sit and rest a while. If you can't get up, don't force it: shout, phone someone, bang on the floor, keep warm with cushions and clothing, and wait for help.
    • Hurt or not: tell your family and a doctor, and get the cause of the fall checked once. Someone who has fallen once is at higher risk of falling again.
    • Where this comes from: A | this is the handling procedure set out in both the Chinese Ministry of Health's Technical Guidelines for Fall Interventions in Older Adults (《老年人跌倒干预技术指南》)(2011) and the Chinese CDC's core messages (official wording).
    • Who this is for: everyone. If you live alone, write the "one emergency thing" on a sheet of paper and tape it to the wall in advance: 120, the community phone number, and the phone number of your nearest child.
    • What you get back: independence and relationships — you stop fearing "falling with no one knowing, or being hauled up carelessly"
    • How this works in China: call 120; if you have a contracted family doctor, save the number in advance. The handling procedure is in items 12 and 13 of the Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre's Core Health Education Messages for Preventing Falls in Older Adults.

    5.12 Four things to check to prevent falls: eyesight, blood pressure, medication, bones¶

    • What it costs: one outpatient visit (money: registration plus tests | time: half a day | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Fall prevention isn't one thing; it's several things looked at together. Bring a checklist with you and the doctor won't miss anything.
    • What you can do: hand this checklist to the doctor and ask them to go through it item by item.
    • Eyesight: check vision, check for cataracts, ask whether your glasses still fit. If you can't see well at night or things are blurry, say so specifically.
    • Blood pressure: measure it lying down and again on standing, to see whether you have orthostatic hypotension.
    • Medication: bring all your drugs and supplements, do a medication review, and see whether any "make falls more likely".
    • Gait and balance: have the doctor watch how you walk and whether you can stand up from a chair, and refer you to rehabilitation if needed.
    • Bones: assess your osteoporosis risk and check bone density (DXA). China has the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (《原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）》) and the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Osteoporosis in Older Adults in China (2023) (《中国老年骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2023）》) to follow.
    • Feet: look at the feet, look at the shoes.
    • One more question: which single spot at home should be fixed first.
    • Where this comes from: A | this "screen first, then assess, then intervene" checklist comes from the widely used overseas approach to fall assessment (CDC STEADI), and China's core messages cover the same direction.
    • Who this is for: everyone; people who have fallen within the past six months, walk unsteadily, or take several drugs should go even more. If you can't cover it all in one visit, two visits is fine too.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "not knowing where to start"
    • How this works in China: book a geriatrics, rehabilitation, or general-practice outpatient appointment; everyone aged 65 and over gets a free annual health-management check, which includes a physical examination (a rough check of the mouth, eyesight, hearing, and movement) plus health guidance on falls and osteoporosis — ask your community health centre about the exact items (National Basic Public Health Service Specification (国家基本公共卫生服务规范), third edition). Bone density and rehabilitation assessments usually require referral to a higher-level hospital. Who does the assessment is up to the doctor, with the family doctor team following up in between.

    5.13 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    Your hardest part is usually not knowing that falls need preventing. It's getting him to accept the changes.

    There are three things you can do without his approval: ① start with the entryway and the bathroom — if he won't agree to grab bars, first buy an unobtrusive non-slip mat and a shoe stool and say "it's handy for me to use too"; ② photograph his medications and keep the record — bring it to the doctor when you go with him, rather than letting him tell the doctor himself that "I don't take much"; ③ ask at the community once — how to apply for age-friendly renovation, how the free health check for people over 65 works. Find out first, then decide.

    He may not admit it out loud. His body will.

    5.14 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    This chapter originally carried four items flagged to verify; three have been traced back to source: the fall rate back to the original Chinese Journal of Public Health article, the number-one cause of injury-related death back to the Disease Control Bureau of the National Health Commission and the Chinese CDC Chronic Disease Centre's Core Health Education Messages for Preventing Falls in Older Adults, and "the spots that can be prevented" back to the Ministry of Civil Affairs and 8 other departments' Minfa [2020] No. 86 and the Guangzhou civil affairs official notice. The remaining three are limits of the data itself, not things I couldn't find; I've kept them in honestly:

    • The 14.7% fall rate comes from a sample of 21,084 people across 6 provinces and cities in the 2018 PINDEC project, not a national probability sample, so it may differ from the national figure.
    • The "below-average" reference lines for the chair-stand test come from a US population (CDC STEADI); China has no corresponding age- and sex-specific reference values, so they can only serve as a comparison, not a diagnostic standard.
    • The subsidy amounts and item lists for home age-friendly renovation differ by province and city (this chapter gives one Guangzhou example); the local latest notice governs.

    One honest note: some falls can't be stopped. The older you get, the more your balance, eyesight, and muscle decline, and no matter how much you prevent, one day you may still fall. What this chapter can do is stop the ones that can be stopped, first.

    5.16 The one thing you can do today¶

    Take every bath mat in the house — the one at the bathroom door, the one at the entryway, the one by the bed — all of them out.

    Not put away. Out of the house. Bath mats are the most common thing to trip over, and removing them costs nothing. Do it tonight.
