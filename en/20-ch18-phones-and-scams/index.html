    Chapter 18 · Phones and scams: get the thing done, don't get taken¶

    Seven in the morning. You want to book an appointment on your phone. You poke at the screen for a long while and can't find the place. The letters are too small, there are too many steps. You put the phone down and say, "I'll go early and queue tomorrow."

    Put it down, and you have handed someone else a thing you could have done yourself.

    18.1 What this chapter is for¶

    This book works out four accounts, and the first is self-reliance. The old saying about managing for yourself meant being able to feed yourself and get to the toilet on your own. Today that sentence is only half of it — you have to add one more line: being able to pick up your own phone and get done the things that mean seeing a doctor, going out, paying money.

    Appointments are on the phone, payments are on the phone, and to get into a hospital you first have to show a code. If you can't do the things on the phone, you have to ask for help everywhere. Not being able to use a phone already amounts to being half-disabled today.

    This chapter has two halves. The first half is about getting things done on the phone; the second half is about scams — because what a scammer is after is exactly this screen you now depend on most.

    18.2 First set the phone so you can see it and hear it¶

    • What it costs: ten minutes, one setup (money: none | time: ten minutes | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
    • In short: Every phone hides a switch that makes the letters bigger and the sound louder, and it goes by different names: on the phone itself it's "simple mode / large font", in WeChat it's "care mode", in Alipay it's "elder mode". Turn it on and you don't need a new phone.
    • What you can do: ① The phone itself: "Settings" → "Display" or "Accessibility" → "Simple mode / elder mode / large font". On an iPhone, "Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size"; on Huawei, "Settings → System & updates → Simple mode" (after a system upgrade this moved into "care mode", under "Settings → Care and accessibility → Care", as "simple layout"). ② WeChat: Me → Settings → Care Mode → turn on (in newer versions there is an extra "More" step inside "Settings"). ③ Alipay: Me → Settings (top right) → Elder Mode, or just search "elder mode" in the search box. ④ Sound: turn "media volume" and "call volume" up to the maximum.
    • Where this comes from: C | Button names and their positions change with the version; the direction doesn't — if you can't see it or can't hear it, look first for "care", "elder", "large font".
    • Who this is for: People whose sight and hearing are getting worse but who can still hold a phone themselves. If you can't see the screen at all, move to voice assistants and have your children operate it for you.
    • What you get back: self-reliance — no longer afraid that "I can't make out a single character on this phone"
    • How this works in China: If you can't get it right yourself, take the phone to a community eldercare service station, a university of the third age, or a phone brand's own shop, and have the staff set it up in front of you. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.3 When your children help from far away, there is only one hard rule¶

    • What it costs: one phone call, one video call (money: none | time: a few sessions | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: Your children aren't nearby — so how do they teach you to use the phone? The steadiest way is a video call: you hold the phone up facing the screen, and they watch and say "tap this next". Software that lets the other person take over your phone screen — don't use it if you can do without it.
    • What you can do: ① Have your children call by video and teach you one step at a time: you tap, they watch. ② If you really want remote assistance, use only the one that came with your phone brand; have your children install it for you face to face, and teach you how to agree and how to hang up. ③ In any remote session, never read a verification code out to the other person on the phone — not even to your own son.
    • Where this comes from: C | Common practice. Screen-sharing software helps people and is also often exploited; the rules have to be set in advance, see 18.6.
    • Who this is for: Households with children who can do a video call. If your children aren't comfortable with phones either, swap "the person who teaches you" for a young volunteer at the community office or the university of the third age.
    • What you get back: self-reliance + relationships — no longer afraid that "no one will teach me, and I daren't tap anything"
    • How this works in China: Communities, universities of the third age and libraries often run phone classes. Ask once at the community office: "Is there any activity that teaches older people to use a phone?" The latest local wording prevails.

    18.4 Getting it done: six tasks, and where the first tap goes¶

    • What it costs: about fifteen minutes per task, one task at a time (money: none | time: fifteen minutes each | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: There are only a few things you need to do on a phone: book an appointment, call a car, pay, pay bills, show your medical insurance code, and prove that you are you.
    • How to do it (each step starts from what to tap the first time): ① Book an appointment: WeChat → search box → type the hospital name → open the hospital's official account → "Book an appointment". ② Call a car: Amap or Baidu Maps → "Ride" → check "my location" → "Request". ③ Pay: WeChat "Me → Services → Pay and receive", which shows a code for the cashier to scan. ④ Pay bills: WeChat "Me → Services → Utilities", choose water, electricity or gas, and link the account number once. ⑤ Electronic medical insurance credential (the insurance code): install the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app, or search "medical insurance e-credential" in WeChat or Alipay, do the face verification, and show it when you see a doctor or buy medicine. ⑥ Prove that you are you: people drawing a pension do a liveness check once a year, using the "electronic social security card" or the "Zhangshen 12333" app — blink, open your mouth, and it's done.
    • Where this comes from: C | These are all features the various apps already have, and their position shifts slightly with each version; the direction is "search box + scan + payment code".
    • Who this is for: People who can read and can see the screen. If your eyes are poor or your fingers shake, turn on the large font and the voice first, then learn these steps.
    • What you get back: self-reliance — no longer afraid that "I can't even get through the hospital door, I have to ask someone for everything"
    • How this works in China: The insurance code is accepted only from the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app or the official entrances in WeChat and Alipay; the pension liveness check only from "Zhangshen 12333" and the "electronic social security card". Anything else — a mini-program that has you enter a bank card password or read out a verification code — is not official. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.5 If you can't follow it, don't force it: the places and people who can do it for you¶

    • What it costs: one conversation (money: some free, some paid | time: one trip | effort: none | do you have to ask: you do, and it's a little awkward)
    • In short: Not being able to learn it isn't shameful. Tapping around on your own is what's dangerous.
    • What you can do: ① The community: the residents' committee and the community eldercare service station can pay or register on your behalf, and the counter can handle things for you. ② Government service center: ask at the door "which window handles assisted service for older people" — there is usually a fast track. ③ Paid hospital accompaniment: if you live alone, your children are far away, or getting around is hard, you can pay someone to go with you to register, queue and collect medicine; Shanghai and some other places already run official pilots. ④ The bank counter: if you don't want to use mobile banking, do it at the counter. That is your right.
    • Where this comes from: C | Assisted handling and hospital accompaniment are practices being promoted in various places; coverage and charges vary a lot, and accompaniment still has no nationwide qualification standard.
    • Who this is for: People who live in the city and can get out of the house. If you live far out or can't get out, rely on the community's home visits and your children operating the phone for you.
    • What you get back: self-reliance + dignity — no longer afraid that "I can't understand it and daren't ask, so I just sit there fretting"
    • How this works in China: Government services have a reform called "get one thing done efficiently"; hospital accompaniment costs about 198–300 yuan for a half day (it differs from place to place). Who to ask: the community residents' committee, the guidance desk at the government service center, or the manager on the bank floor. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.6 One family agreement, taped to the fridge¶

    • What it costs: one sheet of paper, one signature from each person in the family (money: none | time: half an hour | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: One rule set by the whole family is worth more than an argument after something has happened. Just two lines: verification codes go only to your own family; if a stranger asks for a code, hang up, every time.
    • What you can do: ① Write it down and tape it to the fridge: verification codes, bank card passwords, payment passwords — you give them to no one. Not to someone claiming to be customer service, not to the police or the procuratorate, not to the bank, and not to someone claiming to be a colleague of your child. ② The moment the other person mentions "transfer money", "verification code" or "screen sharing", hang up. ③ Agree at home on one small thing that only the family knows; when the "grandson" on the video opens his mouth, ask him that first.
    • Where this comes from: C | Common practice. But "never give a verification code to anyone" is the first line of almost every anti-fraud leaflet.
    • Who this is for: Families that can still sit down and talk it over. If someone in the house has already lost judgement, the guardian has to enforce this rule for them.
    • What you get back: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "if I've been cheated I daren't say, for fear the family will blame me"
    • How this works in China: Photograph the agreement, send it to the family group chat, save it in the album; the National Anti-Fraud Center's own materials say the same sentence. The latest local wording prevails.

    The scammer is younger than you, and knows the phone better than you do.

    18.7 Fake relatives: the "grandson" on video may be assembled¶

    • What it costs: hang up, call back (money: none | time: one minute | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
    • In short: A scammer can take your grandson's photos and voice and stitch together a face-swapped video to call you, with the face and the voice both close. Don't judge it by "does it look like him" — judge it by "check it another way".
    • What you can do: ① The moment you hear "it's me, something's happened, send money quickly", hang up. ② Call the grandson back on the number you saved yourself, or call his parents; do not call back the number that showed on your screen. ③ Ask him about an old thing that only the family knows. ④ On the video, ask him to put a hand over his face for a moment — fake video often gives itself away there.
    • Where this comes from: A (case) | In Zhengzhou, an older woman known as Grandma Li was sent an AI face-swap and voice-clone video of her "grandson" asking for 50,000 yuan; at the bank the staff flagged the account as risky, and a call-back to verify stopped the transfer second-hand (People's Daily Overseas Edition, 2026-04-13). Another report, citing Ministry of Public Security data, says that in the first quarter of 2025 the number of AI face-swap and voice-clone fraud cases filed nationwide rose about 45% quarter on quarter, and people over 60 made up 38% of those cheated second-hand (same paper, 2026-04-13).
    • Who this is for: Everyone, especially older people who watch a lot of short video and often video-call their grandchildren. If cognition has already declined and they can't tell true from false, the family has to hold the "call the child before giving money" line for them.
    • What you get back: money — no longer afraid that "the person on the video is obviously him"
    • How this works in China: After hanging up, dial 96110 for advice; the Zhengzhou case was stopped only because bank staff flagged the account as abnormal, so doing a large transfer at the counter adds one more human check. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.8 Fake customer service: if they say "automatic monthly charge", hang up first¶

    • What it costs: one hang-up (money: none | time: one minute | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
    • In short: "You've signed up for a membership / a livestream, and it charges automatically every month" — that sentence itself is the hook. Real customer service does not call to teach you how to cancel a charge, and certainly does not have you download software or give a password.
    • What you can do: ① The moment you hear "automatic charge" or "it affects your credit record", hang up. ② If you want to check, call the platform's official customer line yourself — for the short-video platform in this case, 95152 — not the number that called you. ③ Don't download any "meeting", "screen cast" or "security" app the other person names; that step is how they get to see your phone and operate it for you. ④ A bank card password, an SMS verification code, a face scan — if the person on the phone asks for any of them, it's a scammer.
    • Where this comes from: A (case) | In Shenzhen, a 70-year-old woman known as Aunt Yi was told by someone posing as platform customer service that "you've opened a livestream and will be charged"; she did as told and installed three unfamiliar video-meeting apps, and 147,000 yuan was transferred out within an hour (Shenzhen Municipal Public Security Bureau anti-fraud bulletin, 2025-07) second-hand.
    • Who this is for: People who often watch video or shop online on the phone. If someone is already disabled, the money and the phone should not both be in their hands alone.
    • What you get back: money — no longer afraid that "he sounded so plausible that I believed him"
    • How this works in China: If a suspicious call comes, hang up and dial 96110 or the platform's official hotline; install the National Anti-Fraud Center app and leave call warnings on. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.9 "Housing for pension": the real one is insurance, the fake one has you mortgage the house¶

    • What it costs: one more question, one more day (money: none | time: one trip | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: "Housing for pension" comes in a real version and a fake one. The real one is called "reverse mortgage pension insurance", a state-approved insurance product: you mortgage the house to the insurer, keep living in it, and draw a pension monthly. The fake one borrows the name, has you mortgage the house and hands the money to him to put into "high-interest wealth management" — the crux is "the mortgaged money has to be invested again".
    • What you can do: ① Tell the two apart: real — mortgaged to an insurer, you keep living there, money monthly, and it is insurance; fake — the house is mortgaged, the money goes to a "wealth management company" to invest, and high interest is promised. ② The moment someone says "mortgage the house → put it in our product → high interest every month", stop. ③ If you are genuinely interested, go only to an insurer's counter and to products filed with the financial regulator, and ask face to face; don't go by the leaflet.
    • Where this comes from: A | "Reverse mortgage pension insurance" is a state-approved pilot insurance product (Baojianfa [2014] No. 53, in force from 2014-07-01), run by insurers and not a wealth-management product; the volume of business has always been very small.
    • Who this is for: People over 60 who own their home outright. If the flat is your only place to live and your children still live with you, talk it through with the family before mortgaging.
    • What you get back: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "even a state policy gets used to cheat me"
    • How this works in China: For the real product, ask at a regular insurer's counter; if you meet "housing-for-pension wealth management", dial 96110 or report it to the financial regulator. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.10 The free-eggs lecture isn't selling eggs¶

    • What it costs: one trip, one time holding back (money: none | time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: "Free eggs, free health lecture" is a way of pulling people in, not a kindness. First a small freebie, then you are led into a group chat, and slowly they sell you "miracle medicine" at thousands or tens of thousands of yuan. Most of those "medicines" are sugar pills, or food.
    • What you can do: ① If "free gift + lecture + expensive health product" come as a set, don't go. ② If you are already in the group, leave it; if they tell you "don't tell your children", that sentence itself is the alarm. ③ If you really want a supplement, ask your children first, or ask at a proper pharmacy or hospital; don't trust an "expert's live lecture". ④ Keep the receipts and the transfer records.
    • Where this comes from: B | CCTV Finance, China News Service and several other outlets have reported the "free eggs" routine; some cases have been found by the courts to be fraud and drawn prison sentences.
    • Who this is for: Older people who live alone, like company, are afraid of getting ill and have a bit of money are the easiest to catch. What the family should do is not scold them — it is to go with them once to ask at a proper channel.
    • What you get back: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "I'm scared of getting ill, so I want to buy some peace of mind"
    • How this works in China: Report false advertising to the market regulator on 12315; if illegal fundraising or fraud is involved, call the police. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.11 The "roaming retirement" membership that costs hundreds of thousands up front — don't pay it yet¶

    • What it costs: one check, one refusal to pay (money: none | time: one trip | effort: none | do you have to ask: you do, and it's a little awkward)
    • In short: "Pay a hundred-odd thousand, or several hundred thousand, once to become a member, and later you can stay at any of our retirement bases anywhere in the country" — with prepaid membership fees like this, the thing to fear is the day the company's money runs out and the people are gone.
    • What you can do: ① Remember the lines the state has drawn: a care home may collect service fees at most 12 months ahead; the deposit taken from a single older person may not exceed 12 times the monthly bed fee, and beyond that it is non-compliant. ② If someone wants several hundred thousand in "membership fees" or a "lifetime card" in one go, they have probably crossed the line already. ③ Before paying, check whether the institution is on file with the local civil affairs department. ④ If you want to spend a few winter months in the south, paying a year at a time, one month's deposit against one month's rent, is enough — don't stake your whole savings at once.
    • Where this comes from: A | The Guiding Opinions on Strengthening Supervision of Prepaid Fees at Elderly Care Institutions (《关于加强养老机构预收费监管的指导意见》), issued by the Ministry of Civil Affairs and six other departments (Minfa [2024] No. 19, effective from 2024-10), sets out the prepayment caps and the escrow of funds.
    • Who this is for: People who own a flat, have savings, are still in reasonable health and fancy "roaming retirement". If you are already disabled and need care, you are not a candidate for roaming — you are a candidate for care.
    • What you get back: money — no longer afraid that "I went looking for a dignified old age and put the principal in"
    • How this works in China: Before paying, go to the local civil affairs bureau and ask about the institution's filing and how its prepaid fees are supervised. Ask three things: Is it on file? How are prepayments supervised? How are refunds calculated? The latest local wording prevails.

    18.12 Set yourself one rule: big money waits a day before it leaves¶

    • What it costs: one day (money: none | time: one day | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to ask)
    • In short: What a scammer fears most is that you wait. Almost every scam contains a "if you don't do it today it's too late", precisely so that you have no time to think. Put a "one day later" on big money and the scammer's words stop working.
    • What you can do: ① Agree with the family: above a certain amount (you set it — three or five thousand, say), nothing goes out that day; sleep on it and do it the next day. ② When transferring in mobile banking, choose "arrives in 24 hours", not "instant". ③ To be safer still, put the large sums into a fixed-term deposit, or open a separate account where getting money out means going to the bank.
    • Where this comes from: C | This is a house rule. Years ago a transfer from an ATM to an account not in your own name was required to arrive 24 hours later; that was later relaxed and instant transfer allowed, so this cooling-off period now has to be set by you.
    • Who this is for: Everyone. Even in a real emergency (a hospital deposit) there is usually another way; calling the family first often works better than transferring first.
    • What you get back: money — no longer afraid that "I transferred in a hurry, with no time to think"
    • How this works in China: Look for the "transfer arrival time" setting in your mobile banking app; if you're not sure, dial 96110. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.13 If you really are cheated: stop the payment, keep the evidence, call the police — and don't hide it from the family¶

    • What it costs: the first few hours (money: none | time: a few hours | effort: none | do you have to ask: hard to bring up)
    • In short: If you have been cheated, don't start by cursing yourself, and don't hide it. Whether the money can be recovered depends on these first few hours. The order is: stop the payment → keep the evidence → call the police.
    • What you can do: ① Stop the payment: call the bank's hotline or go to the counter at once, and ask them to freeze or stop that payment — whatever can be held back, is. ② Keep the evidence: don't delete the chat records, the transfer text message, the incoming call; take screenshots, record, the fuller the better. ③ Call the police: dial 110, or 96110 to explain the situation, the sooner the better. ④ Tell the family: hiding it only makes the hole bigger, and you may be cheated a second time.
    • Where this comes from: A | The Ministry of Public Security has introduced 96110, the National Anti-Fraud Center app and other measures; the Anti-Telecom and Online Fraud Law of the People's Republic of China took effect on 2022-12-01 and provides for stopping payments, freezing funds and recovering assets.
    • Who this is for: Everyone. If someone already has dementia and has been "played" for a long time, the family should go with them in person to the police and the bank.
    • What you get back: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "if I've been cheated no one will help me, and I daren't say it out loud"
    • How this works in China: 110, 96110, the bank counter; the National Anti-Fraud Center app has a one-tap report. The latest local wording prevails.

    18.14 If you can only do one thing today¶

    Today, set the phone's font size to the largest, and turn on WeChat's care mode. Just that one thing.

    With bigger letters you can see everything in this chapter clearly — where the first tap goes for those six tasks, and the one place in a scam where something doesn't sit right.

    18.15 If you are reading this for your parents¶

    It is hard to say "be careful, don't get cheated" straight out. The moment you say it, it becomes distrust.

    How to say it: turn the conversation onto yourself. Don't say "let me adjust your phone for you"; say "I want bigger text on mine too — let's do both together." While he watches you do it, he learns the method himself.

    To guard the gate, start with three things that don't need his approval: ① install the National Anti-Fraud Center app on his phone and turn call warnings on; ② save your number, 96110 and the nearest bank branch's number into his phone, and teach him one sentence — "if they ask for a code, hang up"; ③ set large transfers to "arrives in 24 hours", and agree with him that above a certain amount he calls you first.

    If he won't agree, what you can still do: ask at the community whether there are phone classes or assisted handling; get his insurance code and pension liveness check working smoothly on his phone; print out the family agreement in 18.6 and tape it to his fridge.

    The hardest part: don't let him feel that you are investigating him or guarding against him. For someone who has been cheated, what they fear most is often not that the money is gone, but being scolded by the family. If he is willing to tell you first thing, the money can still be saved.

    18.16 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • unverified Two figures on AI fraud — "up about 45% quarter on quarter" and "older people made up 38% of those cheated" — this chapter found only the People's Daily Overseas Edition (2026-04-13) and reprints by the Legal Daily site and Credit China saying they are cited from Ministry of Public Security data; I did not read the ministry's original bulletin, so they are still treated as second-hand. To check the original wording, go to the ministry's website or look up the transcript of a ministry press conference.
    • The entrances for elder mode and care mode have to be found again every time a version changes: the current paths for Apple, Huawei and WeChat can all be matched against their own official help pages (see 18.17); Huawei, for one, merged its earlier "simple mode" into "care mode", which shows that renaming really does happen. Alipay has no long-term stable official page, so go by what the app on your own phone actually shows.
    • There is no nationwide system for hospital accompaniment, and no nationwide qualification standard. Shanghai is furthest ahead (see Chapter 17 and 18.5): open to everyone over 60, with no household-registration requirement, so older people who have come to Shanghai from elsewhere can buy it too. A few cities (Xiamen, for instance) have also issued municipal accompaniment schemes through the civil affairs department in recent years, but most have not. Whether your city has one, and what it charges, you have to ask the local civil affairs bureau.
    • On how many disputes "roaming" and prepaid retirement membership fees have actually produced, there is no public authoritative statistic, and we did not find a number — what this chapter gives is the regulatory wording (Minfa [2024] No. 19) plus an official risk notice: on 2025-09-09 the National Office on Aging, the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the China Association of Aging and the China Consumers Association jointly issued an eldercare consumption notice, naming the problem of "membership-based eldercare" and "prepaid cards" charging large membership fees. To check whether a particular institution is compliant, go to the Ministry of Civil Affairs' National Elderly Care Service Information Platform (see 18.17), or ask the local civil affairs bureau.
    • The default for "arrives in 24 hours" in mobile banking differs from bank to bank, and there is no nationwide page explaining it: some default to instant, some make you choose "next-day arrival" under "arrival time" when you transfer. Open your own mobile banking and look for that option once on the transfer screen; if you're not sure, call the bank's hotline.
