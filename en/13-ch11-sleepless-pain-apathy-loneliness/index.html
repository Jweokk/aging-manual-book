    Chapter 11 · Can't sleep, pain, no drive, loneliness¶

    Three in the morning, you wake up and can't get back to sleep. There's a dull ache in your lower back, and turning over doesn't get you away from it. The room is very quiet. Someone asked you during the day how you were doing, and you said, "Pretty good."

    These four things usually arrive together: sleeping badly, hurting, having no drive, and having no one to talk to. And they are the easiest things in the world to wave away with one sentence: put up with it, it'll pass.

    11.1 What this chapter is for¶

    Sleeping badly, hurting all the time, feeling empty inside, nobody to talk to — looked at one at a time, each of them seems like "normal for old age." Each of them can in fact be treated, and they tangle into each other: pain keeps you from sleeping, bad sleep pulls your mood lower, a low mood makes you less willing to go out, staying in makes you lonelier, and loneliness makes the pain and the sleeplessness worse again.

    This chapter is not going to tell you to look on the bright side. It gives you a few things you can do today, and a few phone numbers you can call when something goes wrong. Of these four things, one cannot be endured and cannot wait — that is 11.8.

    11.2 Can't sleep? Don't go looking for a sleeping pill yet¶

    • What it costs: one outpatient visit; adjusting your daily rhythm takes a few weeks to show (money: the price of one registration | time: a few weeks | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: The older you get, the less deep sleep you have, the more often you wake at night, and the earlier you wake in the morning. That is normal; it is not an illness. What needs looking at is not "sleeping short" but whether your days are being dragged down: no energy, poor memory, mood falling apart.
    • What you can do: ① First separate "sleeping short" from "can't sleep" — it is worth investigating as a real problem only if it goes on for weeks, several nights a week, and affects your days; ② bring the "my medicines" sheet from Chapter 6 and ask the doctor whether anything you take is making you drowsy or foggy at night; ③ whether a sleeping pill can be used, which one, and for how long, the doctor decides. The general principle for medicines in older people is one less rather than one more, start at the smallest dose, don't take it long term.
    • Where this comes from: B | China's 2025 insomnia guideline from the Chinese Sleep Research Society recommends "non-drug therapy first for insomnia in older adults" official wording. A sleeping pill can get you to sleep faster, but in older people it costs you daytime drowsiness and falls when you get up at night, and there are also people who need one short term under a doctor's care.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: people who wake often at night but still hold up during the day — don't treat it as an illness yet; people whose days have already collapsed, with their mood sliding down too, need to be seen.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "sleeping on pills, getting more muddled the more I sleep, falling more easily the more I sleep."
    • How this works in China: Register at a sleep clinic, or psychiatry and psychology, or neurology. To find out whether your area has a dedicated sleep clinic, call the hospital and ask.

    11.3 Nail down your wake-up time, cut the nap to half an hour¶

    • What it costs: nothing; what you change is your habits (money: nothing to pay | time: every day | effort: none | asking anyone: not needed)
    • In short: Sleepiness is built up by being awake during the day; once your daily rhythm is scrambled, you have no sleepiness left at night. Catching up on sleep during the day, and lying in bed scrolling on your phone, both take the night's sleep apart.
    • What you can do: ① Fix your wake-up time — however late you slept the night before, get up at the same time the next morning; this is the most important one; ② naps no longer than half an hour, and none after three in the afternoon; ③ go outside in the morning sun for twenty or thirty minutes, so your body knows it is daytime; ④ no strong tea or coffee after midday, and in the three hours before bed don't eat too much and don't drink alcohol; ⑤ the bed is for sleeping only — lie down, can't sleep, get up and sit for a while, and go back when you are sleepy.
    • Where this comes from: C | These items are the "sleep hygiene, stimulus control, sleep restriction" parts of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and they are also part of the first-line regimen recommended in China's 2025 edition of the insomnia guideline official wording. But done here and there they do little; done as a program, with a doctor or therapist guiding you, is what holds.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: most people who sleep badly. People who get up several times a night, feel dizzy and weak in the legs during the day, or snore and stop breathing in their sleep should be checked for those first, and not just have their daily rhythm adjusted.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "lying there with my eyes open at night, and being foggy all day."
    • How this works in China: quite a few hospital sleep clinics and psychiatry and psychology departments can do CBT-I, and there are self-guided versions on your phone; ask your local hospital first whether it has one.

    11.4 Pain is the kind he says "it's nothing" about¶

    • What it costs: a pair of eyes, the ones you already have (money: nothing to pay | time: as usual | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Older people mostly don't say they are in pain. He says "it's nothing," and you can see it anyway: he walks slower, holds the rail going up and down stairs, his face is set tight, he eats less, he doesn't want to go out, he turns over and over at night.
    • What you can do: Don't ask the vague "where does it hurt"; ask about specifics: what hurts first thing when you get up, which movement takes the most effort, are you walking slower than six months ago. Take a sheet of paper and write down when it hurts, how long it lasts, what movements make it worse. If he says "old problem, I'll tough it out," say this: tell me clearly where it hurts and when, and I'll go ask the doctor — you don't have to carry it yourself.
    • Where this comes from: B | The prevalence of chronic pain among Chinese adults aged 60 and over is 35.71% (based on the 2008–2015 CHARLS survey, 27 859 people), and the most common is low back pain, at 13.09% third-party research.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: older people who can still speak. Pain that comes on suddenly, is very sharp, stays in one fixed place, or comes with weight loss or waking from pain at night — don't take it for an old problem, get it checked. Sudden chest pain, abdominal pain, or severe back pain: see Chapter 8 first.
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "hiding the pain and pretending it's nothing, until I can't walk."
    • How this works in China: see the next item.

    11.5 Chronic pain: don't buy painkillers and tough it out yourself¶

    • What it costs: one outpatient visit (money: the price of one registration | time: half a day | effort: a bit tiring, you have to get to the hospital | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Pain that lasts more than three months is itself an illness that needs treating, not a fate you grit your teeth through. What is causing it, and how it can be handled, is something someone has to sort out for you.
    • What you can do: ① Write the place it hurts, the times it hurts, what movements make it worse, and what you have taken onto one page; ② where to register: geriatrics, the pain clinic, rehabilitation, or the specialty that matches the site (joints to orthopedics, nerve pain to neurology or the pain clinic); ③ take that page and ask the doctor three things: what is causing this pain, what options are there, when should I come back for review; ④ for painkillers you take long term, have the doctor look at them regularly, and don't raise the dose yourself.
    • Where this comes from: C | "Chronic pain needs its cause sorted out and needs handling on several fronts" is common practice. Which method to use, the doctor decides.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: people who have hurt for more than three months and whose sleep and going out are already affected. Long-term painkillers need watching for stomach and kidney problems, and they are also no reason to skip going to a doctor (see Chapter 6).
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "hurting too much to move, and going out less and less the more it hurts."
    • How this works in China: most hospitals at tier 2 and above have a pain clinic; some cities run pain care in the community. To find out which hospital you want and whether it has a pain clinic, call the hospital and ask.

    11.6 Having no drive inside is an illness that can be treated¶

    • What it costs: one outpatient visit plus a few follow-ups (money: registration plus medicine | time: a few weeks | effort: none | asking anyone: you have to, it is a little embarrassing, you have to be willing to admit that you feel bad)
    • In short: In old age, having no drive, not wanting to do anything, always saying you are useless — this often gets taken as "that is just what getting old is like." It is an illness, and it can be treated. Older people who have it often don't say they feel awful either; they say again and again that something in their body is wrong, and test after test finds nothing.
    • What you can do: Check yourself against these: more than two weeks with no drive, things you used to love doing left undone, sleeping badly and eating little, saying over and over "I'm useless," "I'm a burden on you," hurting here and there with no cause found. If you match several of them, go and register once at psychiatry and psychology. Don't hand someone "look on the bright side" as medicine, and don't block them with "it's not that bad."
    • Where this comes from: B | In 2023 about 14.75% of adults aged 70 and over had a mental disorder, most commonly anxiety or depression [official wording, WHO]; among Chinese middle-aged and older people aged 45 and over, the detection rate of depressive symptoms is 23.61% (2018 CFPS survey, CES-D scale, with the 80th percentile as the cut-off) third-party research. Depression in late life often has physical discomfort as its main complaint.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: people who have been flat for two weeks and can't pull out of it. You have to tell the difference: low spirits after someone close has just died, or after a heavy blow, are normal and will ease slowly; sinking further and further and not being able to get up needs to be seen. If there is any thought of suicide, or a hint of one, don't wait any longer — go straight to 11.8.
    • What you get back: Relationships + independence — no longer afraid of "it isn't him making a fuss about it, he's ill and nobody is taking it seriously."
    • How this works in China: see 11.9 and 11.10.

    11.7 Loneliness is itself a medical problem¶

    • What it costs: time, and the bit of energy it takes to go out (money: not much money | time: every week | effort: none | asking anyone: easy)
    • In short: Loneliness is not just a mood; the harm it does the body is real: your chances of getting ill, of becoming confused, and of dying early all go up. Like smoking, it can be managed as a health risk.
    • What you can do: ① First admit that it is real; don't block it with "you've got plenty of people around, you just like to complain"; ② break "having someone with me" into something concrete: a fixed outing once a week (chess, a walk, doing the shopping with someone), one fixed person you can talk to; ③ use what is already there: community activities, the university of the third age, a choir, eating with others at the community canteen; ④ if you live alone, or your spouse has just died, set yourself one rule: every day somebody knows how I am today (one phone call, one knock on the door).
    • Where this comes from: B | The World Health Organization lists social isolation and loneliness as key risk factors for mental health problems in later life, affecting about one quarter of older people [official wording, WHO]; the WHO Commission on Social Connection's 2025 report estimates that loneliness is linked to about 871,000 deaths a year [official wording, WHO]. The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory compared the mortality effect of social disconnection to smoking 15 cigarettes a day third-party research.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: people who live alone, who have been widowed, who moved to a big city to follow their children (the parents who came to look after grandchildren), whose circle of friends broke up after retirement. Being on your own and being lonely are not the same thing: some people live quietly alone and do well, and some are surrounded by people and are still lonely.
    • What you get back: Relationships — no longer afraid of "nobody around, carrying it on my own."
    • How this works in China: community activities for older people, the university of the third age, community canteens; people who moved to a big city with their children have one more hurdle — medical insurance that doesn't travel, and fitting in (see Chapters 15 and 17).

    11.8 "Living is pointless" has to be heard as meant¶

    • What it costs: one serious answer, one phone call (money: nothing to pay | time: this moment | effort: none | asking anyone: easy, put down the taboo about "don't say unlucky things" first)
    • In short: When an older person says "living is pointless," "I'd be better off dead," "I'm a burden on you," many people let it go past them, taking it for grumbling, for fishing for attention. Those words usually are not fishing for attention. Once an older person has made up their mind, they are often very determined, and there is little room to turn back.
    • What you can do: ① Take it in, don't cut across it, don't say "you're overthinking it"; ② ask one question straight out, "Have you thought about not going on living" — asking does not plant the idea, it gives them a way out; ③ if the person is in front of you, don't leave, and put away anything around that could be used to hurt themselves; ④ get help at once: call a psychological assistance hotline, or take them straight to the nearest hospital emergency department or psychiatric department; ⑤ go with them once to be seen in psychiatry, and don't carry it alone.
    • Where this comes from: A | Depression is a risk factor for suicide in older people, and in older people with depression the suicidal thoughts are more stubborn, the plans more complete, and the success rate higher (Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Depressive Disorders in Late Life (2025 Edition) (《老年期抑郁障碍诊疗专家共识（2025 版）》) official wording). In 2013 the suicide mortality rate in China's 80-and-over group was 73.39 per 100,000, far above the standardized rate for the whole population of 9.08 per 100,000 (Chinese Journal of Epidemiology 2017, based on Global Burden of Disease GBD2013 estimates) third-party research.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: anything of this kind, from anyone, has to be taken seriously, and all the more for people who live alone, who have been widowed, who have just been diagnosed with a serious illness, or who have long-term pain. This section writes only recognition and where to get help; not one word about method or detail.
    • Hotlines (save them into your phone now): ① the national unified psychological assistance hotline 12356, set up by the National Health Commission; the official requirement is for a person to answer at least 18 hours a day, and many places run it round the clock official wording; ② the Hope 24 hotline 400-161-9995, 7×24-hour life-crisis intervention third-party research; ③ the Beijing psychological assistance hotline 010-82951332, answered by a person 24 hours third-party research. If someone is in danger right now, call 120 or 110.
    • What you get back: Relationships — no longer afraid of "missing that cry for help and regretting it for the rest of my life."
    • How this works in China: the hotlines can be called from anywhere in the country; for what other numbers exist locally, go and ask at the community health center or the mental health center.

    11.9 Going to psychiatry: mostly outpatient; admission is about risk¶

    • What it costs: one registration (money: the price of one registration | time: half a day | effort: none | asking anyone: you have to, it is a little embarrassing, you are afraid of being labeled)
    • In short: Going to psychiatry does not mean you are "crazy." Being seen for depression, anxiety and insomnia is like being seen for high blood pressure — you are going to get treated. When an older person goes, it is best if someone in the family goes along.
    • What you can do: ① Which department to register with: the psychiatry or psychology department of a general hospital (some are called clinical psychology, some psychosomatic medicine), and one with a geriatric psychiatry department is better; if there isn't one, register with geriatrics or neurology first and ask them to refer you; ② what the family can do: beforehand, write the symptoms, how long it has been going on, what medicines have been taken, and whether anything hopeless has been said lately onto one page and hand it to the doctor; during the consultation don't jump in to answer for the older person — let them speak, and when they can't go on, you fill in; ③ the difference between outpatient and inpatient: most mild and moderate cases are seen as outpatients with regular follow-up; where there is serious suicide risk, hallucinations or delusions, not eating or sleeping to the point of wasting away, or things getting worse despite outpatient care, the doctor will suggest admission — admission is to keep watch on the risk and adjust treatment in one concentrated stretch, and there is nothing shameful in it; ④ go to follow-ups on time, don't stop the medicine yourself, and give it a few weeks to work — it doesn't work after one day.
    • Where this comes from: C | "Mostly seen as outpatients, admitted only when severe" is common practice. Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Depressive Disorders in Late Life puts psychotic symptoms — hallucinations, delusions, catatonia, stupor — among the indications for urgent psychiatric intervention, and notes that for patients with suicide risk, refusal to eat, or psychotic symptoms, doctors have more active treatment options; where things get worse on outpatient care and any of this appears, the doctor will suggest admission official wording. Who is admitted and who is seen as an outpatient is for the doctor to assess.
    • Who this is for: who it holds for: people with depression, anxiety, insomnia, heavy suspiciousness, or clearly worsening memory. When an older person has been through department after department and nothing is found, don't forget to think of psychiatry and psychology.
    • What you get back: Dignity + relationships — no longer afraid of "being taken for crazy, and dragging it out instead of going to be seen."
    • How this works in China: see 11.10.

    11.10 How this works in China: care points, mental health prevention doctors, hotlines¶

    • Elderly psychological care points: the National Health Commission ran the "Elderly Psychological Care Action" from 2022 to 2025; by the end of 2025, in principle every county (city, district) has at least one community or village with an elderly psychological care point, which does a mental health assessment for residents aged 65 and over, with priority for older people who are in financial difficulty, empty-nest (living alone), left behind, disabled (or with dementia), or in families under the family planning special-support scheme official wording. Go and ask at the community: do we have one of these here?
    • Community mental health prevention doctors: where a household has someone with a severe mental disorder (schizophrenia, for example), the community health center or township health center has a designated prevention doctor responsible for follow-up management; it is part of the national basic public health service, with at least 4 follow-up visits a year official wording. Go and ask at the community: who is the prevention doctor, and how do we get on the register?
    • Hotlines: see 11.8, and get them into your phone first.
    • These services are spread unevenly from place to place; asking once at the community is more accurate than guessing at home. What the local office says most recently governs. The arrangements above are the mainland-China ones — state-run community health centers, and a public health register kept by the local authorities. If you are reading this outside mainland China, the nearest counterpart is the community mental health team and your local crisis line; the structures differ, and this book does not judge which is better.

    11.11 If you can only do one thing today¶

    Save 12356 and 400-161-9995 into your phone and into your parents' phones. Then, at some quiet moment, ask your parents one question: "How have you been sleeping lately — is anything hurting?"

    Ask first; don't wait for them to bring it up. Once that question is out, at least one of the four things stops being "put up with it" and becomes "somebody knows."

    11.12 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    How to say it to him: don't start with "are you depressed?" Start from something concrete: "I've noticed you haven't wanted to go downstairs these past few weeks," "you said you can't sleep — how long has that been going on?" Put the word "illness" later. About pain, don't ask "where does it hurt" either; say "let me take you to get it looked at."

    What you can still do when he won't agree (no need for his nod): ① save 12356 and 400-161-9995 into his phone first, and into yours; ② quietly note what time he goes to bed, how many times he wakes at night, how much he eats, and whether he has said anything hopeless, make it into one page, and show it to a doctor when needed; ③ go to the community and ask whether there is an elderly psychological care point, and what the prevention doctor can help with; ④ the day he is willing to go to the hospital, go with him, write the symptoms on paper first and hand it to the doctor, and don't leave him alone to tell the doctor "I'm fine."

    11.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The nationwide figures on suicide in older people come from 2013 (based on Global Burden of Disease GBD2013 estimates), which is on the early side; for a more recent national picture, the latest mortality surveillance should govern.
    • How sleep clinics, pain clinics, geriatrics and psychiatry are set up and how you register differs from hospital to hospital; whether there is one locally and how to get in, the local situation governs.
    • The 23.61% comes from the 2018 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), where the age cut-off is 45 and over and the scale is CES-D (with the 80th percentile as the cut-off); it, the WHO figure of "14.75% of those aged 70 and over with a mental disorder," and CHARLS-type surveys do not use the same sampling or the same scale, and cannot be compared with each other.
