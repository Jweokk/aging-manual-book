    Chapter 8 · Three red lines for going to hospital, and the first hour¶

    Three in the morning: a heavy tightness in your chest wakes you up, and your back breaks out in a cold sweat. You lie there thinking: wait a bit longer, go to the hospital when it's light.

    Eight in the morning: while brushing your teeth, one of your hands suddenly won't lift, and the cup drops into the sink.

    The right thing to do is the same sentence both times: call 120 — China's ambulance number — right now.

    8.1 What this chapter is for¶

    This chapter is not about diseases, and not about how anything is treated. It settles one thing: in those few minutes when certain symptoms appear, should you call 120 — and what do you say on the phone.

    You are better off never needing it in your life. Sometimes you call 120, get checked, and go home with nothing wrong — a wasted trip. That is better than "wait and see."

    8.2 When you can't tell, calling 120 is not a mistake¶

    • What it costs: one phone call, no money (do you have to talk? not necessarily)
    • In short: It doesn't matter if you can't remember the rest of this. One sentence is enough: when you can't tell whether it's an emergency, call 120 and let the professionals sort it out for you. The dispatcher asks a few questions, then either sends an ambulance or talks you through what to do.
    • What you can do: Put 120 on your phone's speed dial; tape the table in 8.10 next to the phone or on the fridge.
    • Where this comes from: A | The National Health Commission's emergency guidance is simply "if life is in danger, dial 120" — it does not ask you to judge accurately first.
    • Who this is for: It holds for everyone. Don't use 120 as a taxi (for an ordinary doctor visit, a non-emergency).
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "running out of time."

    8.3 Chest pain that won't ease after twenty minutes, with sweating and shortness of breath, means it is time to call 120¶

    • What it costs: one phone call, no money
    • In short: The most typical heart-attack pain is a squeezing feeling in the centre of the chest or just left of it, radiating to the left shoulder, left arm, jaw or back, along with cold sweat, shortness of breath and nausea. The National Health Commission interview gives this dividing line: when this kind of chest tightness or chest pain won't ease after 20 to 30 minutes, you should strongly suspect a heart attack.
    • What you can do: ① Stop at once, sit down or half-recline where you are, and don't keep walking or climbing stairs; ② call 120 immediately, and if you are alone at home, tell your children or a neighbour as you do; ③ if you have a confirmed diagnosis of coronary heart disease and your doctor has prepared emergency medicine for you, take it as prescribed; ④ wait for the ambulance indoors — don't drive yourself.
    • Where this comes from: A | The "three dos and three don'ts" from a Fuwai Hospital specialist in the National Health Commission online interview (2026-09); that heart-attack symptoms in older people and in people with diabetes are often atypical and easily delayed is a point spelled out in the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (2019) (see 8.14).
    • Who this is for: It is useful for people with typical symptoms. If you have diabetes or are very old, you may have little chest pain and instead show sudden shortness of breath, a whole-body cold sweat, nausea, or even fainting — for those cases, think emergency even more. Don't go looking for medicine to take to push the symptoms down — that masks the cause and makes the doctor judge wrong.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid that "toughing it out will make you too late."
    • How this works in China: When you call 120, say clearly "chest pain, how long it's lasted, whether you're sweating," and the dispatcher will send you to a nearby hospital with the capacity to treat chest pain.

    8.4 Face drooping, one arm that won't stay level, unclear speech — any one of these, call 120¶

    • What it costs: one phone call, no money
    • In short: Stroke (cerebral stroke) has a mnemonic ordinary people can remember, called "Stroke 120":
    • 1 — look at one face: ask him to smile, and see whether one side of the face droops while the other is level;
    • 2 — check two arms: ask him to hold both arms out level for ten seconds, and see whether one side drifts down;
    • 0 — a homophone for "listen": listen to speech — ask him to say one complete sentence, and see whether it's slurred or he can't get it out. Sudden numbness in the limbs on one side, severe headache, unsteady walking, or blurry vision may also be a stroke.
    • What you can do: ① If any of these appears, call 120 at once; ② write down the exact time the symptoms started — that time is the most useful thing of all; ③ lay him flat or on his side with the head turned to one side, and loosen the collar to stop vomit choking him; ④ don't give water or medicine, and don't move him back and forth.
    • Where this comes from: A | "Stroke 120" is endorsed by the National Health Commission online interview (2026-09) and the Stroke 120 project (The Lancet Neurology, 2016).
    • Who this is for: It holds for everyone, including people whose symptoms go away on their own after ten or fifteen minutes. That "getting better" is often a "mini-stroke" (transient ischaemic attack), a strong warning of a major stroke to follow — don't let it go just because the symptoms have passed.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "thinking it has passed and it's fine."
    • How this works in China: The sooner you reach a hospital able to give clot-dissolving treatment, the better. Intravenous thrombolysis has a time window: the standard window in current domestic guidelines is within 4.5 hours of onset; a proportion of patients screened by imaging can be extended to 4.5–9 hours after onset (see 8.14).

    8.5 Won't wake up, answers beside the point, suddenly collapses unconscious — that is 120¶

    • What it costs: one phone call, no money
    • In short: Shake his shoulder, shout at him, and see whether there is any response. No response means unconscious. Some people aren't unresponsive to being woken — they answer beside the point, and that counts as another red line.
    • What you can do: ① Call 120; ② see whether his chest rises and falls, and whether he is breathing. If he is not breathing, or takes only one or two sighing breaths (agonal breathing), start CPR at once: stack your hands and press on the midpoint of the line between the two nipples, at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute and a depth of 5 to 6 cm, letting the chest fully recoil each time; ③ if you know how to give rescue breaths, do 30 compressions to 2 breaths; if you don't, just keep compressing without stopping until the ambulance arrives; ④ if there is an AED (automated external defibrillator), fetch it and use it to defibrillate.
    • Where this comes from: A | The CPR and call-for-help wording given in the National Health Commission online interview (2026-09).
    • Who this is for: It holds for unconsciousness that comes on suddenly. Don't take a coma for "asleep," don't pinch the philtrum, don't give water or medicine, don't wait for it to "pass" — none of that helps in an emergency, and all of it can cause choking.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid that "something happens to me alone at night and no one knows."

    8.6 The first hour after a fall: rushing to pull him up often does harm¶

    • What it costs: spend ten minutes observing first, then decide (money: nothing to pay)
    • In short: When an older person falls, the greatest fears are a broken bone and bleeding in the head. "Get him up quickly" is kindly meant, but it can turn an unbroken bone into a broken one and stir the bleeding worse. The original wording of the Technical Guidelines for Fall Intervention in Older Adults is: don't rush to lift him up; handle it according to the situation.
    • What you can do: First don't move him, and ask two questions: "Where does it hurt?" and "How did you fall just now — do you remember?" Then match against the list —
    • If any of the following appears, don't lift him yourself — call 120 at once:
      • won't wake up, dazed, answers off the point, can't remember how he fell;
      • face drooping, slurred speech, can't move the limbs on one side (possibly a stroke);
      • severe headache, severe pain in the neck or lower back, loss of bladder or bowel control, a leg out of shape or agonising pain on any movement (possibly a fracture or spinal injury);
      • if there is bleeding, press on it on the spot with a clean cloth; if he vomits, turn the head to one side and clear the mouth.
    • If he is conscious, can answer, and none of the above applies: you can help him sit up slowly and rest a while, watching him. But that same day someone must go with him to hospital to be seen once — the guidelines say "after any fall, someone should accompany the person to hospital for diagnosis and treatment" — because this fall is often a signal the body gave first (see Chapter 5).
    • Where this comes from: A | The Ministry of Health, Technical Guidelines for Fall Intervention in Older Adults (老年人跌倒干预技术指南) (2011-09-06); in 2026 the National Health Commission issued the further industry standard WS/T 887—2026, Technical Standard for Fall Prevention and Control among Older Adults in the Community (社区老年人跌倒预防控制技术标准).
    • Who this is for: It holds for older people who fall at home. Don't drag or heave him up alone; you should keep him lying as flat as possible and move him steadily; and don't think "it'll be fine after a rest." One more point: people taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs should get to hospital soon even if they only knocked their head and were lucid at the time — this is one of the indications for a head CT (see 8.14).
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "never getting up again after a fall."
    • How this works in China: Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death among older people in China; for what to change at home, see Chapter 5 and Appendix A.

    8.7 In the minute someone is choking, letting them cough beats any manoeuvre¶

    • What it costs: a few dozen seconds (money: nothing to pay)
    • In short: If he can cough and can make a sound, let him cough — don't touch him. Coughing works better than any manoeuvre. Someone who is completely choked is: unable to get air, unable to make a sound, both hands clutching the throat, face turning blue.
    • What you can do: ① For complete choking, lean his upper body forward and give 5 back blows with the heel of the hand between the two shoulder blades, one per second; ② if that doesn't work, do abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre): one hand in a fist, the other wrapping it, placed above the navel, and thrust quickly up and in 5 times; ③ alternate the two actions until the object comes out; ④ if he loses consciousness, lay him flat and start CPR while someone else calls 120.
    • For how to save yourself if you live alone, what to do for a pregnant woman or a child under one year old, and the full four-step write-up, see Chapter 7, 7.2 — that is the full text of this item, and its source is noted there.
    • Where this comes from: A | Same source as Chapter 7, 7.2: the first-aid algorithm updated in 2025 by the American Heart Association and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (back blows first, then abdominal thrusts), which the Red Cross Society of China's training has taught this way all along.
    • Who this is for: It holds for older people choking on food or medicine just as much as for anyone else. Don't give water, and don't dig with a finger (something you can't see just gets pushed further in the more you dig).
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid of "a mouthful of food killing someone."

    8.8 Four of the most lethal thoughts — don't believe any of them¶

    What you will think Why it's dangerous What to do
    "Sleep it off / it'll pass with a rest" Stroke symptoms can go away on their own and it can still be a "mini-stroke"; a heart attack certainly won't improve with sleep Call 120 the moment the symptom appears
    "Wait and see" Treating a heart attack or stroke is counted in minutes; the time you spend waiting is heart muscle and brain that can't be saved Call now
    "Take some medicine first to push it down" Taking medicine at random masks the symptoms and makes the doctor judge wrong; chest pain may not be a heart attack either Take medicine only as your own doctor prescribed, and never as a substitute for 120
    "Drive / cycle to the hospital myself" Your judgement and strength are both dropping, and if something happens on the way no one can help; 120 can take you to a nearby hospital with the capacity to treat you and starts care on the way Call 120 and let the professionals come

    8.9 Say three things on the 120 call, then notify your children as well¶

    • What it costs: one to two minutes (money: nothing to pay)
    • In short: The 120 dispatcher will ask several things, but wants three first: address, condition, phone number. Once these three are clear, the vehicle is sent out first, and the rest is filled in as it goes.
    • How to do it — say it like this:
    • Address: province, city and district + street and neighbourhood + building number + entrance + unit number. If the unit number is hard to find, give an obvious landmark. Then send someone to wait at the neighbourhood gate or the junction, and wave when they see the ambulance.
    • Condition: one sentence with age, sex, what the symptom is, and when it started — for example, "seventy years old, male, chest pain for half an hour, pouring cold sweat."
    • Phone number: leave a number that is on the scene, not just your mobile phone somewhere else. When calling 120 from outside the area, this is the easiest point to get wrong. Don't hang up in a hurry — wait until the dispatcher has confirmed, and keep the line open.
    • Whether to notify your children at the same time: yes. Calling 120 and calling your children don't conflict; it's one phone call done alongside. Your children can get there to meet the ambulance and bring the medical-insurance card (yibao card) and the medication record.
    • Who this is for: The order is call 120 first, then notify your children. Don't "wait for the children to arrive before deciding" — that's the slowest way to wait. If you are alone at home and already can't speak, use your phone's SOS if you can, or bang on the door, knock on the heating pipes, or phone a neighbour.
    • What you get back: Independence + relationships — no longer afraid of "not being able to explain when something happens, with no one to lend a hand."

    8.10 Print this page out and tape it next to the phone¶

    (This is the full-text version of Appendix D.)

    What you see What it suggests What to do
    Squeezing chest pain radiating to the left shoulder, arm or jaw, with sweating and shortness of breath, not easing after 20–30 minutes Suspected heart attack Stop; sit or lie down; call 120; don't drive yourself
    One side of the face drooping / one arm drifting down when both are held out / unclear speech; or sudden numbness on one side, severe headache, unsteady walking Suspected stroke Note the time it started to the minute; call 120; lay him on his side to prevent choking; give no water and don't move him
    Won't wake up, answers beside the point, suddenly collapses Unconscious Check breathing; if not breathing, start CPR; call 120
    After a fall: won't wake up / answers off the point / face drooping / severe headache / severe back pain / loss of bladder or bowel control / a leg out of shape and can't move Suspected fracture or problem inside the skull Don't lift, don't move; call 120; press on bleeding on the spot
    Can't speak, clutching the throat, face turning blue Complete choking 5 back blows ↔ 5 abdominal thrusts; if he loses consciousness, CPR + 120
    Can't get air, extreme difficulty breathing A serious problem with breathing Call 120, don't wait
    Heavy bleeding, blood flowing out and won't stop Blood loss Call 120
    Convulsions (seizures) that won't stop, or one after another Something acute happening in the brain Call 120
    Took the wrong medicine, or an unknown substance Risk of poisoning Call 120, don't wait for it to pass
    Wetting yourself / forgetting to turn off the stove / a one-off headache / a one-off racing heart, while awake and fine soon after Not necessarily an emergency Write it down, see a doctor in good time; no need to call 120

    The last four of these weren't listed as separate items above, and they are just as lethal: can't get air, extreme difficulty breathing — not enough air, and a few minutes' delay can be more than the person can take; heavy bleeding, blood flowing out and won't stop — the blood keeps draining and the person gives out fast; convulsions (seizures) that won't stop, or one after another — seizing without let-up means something acute is happening in the brain; took the wrong medicine, or an unknown substance — nobody knows what was swallowed, and waiting only makes it worse, so don't wait for the person to come round slowly. If you see any one of these, do one thing only: call 120, and say clearly "what the symptom is and when it started."

    Two rules that don't fit in the table but matter more: ① If you can't tell, call 120. ② Call 120 first, then notify people.

    Print it out, fill it in, and tape it next to the phone:

    The chest-pain centre / stroke centre nearest my home: __________ Community or family-doctor phone: __________ Children's phones: __________ Neighbours' phones: __________

    8.11 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    Print the table in 8.10 out larger and tape it next to the phone at your parents' home, with the address, community phone, and your and your siblings' numbers filled in. When you call 120 on a parent's behalf, the medication list, medical history and insurance type in your hands are the most useful things (Chapters 0 and 19).

    8.12 The one thing you can do today¶

    Take a photo of the top three rows of this chapter's decision table, save it to your phone's album and pin it; then add 120 to your phone's speed dial.

    You don't have to remember all of it today. When the day really comes, the only things you need to recall are: call 120, don't drive yourself, and note down the time to the minute.

    8.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The thrombolysis time window updates with the method of treatment (there is now an extra tier of "extended time window after imaging screening"). This chapter follows the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischaemic Stroke 2023 in writing "within 4.5 hours of onset as the standard, extendable to 4.5–9 hours with imaging screening"; for exactly what to do, the guideline and protocol your hospital is following that day governs.
    • For how to assess people on anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs after a fall, and when to do a CT, this chapter writes only "seek medical attention as soon as possible"; the specific handling is left to the emergency department and neurosurgery, and this text does not decide it on their behalf.
