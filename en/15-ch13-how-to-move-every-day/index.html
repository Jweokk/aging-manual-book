    Chapter 13 · How to Move Every Day¶

    After dinner you go downstairs and walk two laps around the compound. Ten minutes in, your knees ache, and you remember the line you hear all the time — "you're old now, don't push yourself" — so you turn around and go home. The next day, two laps again. The third day it rains, you don't go. A month later, the two laps are gone too.

    Nobody stopped you. You stopped yourself. This chapter is for starting again, and it's also for you if you're the one trying to get your parents moving.

    13.1 What this chapter solves¶

    Of the four accounts, movement is the cheapest one, and the only one that improves several at once: whether you can still dress yourself and get out of bed (independence), your mood, your blood sugar and blood pressure, whether you can sleep at night. Most pills fix one thing. Move once, and several things move together.

    Two sentences stop most people: "to exercise you have to run, and work up a sweat," and "you're old, don't go messing about with exercise." Both are wrong, and wrong in different ways. Below I take the two sentences apart, then give you numbers you can measure, movements you can do sitting down, and where it's worth doing them.

    13.2 Move once, and four accounts get paid down together¶

    • In short: in the World Health Organization's guidelines, regular physical activity lowers the risk of all-cause death, death from cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes and falls in older adults, and improves anxiety and depression, cognition and sleep (official wording). No drug covers that many at once.
    • Where this comes from: A | this is the direction written into both the WHO guidelines and China's guidelines.
    • Who this is for: people who can still walk a few steps on their own. If you're already bedbound and can't get up, the way you move is completely different and a rehabilitation therapist has to set it (see 13.12).
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "every day I can do a little less"
    • How this works in China: it costs nothing. Whether you need tests, and how to train, ask the contracted family doctor at your community health centre (see 13.12).

    13.3 What stops you isn't your body, it's two sentences¶

    The first sentence: "to exercise you have to run, and work up a sweat." A lot of people hear "exercise" and think of running, and decide "I can't run, so never mind." The aerobic activity in the guidelines means endurance activity — walking, cycling, swimming, dancing — and brisk walking counts (official wording). Running is only one of them, not the entry threshold.

    The second sentence: "you're old, don't go messing about with exercise." It sounds like concern. It does the most damage. You're afraid of falling, so you stop moving; the less you move, the weaker your legs and the worse your balance, and the more likely you are to fall. The Chinese CDC's core messages on preventing falls list "being so afraid of falling that you just stop moving" as something to avoid (official wording).

    Put the two sentences together and you're wedged between them: the big movements aren't allowed, and the small ones don't count.

    • In short: it isn't that you don't want to move; it's that you haven't found the movement that is "small enough, and not embarrassing". 13.6 is for that.
    • Where this comes from: A | the World Health Organization and the Chinese CDC say the same thing (official wording).
    • Who this is for: people who are afraid of falling, afraid of looking foolish, afraid they can't do it.
    • What you get back: dignity — you stop fearing "I want to move, and I feel it's too late, that I can't"
    • How this works in China: "don't stop moving because you're afraid of falling" sits inside the Chinese CDC's twelve-character fall-prevention slogan — "exercise, accept that you're older, make your home age-friendly, treat early" (see Chapter 5).

    13.4 Four kinds of movement, each with its own job¶

    Kind What it covers Examples
    Aerobic heart and lungs, blood pressure and blood sugar, mood, sleep brisk walking, cycling, swimming, square dancing
    Resistance (strength) strength in your legs, enough to stand up and hold yourself sit-to-stand, heel raises, resistance bands, water bottles
    Balance catches you when your body can't adjust in time standing on one leg, heel-to-toe walking, tai chi
    Flexibility joints that can reach, and reach far shoulder rolls, calf stretch
    • In short: aerobic keeps the "engine" running; resistance keeps the "frame" standing. The falls in Chapter 5 are mainly covered by resistance and balance; this chapter only fills in the amounts.
    • Where this comes from: A | the four-way split is the framework both the WHO and China's guidelines use (official wording).
    • Who this is for: everyone. Walking without any strength work is the most common imbalance in older people.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "I've been at this for ages and I don't know which thing I'm training"
    • How this works in China: tai chi classes and fitness groups in the community, in parks, at the university of the third age bring aerobic and balance together.

    13.5 Working out the weekly amount¶

    This is the one set of numbers to remember from this chapter.

    • Aerobic: 150 to 300 minutes a week at moderate intensity, or 75 to 150 minutes at vigorous intensity, and a mix of the two counts as well (official wording World Health Organization, 2020; Physical Activity Guidelines for the Chinese Population (2021) (《中国人群身体活动指南（2021）》)).
    • Resistance: at least 2 days a week, working the main muscle groups (official wording same as above). The British Journal of Sports Medicine pooled 16 studies: 30 to 60 minutes of strength training a week goes with about 10% to 20% lower risk of all-cause death (third-party research Momma et al., 2022).
    • Balance plus strength (multicomponent): at least 3 days a week (official wording World Health Organization, 2020).
    • Broken down to a day: 150 minutes divided by 5 is 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week (official wording Healthy China Initiative, National Fitness Action).
    • What counts as moderate: score it by feel, 5 to 6 out of 0 to 10 — you can talk, but you can't sing (official wording). The Healthy China Initiative gives another way to work it out: a heart rate of 64% to 76% of your maximum, where maximum heart rate is 220 minus your age (official wording).

    • How older people adjust: the adult recommendation applies to older adults too; if your body won't allow 150 minutes of moderate intensity a week, move as much as you can (official wording Physical Activity Guidelines for the Chinese Population (2021) (《中国人群身体活动指南（2021）》)).

    • Where this comes from: A | on amount, intensity and frequency, the international and the Chinese guidelines line up.

    • Who this is for: people who can stand and walk. If you can barely move at all, or a certain movement hurts as soon as you try it, put the numbers down and go ask a doctor (see 13.7, 13.12).
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "I don't know how much counts as enough, so I just don't move"
    • How this works in China: this set of numbers is in the Physical Activity Guidelines for the Chinese Population (2021) (《中国人群身体活动指南（2021）》); whether you're getting enough, ask the contracted family doctor at your community health centre.

    13.6 Do it sitting down, do it holding the chair back, do it while the TV is on¶

    One rule first: if any of the feelings in 13.7 shows up afterwards, stop.

    Sitting down (after a meal, in front of the TV, anytime):

    • Seated leg raise: sit up straight, slowly lift one leg until it's level, hold 3 seconds, put it down. 8 to 12 times per leg, 2 sets.
    • Ankle pumps (flex and point): heel on the floor, toes up, then press down. 20 times. If you sit a lot, this helps the blood in your calves flow back.
    • Seated desk push: both hands on the edge of the table, push slowly for 5 seconds, then relax. 5 times.
    • When to stop: sharp pain inside the knee, numbness in the leg, a racing heart.

    Holding the back of a chair (closer to daily life):

    • Chair-supported heel raise: hold the chair back firmly, slowly rise onto your toes and come down. 10 to 15 times, 2 sets.
    • Chair-supported single-leg stand (balance): hold the chair back firmly, lift one foot and stand for 10 to 30 seconds, 3 times per leg; once you're steady, slowly take away the support of your hands.
    • Chair-supported sit-to-stand: stand up from the chair and sit down again (see 5.8), 8 to 12 times, 2 sets, using your hands as little as possible.
    • When to stop: unsteady on your feet, dizzy, tightness in the chest.

    While the TV is on:

    • When the ads come on, get up — march in place or go and pour a glass of water. Two minutes.
    • Wall sit: back against the wall, feet moved half a foot forward, slide down to a height that doesn't hurt, hold 10 to 30 seconds.
    • Use a resistance band or a bottle of water for curls and shoulder presses, while you watch. 2 sets.
    • When to stop: heat or pain at the front of the knee, your lower back can't hold it.

    Flexibility (save it for the end): shoulder rolls, calf stretch, seated arm raises. Slow; stop when it feels "a bit tight"; don't bounce.

    • Where this comes from: C | the movement categories are taken from the few the WHO and the Chinese CDC mention (heel raises, sit-to-stand, standing on one leg and so on); the repetitions and sets follow common practice, and no study has specifically tested "how many sets you should do while watching TV".
    • Who this is for: people who can sit unaided and stand holding something. If you use a walker, have had a joint replaced, or have severe osteoporosis, have a rehabilitation therapist run through the movements once first.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "I want to move and I don't know which movement to start with"
    • How this works in China: you can do these at home. If you want someone watching you do them, ask at the community activity room or the community health centre whether they have fitness instruction.

    13.7 If any of this shows up, stop and ask a doctor first¶

    During exercise, if any one of these shows up, stop at once — don't push through: tightness, pressure or pain in the chest, or pain that runs up into the shoulder, arm or jaw; dizziness, black spots before your eyes, feeling sick; breathlessness so bad you can't speak, a racing or irregular heartbeat; breaking out in cold sweat.

    Beijing's 120 service warns that chest tightness during exercise needs to be taken seriously, and that you should call 120 promptly (official wording Beijing Municipal Health Commission, 2022). If speech goes slurred, or one side of your body goes weak, call 120 straight away.

    In these situations you have to ask a doctor before you move: a heart attack or stroke recently, or just out of hospital; blood pressure or blood sugar badly out of control; cancer that has spread to bone (bone metastases), or a fracture recently; fever, infection, or an acute illness that isn't over yet. For people with chronic disease, China's guideline states plainly that you should consult a doctor before exercising and exercise under professional guidance (official wording Physical Activity Guidelines for the Chinese Population (2021) (《中国人群身体活动指南（2021）》)).

    If you're a cancer patient, a Chinese expert consensus draws this line more finely: with bone metastases, or a pathological fracture or spinal cord compression already present, exercise is not suitable; with bone metastases, or known or suspected osteoporosis, don't do routine muscle strength and muscular endurance testing; with an acute systemic illness, fever, or the acute phase of a cerebral hemorrhage or cerebral thrombosis, don't move yet (third-party research Chinese Expert Consensus on Exercise Therapy for Patients with Malignant Tumors (2022 edition) (《中国恶性肿瘤患者运动治疗专家共识（2022 年版）》)).

    • Where this comes from: B | "chest tightness or a racing heart means stop at once and get medical care" is written repeatedly in official material; the bone-metastasis and acute-fever lines are set out item by item in a Chinese expert consensus (see sources); "a fracture recently" is standard orthopaedic rehabilitation practice, and I found no specific guideline line for it.
    • Who this is for: everyone; if you have a history of heart disease, diabetes, osteoporosis or cancer, the line has to be drawn tighter.
    • What you get back: independence and money — you stop fearing "I'm afraid something will happen while I'm exercising, and I don't know where the red line is"
    • How this works in China: for an emergency, call 120; read these lines out to your contracted family doctor at the community health centre and ask them to draw the line for you once.

    13.8 "Stop when it hurts" is wrong, but you have to tell which pain¶

    "Don't move if it hurts" and "train through the pain" are equally bad. The thing to separate is two kinds of pain.

    • Soreness from doing it right: the soreness sits in the muscle, arrives a day or two after, and eases when you move a little or rest. It's usually the response to a new movement or a heavier load.
    • Pain that means stop: the pain sits in a joint (knee, shoulder), shows up during the movement, catches or feels like a needle when you move in a certain direction, or gets worse the more you train and swells. This is your body calling stop.

    • How to use it: if it's muscle soreness, you can carry on at low intensity; if it's sharp joint pain, catching or swelling, stop and go to orthopaedics or rehabilitation.

    • Where this comes from: C | "separate muscle soreness from joint-injury pain, and the latter needs medical care" is standard sports-medicine practice; hospitals' patient-education material says the same, and there is no graded guideline.

    • Who this is for: people who can tell "sore" from "pain" and can still move normally. If you wake at night with the pain, if it's bad enough to disturb sleep, or if there's fever with it, see a doctor soon.
    • What you get back: independence — you stop fearing "it hurts when I move, and I don't know whether I should tough it out"
    • How this works in China: register at an orthopaedics or rehabilitation medicine outpatient clinic; the contracted family doctor at the community health centre can also make a first judgment and refer you upward.

    13.9 Sitting too long is the most underrated item¶

    Sitting too long is harmful in itself, and whether you also go and exercise is a separate account. In 2020 the WHO put "sit less" into its guidelines as its own item for the first time: sit less, and replace sitting with activity of any intensity — even light intensity counts (official wording).

    • What you can do: cards, TV, the car, the phone — set an alarm, get up for two minutes every hour: pour a glass of water, walk a lap, do a few heel raises.
    • A small randomized crossover trial gives a finer version: 11 people each sat for a full 8 hours under several "break up the sitting" schedules, and only the "get up and walk for 5 minutes every 30 minutes" arm had a clearly smaller rise in blood sugar after meals; for blood pressure a smaller dose was enough, and getting up to walk for 1 minute every 60 minutes lowered it the most (third-party research Duran et al., 2023).
    • Where this comes from: B | "less sitting is better" is the line both international and Chinese guidelines agree on; the "5 minutes every 30" comes from a small randomized crossover trial of only 11 people, looking at that day's blood sugar and blood pressure response, and the long-term outcome isn't settled (see 13.15).
    • Who this is for: everyone who sits a lot. But for people who can't stand at all (wheelchair, bedbound), the substitute is different — ask a rehabilitation therapist.
    • What you get back: independence and money — you stop fearing "a day of sitting, and by evening my legs are swollen and my blood sugar is up"
    • How this works in China: no equipment at all; an alarm on your phone is enough.

    13.10 Only a few things are worth buying, and only a few places are worth going¶

    • Small money worth spending: a pair of well-fitting shoes with a treaded sole that isn't too soft (how to choose, see 5.6); a resistance band (a few dozen yuan); an empty water bottle filled with water as a dumbbell.
    • What can wait: big items like a treadmill or a spin bike. Before you buy, ask yourself: the last time you stuck with exercise, how many weeks did it last?
    • Don't spend extra on these: fat-shaking machines, "qi and blood circulation" machines, "unblocking" massagers. Claims like these are the manufacturer's, not evidence ([manufacturer's claim]).

    • Where to go: if you only want to walk, the compound, the park, the walking trails — free is best; if you want balance and strength work and you're afraid of falling, go where there are people — the community activity room, a fitness group; if you have heart disease or diabetes, or have had surgery, one session of an "exercise prescription" or at a rehabilitation clinic is worth the money (see 13.12).

    • Where this comes from: C | "put your money into a good pair of shoes and a resistance band" is standard advice, with no controlled study behind it.

    • Who this is for: people on a limited budget who are just thinking of starting. If you already train regularly at a gym, you don't need to change where you go to save money.
    • What you get back: money — you stop fearing "I buy a pile of equipment and it all ends up gathering dust"
    • How this works in China: the free equipment points in parks and communities and the classes at the university of the third age are the cheapest tier; ask the residents' committee where the nearest ones are.

    13.11 Stick with it through a partner and a record¶

    Getting moving isn't hard. What's hard is going on the third day, when it rains.

    • Hang it on a habit you already have: tie movement to something you do every day and can't forget. "After dinner I go downstairs." "After the news, one set of sit-to-stands."
    • Find a partner: fix on one person, and whoever doesn't show owes the other one. Square-dance teams, walking groups, tai chi groups are all partners.
    • Keep a record: tick a calendar, count steps on your phone. It doesn't have to be exact; a tick is enough.

    • Start small: the WHO's advice is to do a little first, then slowly add amount and time (official wording). 5 minutes today is more likely to survive than a plan to "start Monday, 30 minutes a day".

    • Where this comes from: C | these behavioral methods are standard practice; which one works best differs from person to person.

    • Who this is for: everyone. If you've been diagnosed with depression, or the pain keeps you from getting downstairs, deal with that first (see Chapter 11, 13.12).
    • What you get back: relationships — you stop fearing "on my own, I move twice and then stop"
    • How this works in China: find a partner in a community fitness group or a square-dance team; ask the residents' committee or the community eldercare service station how to join.

    13.12 How this works in China¶

    • The equipment in housing compounds and parks is mostly free. The state is pushing a "15-minute fitness circle" in urban communities, meaning a place to exercise within 15 minutes' walk or cycle; a 2025 survey by the General Administration of Sport found that 90.4% of residents aged 19 and over can reach one within 15 minutes (official wording). Ask the property office or the residents' committee where the nearest equipment point and the bigger parks are.
    • If you want people to move with: community aerobics groups, square-dance teams, tai chi groups, walking groups — mostly in parks, on squares, in community activity rooms. Where to ask: the residents' committee, the community eldercare service station, the university of the third age.
    • If you want to know whether you're moving enough: communities and physical fitness monitoring stations run the national physical fitness test, which measures balance, strength and flexibility, using the National Physical Fitness Test Standards (2023 revision) issued by the General Administration of Sport (official wording). Where to ask: the community health centre, the district physical fitness monitoring station.
    • If you have a chronic disease, have had surgery, or are afraid of falling: register at a rehabilitation medicine or geriatrics department and have a doctor set out an "exercise prescription" once — they cover everything from cardiac rehabilitation to fall-prevention training. Where to ask: the rehabilitation medicine clinic at a tier-3 hospital, or your contracted family doctor.
    • If you're not sure how to apply locally or what it costs: ask the community health centre and the residents' committee. Specific programs and subsidies differ from place to place; go by the latest local wording.

    13.13 If you can only do one thing today¶

    Set an alarm on your phone for "get up for two minutes every hour", starting this afternoon.

    No changing shoes, no going out. When the alarm goes, stand up and walk about, pour a glass of water, do a few heel raises. Sitting too long is the cheapest thing in this chapter to change, and the one fewest people change.

    13.14 If you're reading this for your parents¶

    The most useless sentence in trying to get your parents moving is "you need to exercise more". What he hears is "you're finished".

    Say it differently, start with "I": "My back can't take sitting all day lately. Let's walk for half an hour together after dinner — come with me." You're not sending him off to exercise; you need him to keep you company. Older people are more willing to go out for "being needed".

    When he won't agree, the things you can do that don't need his agreement: change the environment first — walk the route from his building to the park with him, find the nearest free equipment point and the easiest flat path; find him a partner, don't be his supervisor — help him connect with a group, with someone he knows; whoever can get him out the door does more than a hundred things you say; hang it on his old habits — whatever he does every single day, put the walk right after it; see the doctor when it's a doctor matter — chest tightness, dizziness, bone pain, go with him to register once, and read the lines in 13.7 out to the doctor.

    He may not admit it out loud. His body will.

    13.15 What I'm not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The "get up and walk for 5 minutes every 30 minutes" arm comes from a small randomized crossover trial of only 11 people, looking at the acute response of blood sugar after meals and blood pressure that same day; whether it translates into fewer illnesses and fewer hospital stays over the long run is not settled, and there is no local research on the equivalent dose for Chinese people. This chapter treats it as a direction, not a precise prescription.
    • The square-dancing randomized controlled trial had a sample that was mostly women (about 78%); the intervention group trained 3 times a week, while the control group had only 3 health-education classes in 12 weeks, so the two groups' contact time was not equal; the authors' own conclusion is "associated", not "causal", and you should be careful about generalizing to men, or about treating it as "square dancing definitely does this".
