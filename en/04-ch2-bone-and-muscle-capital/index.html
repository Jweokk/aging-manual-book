    Chapter 2 · Bone and Muscle Capital: Two Accounts You Can Only Draw From, Never Pay Into¶

    Last winter, your mother stumbled in the stairwell and only kept from falling by grabbing the handrail. She waved it off as nothing. But you noticed: she has got a little shorter over these past two years, and her hands aren't steady when she carries things.

    You assume this is just "getting old". In fact there are two accounts she never paid into enough back then, and now she draws them down year by year.

    This book keeps four accounts, and the first one is independence. Being able to walk on your own, get up on your own, carry things on your own — that rests on two accounts: bone and muscle. This chapter covers three things: how to tell how much you have left, where you can top up today, and when you should see a doctor instead of just pushing through on your own.

    2.1 These two accounts are the exact opposite of a savings account¶

    Money can be saved late in life; bone and muscle can't. Both have a window, up to roughly age thirty: eat right and move enough before then, and the account grows year by year. Past that window, less and less goes in and more and more comes out. The question isn't "what do I do once I'm old" — it's "how much can I still put in now".

    2.2 Bone capital: it tops out in your thirties, then only goes out¶

    Bone grows your whole life, reaching its peak around age thirty — this is peak bone mass. After thirty to thirty-five it starts to decline (official wording Beijing Municipal Health Commission, "Strengthen Your Bones, Refuse 'Osteoporosis'" 2023; Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025). The higher the peak and the later it comes, the more you can draw on for the rest of your life.

    Why it declines: bone contains two sets of cells, one that tears down old bone and one that builds new bone. When you're young, building outruns tearing down; after thirty, tearing down overtakes it. The trabeculae — the spongy struts inside bone — thin out and grow sparse, and the bone turns brittle.

    Women lose the most at menopause. Oestrogen normally holds the brake on the "bone-tearing" cells; after menopause it plunges, and bone is lost fastest in the first five to ten years after menopause, at 2 to 3 percent a year (official wording Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）); third-party research Xinhua Net 2025). Men lose bone too, just more slowly and later: prevalence in men over 65 is 10.7 percent, with gonadal function declining after 70 (official wording Beijing Municipal Health Commission 2023).

    The numbers for China: prevalence over age 50 is 19.2 percent (women 32.1, men 6.9); over age 65, 32 percent (women 51.6, men 10.7); and among people over 40, 40.9 percent already have low bone mass (official wording Beijing Municipal Health Commission 2023; Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025).

    One honest thing: half your foundation is genetic, and you can't change that; whether you lose bone fast or slow is half in your own hands.

    2.3 Muscle capital: it peaks at thirty too, and falls faster after sixty¶

    Muscle mass generally peaks around the thirties or forties, then declines year by year. How fast varies by source: one popular-science piece says it peaks at 30, then drops 1 to 2 percent a year, accelerating after 60 (third-party research China Food and Drug Network 2022); another article reviewed by the Chinese Medical Association says that after 50, leg muscle mass falls 1 to 2 percent a year and muscle strength 1.5 to 5 percent a year (third-party research Xinhua Net 2024). The two sources don't fully agree on the peak age or the rate, but they agree on the direction: past the peak it declines every year, and faster after 60. Drop far enough and it becomes a disease called sarcopenia; among community-dwelling older people in China prevalence runs 8.9 to 38.8 percent, reaching 67.1 percent over age 80 (third-party research Xinhua Net 2024).

    Once the muscle on your legs thins you walk unsteadily, walking unsteadily makes you fall, and a fall puts you in bed for three months and costs you still more muscle — the same loop as the falls loop in Chapter 5. Be careful with the old saying "you can't buy leanness in old age": thin is not the goal, and being underweight is itself a risk factor (official wording Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）)).

    2.4 A rough self-test at home: grip strength, walking speed, calf circumference¶

    • What it costs: basically nothing; a grip dynamometer is optional (Money: barely any money ｜ Time: ten minutes ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: no)
    • In short: these three numbers are the screening method set by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia — screen roughly at home first, and leave the formal judgment to a doctor.
    • What you can do: ① Grip strength: with your writing hand, squeeze the dynamometer with the arm hanging down; under 28 kg for men, under 18 kg for women counts as low. ② Walking speed: walk a straight 6-metre path; under 1 metre per second is slow. ③ Calf circumference: measure the thickest part while sitting; under 34 cm for men, under 33 cm for women suggests too little muscle. ④ The five-question SARC-F questionnaire: take a score of 4 seriously. Muscle mass itself requires DXA at a hospital; below 7 kg/m² for men, below 5.4 for women counts as low (all above third-party research: AWGS 2019).
    • Where this comes from: B ｜ the above are the standard criteria of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS 2019) (diagnostic criteria). But the cut-offs come from multi-country Asian populations; finer China-specific lines are still being researched, so don't treat this as a diagnosis. Also note: a looser set of figures also circulates online — grip strength under 25 kg for men, walking speed under 0.8 m/s; those are an earlier line used for preliminary screening, and AWGS 2019 governs an actual diagnosis.
    • Who this is for: people who can stand and walk on their own. If you can't grip, get winded after a few steps, or have fallen in the past six months, don't test at home — go straight to the hospital.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I'm not sure whether I can still manage"
    • How this works in China: once you've measured, ask the contracted family doctor team at your community health centre whether it matters and whether you need a DXA scan.

    2.5 Resistance training and weight-bearing activity: make your muscles work, put load on your bones¶

    • What it costs: a resistance band costs a few dozen yuan; a pair of small dumbbells or two bottles of water will do (Money: barely any money ｜ Time: 20 minutes a session, two or three times a week ｜ Effort: somewhat tiring ｜ Need to ask someone: no)
    • In short: making your muscles work against a resistance (resistance training) and letting your body weight press down through your legs and spine (weight-bearing) are the two things that build muscle and bone — walking alone can't make up for it.
    • What you can do: ① Resistance: two to three times a week, working the same muscle again after a day's gap; 8 to 12 repetitions per set, two to three sets; start with legs and back — sit-to-stand, wall squats, band rows, biceps curls, and grip-squeezing all count. ② Weight-bearing: walk enough each day, totalling 150 minutes of moderate intensity a week; brisk walking, jogging, and stair-climbing (if your knees are okay) all load the bone, while swimming and cycling put little load on bone and can't replace them ([third-party research: West China Hospital reprint of the Chinese Expert Consensus on Prevention and Intervention of Physical Function Impairment in Older Adults (2022) (《老年人躯体功能受损防控干预中国专家共识（2022）》); official wording: Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025]).
    • Where this comes from: B ｜ both kinds of training are unanimously recommended by Chinese geriatric-medicine consensus. But swimming and cycling have limited effect on raising bone density.
    • Who this is for: people who can stand steadily, sit and stand on their own, and walk without pain — start from a seated position and your own body weight. If you have heart problems, painful joints, uncontrolled blood pressure, or get winded after two steps, have a doctor look first before you start; while training you should still be able to talk normally.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "my legs are getting weaker and can't hold me up"
    • How this works in China: communities and universities of the third age often run resistance and resistance-band classes and walking groups; ask the community health centre or the community eldercare service station.

    2.6 Jumping movements: those with strong bones train with it, those with brittle bones avoid it¶

    • What it costs: nothing (Money: nothing ｜ Time: a few minutes at a time ｜ Effort: quite tiring ｜ Need to ask someone: yes, easy)
    • In short: when you jump and land, the ground throws a jolt back up into your leg bones and spine; for bones that are still solid, that "shake" is a signal telling them to get harder.
    • What you can do: if your bones have been checked and are fine, you can start with very small hops in place, or hopping off a low step — keep the dose small and build up slowly. If your knees, ankles, or lower back are injured, switch to landing on the balls of your feet and walking briskly downhill instead.
    • Where this comes from: C ｜ the idea that "impact-type movement stimulates bone" has research support. But how much jumping actually helps bones get harder has no quantified domestic evidence; a 2025 consensus warns that people with exercise contraindications should avoid explosive and high-impact movements (official wording Shenyang Municipal Health Commission 2026, citing the Expert Consensus on Nutritional and Exercise Management of Primary Osteoporosis and Sarcopenia (2025) (《原发性骨质疏松症与肌少症的营养和运动管理专家共识（2025年）》)).
    • Who this is for: the "who this holds for" matters most with this one. People already diagnosed with low bone mass or osteoporosis, or who have had a fragility fracture, should not practise jumping on their own — the impact of landing can crush a bone outright; people with brittle bones should train weight-bearing, resistance, and balance, not jumping. Those who are overweight or have knee or ankle problems aren't suited to it either.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I can't tell whether bone gets harder or more brittle the more I use it"
    • How this works in China: this one is not something to practise, it's something to ask — take "can I jump, can I run?" to an orthopaedics or rehabilitation doctor.

    2.7 Calcium and protein: get both from your plate, don't count on calcium tablets alone¶

    • What it costs: a glass of milk, an egg, and a handful of soy products on the table every day — several dozen to one or two hundred yuan more a month (Money: some money ｜ Time: daily ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: no)
    • In short: calcium is the brick in bone, protein is the raw material for building muscle. Get enough of both from food first — a handful of calcium tablets alone won't rebuild muscle.
    • What you can do: ① Calcium: 300 to 500 mL of milk or yoghurt a day, plus tofu, dried tofu, and bean curd sticks; add rape greens, Chinese kale, and broccoli; small fish and shrimp eaten whole with the bones, and sesame paste, contribute a little too. Drink fewer carbonated drinks and less strong coffee, and use less salt. ② Protein: eggs, milk, fish and shrimp, lean meat, and soy products all count — put a little in every meal, don't pile it all into dinner; if chewing is a problem, use steamed egg, fish paste, or tofu instead. People with sarcopenia need more protein, and how much is calculated by body weight (official wording Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025; Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）); third-party research Xinhua Net 2024).
    • Where this comes from: A ｜ "eat more calcium-rich foods, use calcium supplements if intake is short, and get enough protein" is written into Chinese guidelines and the older-adult nutrition consensus. But how much to take daily, whether to use calcium tablets, and how many grams of protein all have to be decided by a doctor, taking diet, blood tests, and kidney function into account.
    • Who this is for: most people. People with kidney stones, poor kidney function, or high blood calcium are different: they must be especially cautious about calcium supplementation and can't eat a lot of protein either — ask a doctor first.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "my bone is draining away and I don't know what to put in my mouth"
    • How this works in China: first go to a hospital for a bone-density scan and a kidney-function test, then decide what to eat and what to supplement. Where to ask: the community health centre, or the hospital's endocrinology or orthopaedics department; if there's a nutrition clinic, have the doctor calculate by body weight. Don't buy calcium tablets on your own from advertising.

    2.8 Vitamin D: sun the right time of day, sun the right body parts¶

    • What it costs: nothing (Money: nothing ｜ Time: a few times a week, ten-odd minutes each ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: yes, easy)
    • In short: for calcium to get into bone, vitamin D has to help carry it. It's made mainly by your own skin from sunlight, and there's almost none in food; if you get little sun, the calcium you eat won't stay either.
    • What you can do (per national guidelines): ① From early summer to late autumn, sunbathe between 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.; ② bare your arms and legs to the sun directly, don't sit behind glass, and don't apply thick sunscreen; ③ 15 to 30 minutes at a time, two to three times a week; ④ In northern winters, the plum-rain season, or for people indoors all day, sun exposure isn't enough and you have to supplement actively — the recommendation is 600 international units a day for people over 65 (600 IU, equal to 15 micrograms) ([official wording: Shenyang Municipal Health Commission 2026; Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025; Chinese Nutrition Society, Dietary Reference Intakes for Chinese Residents (2023 edition) (《中国居民膳食营养素参考摄入量（2023版）》)]).
    • Where this comes from: A ｜ "get sun first, supplement if that's not enough" is written into China's dietary reference intakes; the time of day for sun comes from a domestic clinical expert consensus.
    • Who this is for: most people. People already taking a vitamin D supplement, or with high blood calcium, shouldn't stack supplements on top of each other — ask a doctor; and don't sunbathe until you peel.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I've supplemented for ages and don't know whether it stayed"
    • How this works in China: whether to test the vitamin D in your blood and how much to supplement — go to a hospital for a blood draw and ask a doctor; northern winters are all the more reason to ask.

    2.9 Get a bone-density scan, and read the T-score properly¶

    • What it costs: one clinic visit plus one scan, 100 to several hundred yuan, varying by place (Money: some money ｜ Time: half a day ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: yes, easy)
    • In short: bone density is the most direct test of how solid your bone is, and the internationally accepted diagnostic method is DXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) — not the heel ultrasound at a check-up centre, and not the body-fat scale at home (official wording Healthy China, "Four Groups Advised to Have a Bone-Density Test" 2025).
    • What you can do: ① Who should be tested: anyone over 50 who has had a fracture; women over 65 and men over 70; postmenopausal women up to 65 and men 50 to 70 who have risk factors (such as lower-back pain after activity, being more than 3 cm shorter than in youth, or a stoop); and people taking bone-damaging drugs or with related diseases (official wording Healthy China 2025). ② Where to test: have the endocrinology, orthopaedics, or geriatrics department at a hospital order a DXA, scanning both the lumbar spine and the hip — testing only one site easily misses it. ③ T-score: not below −1.0 is normal; between −1.0 and −2.5 is low bone mass; −2.5 and below is osteoporosis (official wording Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）)).
    • Where this comes from: A ｜ DXA diagnosis of osteoporosis is the international standard, and the T-score cut-offs are written into Chinese guidelines. But don't read the report yourself — a doctor has to judge it together with symptoms and medical history (third-party research Xinhua Net 2025).
    • Who this is for: the four groups above; those already diagnosed and on medication should be re-tested on the schedule the doctor sets — don't stop or adjust it yourself. A normal bone density doesn't mean you won't fracture, and the whole fall-prevention routine (Chapter 5) still applies.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I don't know whether my bones are up to it"
    • How this works in China: first get an order or referral from your contracted family doctor at the community health centre, then have the DXA done at a large hospital; in some cities a community check-up can do an ultrasound bone-density screen first, and those with a problem are referred for DXA (official wording Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025); if you've signed up with a family doctor you can get regular follow-up and re-test reminders. Go by the latest local wording.

    2.10 Smoking, drinking, and sitting still all draw down your bone capital early¶

    • What it costs: nothing — it's about changing habits (Money: nothing ｜ Time: long-term ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: yes, easy)
    • In short: these two accounts — one is being paid into, the other can be drawn down by a few habits. Smoking, heavy drinking, and sitting from morning to night are three ways you draw down early.
    • What you can do: ① Smoking: what's in smoke damages bone cells and also worsens calcium absorption — quitting is the same act for your bones and your lungs. ② Alcohol: drinking too much damages bone and also makes you unsteady on your feet, more likely to fall. ③ Sitting: get up and move a few minutes every hour you sit.
    • Where this comes from: B ｜ "smoking, heavy drinking, and little physical activity" are listed in black and white as controllable risk factors in China's osteoporosis guideline (official wording Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis (2022) (原发性骨质疏松症诊疗指南（2022）)). But there's no precise figure for how much bone mass you can claw back by changing them.
    • Who this is for: it applies to anyone with these habits; when you can't change much, start with "stand up and walk for two minutes every hour you sit" — easier to keep than a grand "quit for good" goal.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I'm topping up and leaking at the same time, and I don't know it"
    • How this works in China: if you want to quit smoking, ask your community family doctor whether there's a cessation clinic or a referral.

    2.11 If these things show up, see a doctor first — don't just train¶

    • What it costs: one clinic visit (Money: the price of one registration ｜ Time: half a day ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: yes, easy)
    • In short: some signals mean the bone or muscle problem has moved past the "just train a bit and it'll be fine" stage — let a doctor sort it out first, then talk about how to move.
    • What you can do — go if you see these: ① Unexplained bone pain: pain at night, or pain whenever you exert yourself, especially lower-back pain. ② Weight loss: a clear, rapid drop in weight. ③ Fracture history: a fracture after age 50. ④ Losing more than 3 cm of height, or starting to stoop. When you go, say clearly where it hurts, for how long, whether you've fallen, and what medication you take, and ask the doctor to assess bone density, vitamin D, and kidney function.
    • Where this comes from: A ｜ these signals are written into China's osteoporosis guideline; treat them as a reason to "go get checked once", not as you diagnosing yourself.
    • Who this is for: anyone with any one of the above. Note: sudden severe bone pain, or being unable to move after a fall, doesn't fall under this — that's an emergency, call 120 (China's ambulance number) per Chapter 5.
    • What you get back: independence + money — no more fearing "a small problem dragging into a big one, and worrying about wasting money"
    • How this works in China: register at a hospital's orthopaedics, endocrinology, or geriatrics department; people over 65 get one free health-management check a year (National Basic Public Health Service), which includes a health-status assessment — ask the community health centre.

    2.12 How this works in China: fold your bone density into the community's annual check-up¶

    • What it costs: no extra cost (Money: nothing ｜ Time: once ｜ Effort: no strain ｜ Need to ask someone: no)
    • In short: the state has already folded these two accounts — bone and muscle — into community and annual check-ups, so you don't have to grope your own way in from a big hospital.
    • What you can do: ① Over 65: a free health-management check once a year — ask "bones, muscles, and whether I might fall" into it too (official wording National Basic Public Health Service Standards (3rd edition) (《国家基本公共卫生服务规范（第三版）》)). ② Some communities or check-up providers can do an ultrasound bone-density screen, forearm DXA, or quantitative CT first, and refer those with a problem to a hospital for DXA (official wording Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025) — note that the standard items of the free check-up don't include bone density, and whether you can add it or be referred varies by place. ③ Contracted family doctor services are a scheme rolled out nationwide; once you sign up you get a fixed family doctor team that can follow up regularly, remind you about re-tests, and help refer you (official wording Guiding Opinions on Advancing Contracted Family Doctor Services (国医改办发〔2016〕1 号), 2016-06-06).
    • Where this comes from: B ｜ free check-ups and contracted family doctor services are basic services rolled out nationwide. But whether a community can do bone density and whether it can refer for DXA varies a lot by place.
    • Who this is for: people in cities and county towns near a community health centre; in rural areas, ask at the township health centre first.
    • What you get back: independence — no more fearing "I want to go get checked but don't know which door to walk through"
    • How this works in China: go to the community residents' committee or the community health centre and ask three questions: how do I book the free check-up, can you do bone density here, and is there a family doctor I can sign up with. Go by the latest local wording.

    2.13 The one thing you can do today¶

    Today, stand up from a chair without using your hands, then sit back down — two to three sets of 8 to 12 repetitions.

    This move is both a test and a training: today it tells you how much capital is left in your legs, and from now on, twice a week, it's money you're paying into that account. From today, remember one number and compare it again on this day next year.

    2.14 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    How to say it out loud. Don't say "you have osteoporosis, your muscles are wasting away" — that's a verdict, and he'll go straight on the defensive. Say something specific that he can't argue with: "Mum, I measured it — you're a bit shorter than the year before last, and you walk more slowly than before. That's what worries me. I want to go with you for a bone-density scan, and a blood draw while we're there. We'll be back the same day." Put "I" into it and leave the judgment to him.

    When he disagrees, what else you can do. Three things, none of which need his nod. First, change the table first: keep milk, eggs, and tofu in the house, a glass of milk and an egg a meal — don't say "calcium", just say "I feel like eating lighter lately". Second, measure without saying anything: while he stands up and walks, count in your head — can he get out of the chair, does he have time to cross the road — note it in your head, don't pass judgment to his face. Third, when you go with him to the clinic, ask the doctor three things on his behalf: whether he needs a bone-density scan, whether he needs a vitamin D and kidney-function test, and whether he can still jump and run. He may not admit it out loud, but his body gives an answer every day.

    2.15 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The specific effect of jumping and impact-type movement on bone density in middle-aged and older adults — I couldn't verify a quantified study in a Chinese population. The direction exists (weight-bearing and impact can stimulate bone), but there's no dose data for a Chinese population on "how much to jump, how long, to make bone harder", so this chapter treats it as "direction established, dose unknown". Next step: check the Expert Consensus on Nutritional and Exercise Management of Primary Osteoporosis and Sarcopenia (2025) (《原发性骨质疏松症与肌少症的营养和运动管理专家共识（2025年）》) and subsequent exercise-intervention studies in Chinese populations.
    • Whether a community can do a bone-density screen, whether it can refer for DXA, and whether the free check-up includes bone density all vary by place. Bone density is not among the standard check-up items of the national basic public health service "older-adult health management"; whether a community can add an ultrasound, forearm DXA, or quantitative CT screen, and whether it can refer for DXA, is up to local arrangements (the Chronic Disease Center of the Chinese CDC, 2025, notes that communities can do ultrasound screening first). Next step: check the free check-up items and referral paths of each province's health commission, province by province.
