    Chapter 17 · Care lands on particular people, and the people giving it should not fall first¶

    Three in the morning. Movement beside you wakes you. Your spouse needs the toilet again. You take their arm and shuffle with them to the bathroom, then shuffle back. Once they are lying down again, you lie with your eyes open until daylight.

    That is roughly how these years have gone. While you look after them, you quietly think: if I go down first, what happens to them?

    This chapter is written for you as you grow old, and also for you as you look after someone. It says two things: when something happens, where your people come from; and the person giving the care needs to keep some breath for themselves too.

    17.1 What this chapter is for¶

    The chapters before this one dealt with the body, money, and medical care. This one deals with people.

    "Someone to look after me" is useless as five words. It has to land on particular people and particular hours: who steadies you, who runs to the hospital, who signs, who pays. Those people and those arrangements all have to be assembled before anything happens.

    The second half of the chapter turns around and is written for the person doing the caring. Care, in the end, always lands on one particular person — most often a spouse, or one particular adult child. That person can be worn out too.

    17.2 Seven kinds of people you can lean on, each with its own limits¶

    There is an old saying: raise sons to guard your old age. On the day disability actually arrives, you find there is more than one kind of person to lean on, and each kind can catch different things.

    According to a 2021 sample survey by the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the National Working Commission on Aging and others, among older people who do have someone caring for them, a spouse is the main carer in 45.5% of cases, children (including daughters-in-law and sons-in-law) in 47.6%, and professional care workers only 3.4%. Those two or three people in the household carry more than ninety percent of the care.

    Who you can lean on What they can catch What they cannot catch
    A partner Closest at hand, there all day, knows your habits best They are getting old too; the two of you going down together is the most dangerous case
    Children Hospital runs, signing, paying, paperwork, making the decisions Mostly not nearby, and they have jobs; the year-after-year work of turning and washing is more than they can carry
    Relatives and close friends Come in for a day or two at the critical moment, lend money, show up and lend weight Each has their own household; they can only help for a stretch
    Neighbors Can knock on the door within minutes, notice that something is wrong, run a small errand If the relationship is not that close it is awkward to ask; weaker still in new developments
    Volunteers Visits, company and conversation, small errands, volunteer hospital accompaniment Inconsistent, limited slots, no substitute for professional care
    The community (residents' committee, eldercare service station, family doctor, eldercare adviser) The entry point to policy: help applying for subsidies, signing up, being introduced to services Not a carer on call; they cannot do the daily feeding and turning
    Professional carers (hospital attendants, care workers, nurses, LTCI services) Know the professional handling, can take the physical load, can cover a night shift Costs money, high turnover, the good ones are hard to book

    The people who can spend every day at the bedside are either a spouse who no longer has the strength, or children who cannot take that much leave. The gap in between is what this chapter is trying to fill.

    17.3 Do not wait for the day it happens to scroll through your contacts¶

    • What it costs: one afternoon and a pen (Money: none | Time: half a day | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: easy to ask)
    • In short: Scrolling your phone for a number on the day something happens is the worst possible delay. Write down now, on one sheet of paper, the people who can come.
    • What you can do: Write it in three columns — can be there at once, can put in money, can handle paperwork and make decisions — with at least one name and phone number in each. Then agree one "signal" with the key person: curtains open, light on, all is well. This sheet is folded into the family index table in Chapter 19.
    • Where this comes from: B | Social support networks are associated with health and survival in older people, with cohort studies behind that. But there is no randomized trial showing that "a written list means fewer crises"; what the list protects is not being in a scramble on the day.
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60 and still sturdy, this list is something you are storing up for your future self; at 75 with a few chronic conditions, the list needs to be checked once a week; already disabled, the list has to be maintained by the children, and cannot wait for the older person to think of it.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid of "something happening at night and no one knowing"
    • How this works in China: Ask at the residents' committee or the community eldercare service station whether your area has visits to people living alone, "knock on the door" rounds, contracted family doctors, and registration of emergency contacts. The latest local wording governs.

    17.4 Hospital accompaniment: paying someone to run the hospital for you¶

    • What it costs: 100 to 300 yuan a time (Money: some money | Time: half a day to a full day | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, and it is a little embarrassing — you have to be willing to admit you cannot work it out yourself)
    • In short: The bigger the hospital, the more it is a maze — registering, paying, collecting medicine, finding the department, walking back and forth. What a hospital companion does is run the errands and do the talking for an older person.
    • What you can do: (1) Is it worth it: most worth it if you live alone, have trouble walking, cannot follow the process, or your children cannot get leave. (2) What counts as reasonable (Shanghai's published figures, updated 2026-10): in Pudong, "Pu Laohui" sets half a day (4 hours) at 198 to 280 yuan; across the city the market rate is mostly 50 to 80 yuan an hour, usually with a 2-hour minimum, an ordinary accompaniment is 100 to 200 yuan, and packages are mostly 200 to 300 yuan for about 4 hours. Each agency sets its own fees, but has to post them clearly at the place of service. (3) How to choose: use a proper agency, check the companion's ID badge, sign a contract, ask clearly whether pickup and help with wheelchairs and stairs are included, and ask for an invoice. (4) Do not count on them to add an appointment slot or grab a number for you: using accompaniment as cover to add slots and sell appointment numbers is explicitly banned. (5) Hand over the medical record and ID card only for that visit's use; do not leave the whole file with them.
    • Where this comes from: A | Shanghai piloted it from January 2025 in nine districts including Pudong, and extended it citywide on 1 July 2026, for people aged 60 and over regardless of household registration; agencies must have at least 5 companions, and companions' ID badges can be checked. But there is still no unified national occupational qualification for hospital companions; the "companion certificate" is mostly a local or industry assessment and is not necessarily recognized in another city.
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60 and in reasonable health you will mostly not need it; at 75 with chronic conditions or already disabled it is most useful, especially for follow-up visits, collecting medicine, and tests. Two things to guard against: privacy leaks (medical records, ID) and disputes (sign the contract first; if something goes wrong, go to the agency and to the consumer protection committee).
    • What you get back: independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "going to the doctor with no one with me, unable to explain myself, and treated as a nuisance"
    • How this works in China: In mainland China, in Shanghai you can order through the district platform (such as "Pu Laohui" in Pudong), or ask at a comprehensive eldercare service center or the service station at the end of your street; older people on the minimum living guarantee or low incomes can be brought into the eldercare service subsidy. In other cities, start by asking the district civil affairs bureau whether there is a "medical assistance and hospital accompaniment service." At national level, 民发〔2024〕52 号 has already written medical assistance and hospital accompaniment into home-based eldercare services. The latest local wording governs.

    17.5 Respite care: a day off for the person who watches every day¶

    • What it costs: depends on the place; sometimes the government pays, sometimes you pay part (Money: some money | Time: a few days a month | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, and it is a little embarrassing — you have to be willing to admit you cannot hold out)
    • In short: One person watching another sick person every day, 365 days a year with no break — the one who breaks first is the watcher. Respite care means either moving the older person into a facility for a few days, or having a professional come to your home and take over for half a day.
    • What you can do: (1) First ask whether your area has it; (2) it comes in two shapes — short-term residential care (the older person goes into a facility for a few days) and home care (a professional comes to your home and covers a few hours to a day); (3) do not buy a month at the start; try one day a month first; (4) keep that day for yourself: your own appointments, errands, or sleeping for a whole day.
    • Where this comes from: A (mechanism) | At national level there is only the word "explore": The 14th Five-Year Plan for National Aging Programs and the Elderly Care Service System (《"十四五"国家老龄事业发展和养老服务体系规划》, 国发〔2021〕35 号) says to "explore respite services for family carers of disabled older people." As of 2026 there is still no unified national respite care system; it exists as separate pilots in places such as Fengtai in Beijing, Qingyang in Chengdu, and Guanghan in Sichuan: where the money comes from (sometimes the government pays, sometimes you pay part), who is covered (mostly only severe disability), and how to apply all differ by place and by year.
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60 and in decent health you will not need it yet, but it is worth knowing such a thing exists; at 75 with someone in the household caring every day, it is worth asking about; if the older person is already disabled, this is the thing most worth asking about, because the family carer is often the one who goes down first.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid of "the carer collapsing first and the whole family going down with them"
    • How this works in China: In mainland China, ask at the sub-district or community eldercare service center, or the district civil affairs bureau: "respite services for family carers, short-term residential care, family eldercare beds — does this area have them, and how do I apply?" Fengtai in Beijing, Qingyang in Chengdu, and Guanghan in Sichuan have all run pilots. The latest local wording governs.

    17.6 If you are the carer, count yourself in first¶

    • What it costs: one self-check and one phone call (Money: none | Time: a few minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, and it is a little embarrassing — you have to admit you might not hold out either)
    • In short: Caregiving is not a cold; it is a chronic drain that hollows a person out bit by bit. One meta-analysis pooled 13 studies and 4,970 people and put the detection rate of depressive symptoms among family carers of disabled older people in China at 41.81% (95% confidence interval about 37.6%–46.1%). Look closely at the words "detection rate": this is a proportion pooled from several studies, not a prevalence rate, and it certainly does not mean "one in every two carers has depression." What it says is that emotional problems are very common in this group, that they have never been treated as an illness, and that they should not be written off as "not being able to let things go."
    • What you can do: Check yourself against these; the more of them fit, the more seriously to take it — (1) you sleep badly and wake still tired; (2) things you used to enjoy, you do not want to do any of them; (3) you flare up for no reason, shout at the older person, and then feel guilty; (4) you cannot eat, or you eat enormously; (5) you keep saying "I can't hold out," "better if I went first"; (6) headaches, stomach pain, a racing heart, and nothing shows up on examination. If several of these fit, make one appointment at a psychiatry or mental health clinic, or make one phone call.
    • Where this comes from: B | The high detection rate of depression among carers has meta-analytic support pooling several studies; but the scales, samples, and regions differ between studies, and the pooled interval itself is wide (95% CI about 37.6%–46.1%, with subgroups from about 33% to nearly 50%), so treat 41.81% as a reminder, not a diagnosis, and certainly not as a "national prevalence."
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60 and in decent health, you can run into this the moment you start caring for your parents; at 75, ill yourself and also caring for someone, it is heaviest; if the older person is already disabled, the carer is often an even older spouse, and the risk is highest. If you hear "there's no point in living" or "I'd be better off dead," take it seriously and do only two things: stay with the person, and call for help. National psychological assistance hotline 12356 (set up by the National Health Commission in 2024, with each line required to provide at least 18 hours of service a day); the Hope 24 hotline 400-161-9995 (24/7). If someone is in immediate danger, call 120 or 110.
    • What you get back: relationships + independence — no longer afraid of "the person doing the caring collapsing first"
    • How this works in China: Call 12356; ask at the community whether there is an older-adult mental health care point or a community mental health doctor (more places to turn are in 17.10). The latest local wording governs.

    There is no version of caregiving that is not tiring.

    17.7 The four hurdles caregiving has to get over: body, money, work, feelings¶

    Whether you are caring for a parent or a spouse, the wearing-down comes in just four forms.

    Hurdle What it looks like One thing you can do first
    Body Turning, lifting, bathing, night trips to the toilet — by the end of the day your back goes first Stop carrying it alone and set a rota: schedule shifts at home, or use short-term residential care or home care to cover a few days
    Money Attendant fees, care supplies, the self-pay part of a hospital stay — one payment after another First check whether local long-term care insurance covers it and whether there is a disability subsidy; see Chapter 4
    Work A job plus caregiving; take too much leave and you fear losing the job Check whether your province has one-child carer leave — there is no national rule, each province sets its own days (from 5 to 20, and in some provinces non-only children can take it too), your province's population and family planning regulations govern; take it if you can
    Feelings Irritability, guilt, grievance; you lose your temper at the patient and then regret it Find one person you can talk to and talk to them regularly; if you cannot hold out, call a hotline and go to a psychiatry or mental health clinic

    The hardest one to say out loud is feelings: you can say you are tired, but being fed up is often something you do not dare say.

    17.8 "It is time for a facility" is about capacity, not filial devotion¶

    • What it costs: one assessment or one conversation (Money: assessments usually go through LTCI, or are free | Time: half a day | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: hard to say — this is the hardest step to take)
    • In short: Whether the older person should move to a facility is not settled by "am I a good son or daughter," but by a few very concrete questions: can they go on safely at home, can the carer still hold out, is there enough money.
    • What you can do: The more of these fit, the more "a facility" or "paying a professional to come in" belongs among the options — (1) safety: repeated falls, wandering off, an emergency at night, forgetting to turn off the stove or the gas; (2) the care tasks: turning, lifting, feeding, changing a urinary catheter, managing pressure sores — can the household do them; (3) the carer's state: the carer's body or mind has already given way; (4) money and skill: round-the-clock care is unaffordable, or the nursing the older person needs simply cannot be done at home. If you want an objective reference point, you can have an assessment under the national standard Specification for Older Adults' Capacity Assessment (《老年人能力评估规范》, GB/T 42195-2022), which grades capacity in five levels, from "capacity intact" to "completely disabled."
    • Where this comes from: B | Capacity assessment for older adults has a national standard, so who assesses and how they assess has a basis. But no cut-off line makes the decision for you; "when is the time" has no single answer, and in the end you and the older person decide together.
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60 and in decent health, what you are thinking about now is "whether, later on"; at 75 with several chronic conditions, have the assessment done once and know where you stand; if the older person is already disabled, the question becomes "can this household still hold up where it is."
    • What you get back: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid of "am I unfilial," "the old person being sent away"
    • How this works in China: For the disability assessment, LTCI benefits, and whether a facility offers a trial stay, ask at the community or sub-district eldercare service center, the district civil affairs bureau, or the medical insurance bureau where the person is insured. If you are unsure, start by having the older person stay in a facility for a few days on trial. The latest local wording governs.

    17.9 Between siblings, count money and time separately¶

    • What it costs: one family meeting (Money: none | Time: an hour or two, possibly several of them | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: hard to say — it means talking about money, and possibly opening old scores)
    • In short: The falling-out is usually not about who gives less, but about no one saying clearly who gave how much. Count the time given and the money given separately, write them on one sheet, and there is less to argue about.
    • What you can do: (1) Split it into two things: the people who give time and the people who give money; (2) set a conversion — whoever cannot give time gives money in proportion, or uses that money to buy services (hospital accompaniment, an attendant, respite) to cover that share of the work; (3) clock the work of "giving time": half a day for one hospital accompaniment, several days for one hospital stay, and record it on the sheet; (4) if you cannot agree, bring in someone to chair it: an elder, the residents' committee, a social worker. How to hold the meeting is in Chapter 21.
    • Where this comes from: C | Common practice and collected experience; there is no research on "how to divide it so that nobody argues." But no split satisfies everyone: the one who lives nearby gives more time, the one who earns more gives more money, and it is often called unfair; families in different cities, only children, and step-families get more tangled still.
    • Who this is for: Who it holds for — at 60, while your parents are still sturdy, set the rules first; at 75, with parents carrying chronic conditions, while their minds are still clear, lay the accounts out; if the older person is already disabled, do not wait for a collapse to talk.
    • What you get back: relationships — no longer afraid of "the family turning on each other over money and care"
    • How this works in China: The community mediation committee, the residents' committee, and social workers can all help get the family into one room to talk; if it really cannot be settled, go the legal route. The latest local wording governs.

    17.10 For the carer: a few places you can call¶

    When you are tired, fed up, or cannot hold out, these are places you can turn.

    • Respite care: have a professional cover for you for a few days (see 17.5).
    • The 12356 national psychological assistance hotline: set up by the National Health Commission in 2024; each line is required to provide at least 18 hours of service a day, and in many places it runs 24 hours. There is also the Hope 24 hotline, 400-161-9995 (24/7).
    • The community mental health doctor: if there is someone at home with a severe mental disorder (schizophrenia, for example), the community health center or township health center has a dedicated mental health doctor responsible for follow-up; it is part of the National Basic Public Health Service, with at least 4 follow-ups a year.
    • Older-adult mental health care points: under the action to care for older adults' mental health that the National Health Commission promoted from 2022 to 2025, in principle every county (city, district) has at least one such point in a community or village. Ask at your community whether there is one.
    • Eldercare advisers: the "eldercare adviser" role the state is promoting helps older people work out what to do among the pile of policies and institutions, and handles errands on their behalf (民发〔2024〕52 号).
    • A contracted family doctor: once signed up, an older person with limited mobility or disability can request home treatment, follow-up, nursing, and a home sickbed, and chronic conditions can be prescribed 4 to 12 weeks at a time.
    • Long-term care insurance: mainland China's LTCI scheme is the money for care after disability — whether it reimburses and how much, ask the medical insurance bureau where the person is insured (see Chapter 4). It is not the same product as the long-term care insurance policies sold in the US or UK; this book describes the mainland system only.

    How far these have been built out differs from place to place; asking once is more accurate than guessing at home. The latest local wording governs.

    17.11 If you can do only one thing today¶

    Take one sheet of paper and write down three names and phone numbers: one who can be there at once if something happens, one who can put in money, one who can handle paperwork and make decisions. When you have written them, stick the sheet on the fridge.

    If the second and third columns on that sheet are both empty, spend today on one phone call instead — to the person you have not been in touch with the longest, and who is most likely to help you.

    17.12 If you are reading this for your parents¶

    This chapter means two things for you: one is finding people for your parents; the other is a reminder that you are also one of the people who has to be counted in.

    Assemble the people for your parents. Take the table in 17.2 and fill it in for your own family, see which kind is missing, and then go and look. Do not underrate neighbors: first help them get to know the people across the corridor and upstairs and downstairs, and leave each other phone numbers — when you are not there, the neighbor is the person closest to them. Ask at the community once whether your area has visits to people living alone, contracted family doctors, meal services, and respite care.

    Do not use yourself up. Many people run everywhere for their parents, cast themselves as the substitute player, come whenever called, and do not dare get sick or get fed up. Once disability is long-term, one person cannot carry it for a year. While there is still time, do three things: settle how the siblings divide money and time (17.9); ask clearly about respite care and short-term residential care (17.5); and keep for yourself one person you can talk to and one hotline (17.10).

    And one more layer. The way you care for your parents today is, most likely, the way your children will care for you later. Do you want your children to quit a job, or break down, for your sake? If not, then now — for yourself and for them — set the ground rules for the road that is "paying someone to help."

    17.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The qualifications and fees for hospital accompaniment: there is no unified national occupational qualification, the "companion certificate" is mostly a local or industry assessment, and whether another city recognizes it I have not checked city by city. Fees are set by each agency; the Shanghai figures in this chapter (198–280 yuan for half a day in Pudong, 200–300 yuan for citywide packages, and so on) represent only Shanghai's published figures in 2026, and other cities have to be asked separately.
    • Respite care, long-term care insurance, and one-child carer leave are almost one pattern per province, sometimes per city: at national level respite care is only at the "explore" stage (see 17.5), LTCI's disability assessment and benefits are still being rolled out, and the number of days of carer leave depends on your province's regulations — this chapter stops at "where to ask," and the specific terms have to come from your own locality.
    • Carer depression at 41.81%: this is a detection rate pooled from 13 studies; the scales differ between studies and the pooled interval is wide (about 34%–49%), so it can only serve as a reminder, not as the prevalence in your area or in any one person.
