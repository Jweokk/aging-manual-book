    Chapter 10 · Urine, stool, and dignity¶

    On the day you were going out, you counted it up in your head first: from home to the hospital, how many toilets along the way, and which ones you could get into.

    When you had finished counting, you put the long trip off to next month. This chapter is written for that "putting off".

    10.1 What this chapter is for¶

    Two things — urine and stool — most people put up with in silence. Holding them in is where leaking, constipation, infection, and falls at night begin.

    There is only one thing to say: most problems of this kind can be improved. Putting up with them is the only real defeat.

    10.2 Leaking urine comes in four kinds, and each has a matching treatment¶

    Leaking urine is not one disease; it is several problems sharing one name. Work out which kind it is first, and the treatment will match (official wording Department of Science Popularization, Chinese Medical Association, 2024).

    • Stress incontinence. It leaks when you cough, sneeze, laugh, run or jump, or lift something, and not at other times. The base of it is slack pelvic floor muscles and not enough support for the urethra; in women it is often related to childbirth, age, body weight, and pelvic organ prolapse (official wording health education prescription from Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology).
    • Urge incontinence. A sudden urgent need, and you can't hold it; often you leak before you reach the toilet. The bladder contracts before it is full — it is oversensitive (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • Overflow incontinence. The bladder never empties properly, and urine seeps out a little at a time. Common causes are a blockage at the outlet (in men, usually an enlarged prostate), or a problem with the nerves or muscles of the bladder (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • Mixed incontinence. Both of the above together. Which one predominates decides which end you treat first (official wording Department of Science Popularization, Chinese Medical Association, 2024).

    There is another kind, called functional: there is nothing much wrong with the pelvic floor or the bladder; you simply don't get to the toilet in time, and it has to do with mobility and memory, so what you change is the route and the position (official wording Mayo Clinic). Among women patients, about 60% have stress incontinence, 10% urge, and 30% mixed (official wording Department of Science Popularization, Chinese Medical Association, 2024).

    10.3 The pelvic floor is a muscle you can train, and both men and women have one¶

    • What it costs: zero to a few hundred yuan (money: some | time: a few minutes a day | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy — no one can tell)
    • In short: The pelvic floor is like a hammock holding up the bladder and the bowel. When it is slack, you leak as soon as you exert yourself; when it is strong, the gate stays shut. Like your arms and legs, it can be trained.
    • What you can do: ① Find it first. Pretend you are holding back urine and wind; the part that tightens is it. Don't actually practice by tightening and releasing while you are urinating — that leaves urine behind and invites infection (official wording Mayo Clinic; MSKCC in Chinese). ② Tighten and hold for five seconds, then relax for five seconds; once that is easy, work up to ten seconds. ③ Ten to a set, at least three sets a day — one lying down, one sitting, one standing. ④ Before you cough, sneeze, stand up or lift something, tighten first. ⑤ It usually takes weeks to months to show; once it works you have to keep at it, and it slips back if you stop (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • Where this comes from: A | This is the first-line approach in Chinese guidelines and international practice alike: for stress incontinence, training it is the first choice, and it helps urge incontinence too (official wording Union Hospital health education prescription; Mayo Clinic).
    • Who this is for: People who can follow the movements. If it does nothing, or you can't find the muscle, it is usually that the method is wrong or you are training the wrong muscle — don't force it; have a pelvic floor rehabilitation clinic or a rehabilitation therapist take a look.
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid that "one cough and I leak, and I can't trust myself to go out."
    • How this works in China: Many hospitals have opened a pelvic floor rehabilitation clinic or a pelvic floor dysfunction clinic, and you can register directly (official wording Beijing Ditan Hospital; Hangzhou First People's Hospital). Biofeedback and electrical stimulation are adjuncts; whether to use them is the doctor's call. Whether medical insurance (yibao — the mainland's public scheme, the counterpart of Medicare or the NHS) covers it, and how much it pays, differs from province to province: at the national level, rehabilitation items have been brought into medical insurance in two batches (9 items in 2010, 20 more in 2016), and "pelvic floor rehabilitation" is mostly added by the provinces themselves; ask the hospital's insurance office or the local medical insurance bureau before your appointment (see 10.14).

    10.4 The more afraid of leaking you are, the less you drink — the most common thing to do, and the most damaging¶

    • What it costs: one day of changing a habit (money: nothing | time: one day | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy)
    • In short: Afraid of leaking, you drink less, so the urine gets concentrated; concentrated urine stings the bladder, the bladder grows more sensitive, you want to go more and more urgently, and so you get more afraid and drink even less. The circle tightens with every turn.
    • What you can do: Break the circle.
    • How much to drink: for most adults, roughly 1.5 to 2.0 liters of water a day (official wording Chinese expert consensus on chronic constipation, 2019). Spread it out, in small sips; don't down one big glass at a time.
    • When to drink: mostly in the daytime — don't cut out the day as well just to save yourself a trip to the toilet.
    • How to arrange it before bed: in the two to three hours before sleep, cut back on large amounts of fluid; go easy on coffee, strong tea, alcohol, fizzy drinks and very spicy food, which drive urination and irritate the bladder (official wording Tri-Service General Hospital; Mayo Clinic).
    • Train the bladder: when the urgency comes, don't panic — sit down firmly, breathe deeply, tighten the pelvic floor, and wait for the wave to pass before you walk; in ordinary times, try putting urination off a little at a time, starting with ten minutes (official wording Tri-Service General Hospital; Mayo Clinic).
    • Where this comes from: B | Adjusting what and how much you drink, and bladder training, are the basic measures every guideline recommends alike; but how much water is right varies from person to person, and anyone with heart failure or kidney disease must follow their own doctor on how much to drink.
    • Who this is for: People whose heart, lungs and kidneys work normally. If you have heart failure, kidney disease, or are on dialysis, ask your doctor how much to drink.
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid that "I daren't go out, in case I can't find a toilet on the way."
    • How this works in China: Write down "what I drank and when I went" for three days and you have a voiding diary; take it to the clinic — it is more use than describing it from memory (official wording Department of Science Popularization, Chinese Medical Association, 2024).

    10.5 Getting up to urinate three or more times a night — don't write it off as "that's what getting old is like"¶

    • What it costs: one clinic visit (money: registration plus tests | time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy)
    • In short: Waking at night because you need to urinate, more than twice a night, already counts as nocturia (official wording Mayo Clinic). It is not an aging phenomenon you simply have to put up with; behind it there is often a cause that can be found and treated.
    • What you can do: Who to see, and what to ask.
    • Who to see: urology (men or women), or geriatrics; women can also go to a clinic in the urogynecology line.
    • What to ask the doctor to check: a urine test (for infection, and for blood); how much urine is left in the bladder after you have been; the three-day voiding diary you kept; blood sugar. If you snore loudly, are sleepy in the daytime, have swollen legs and feet, or pass a great deal of urine at night, tell the doctor that too (official wording Tri-Service General Hospital; Mayo Clinic).
    • Bring your medicines: many common medicines affect urination; write the list on a sheet of paper and have the doctor go through it.
    • Where this comes from: B | The list of causes of nocturia is the same everywhere, but what to check first, and in what order, is for the doctor to decide on your particulars.
    • Who this is for: People who sleep badly at night and have no energy in the day, and people who have already had a fall. Getting up half asleep at night is the time you are most likely to fall (see Chapter 5). If there is blood in your urine, it hurts to urinate, or you have a fever, don't wait — go as soon as you can.
    • What you get back: Independence + relationships — no longer afraid that "something will happen to me alone at home at night."
    • How this works in China: Register with urology or geriatrics; if you have a contracted family doctor, ask at your community health center first, then decide which tier of hospital to go to.

    10.6 Constipation: say nothing, and you can hold it in until you are blocked¶

    • What it costs: changing habits costs nothing; a visit is one clinic appointment (money: some | time: long term | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy)
    • In short: Straining to pass stool is a dangerous move for anyone with heart disease or cerebrovascular disease; let it drag on and you can end up with fecal impaction or a bowel obstruction.
    • What you can do:
    • First see whether there is a cause you can change: eating refined food, too little fiber, too little water, too little movement all make it worse; many medicines cause constipation too, among them some antidepressants, anticholinergics, calcium channel blockers, and opioid painkillers (official wording Guidelines for the Primary Care Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Constipation, 2019).
    • Eating and drinking: eat more things with fiber — roughly 25 to 35 grams of fiber a day; 1.5 to 2.0 liters of water a day (official wording Guidelines for the Primary Care Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Constipation, 2019). Fiber needs enough water to go with it, or you end up more bloated than before.
    • The habit of going: the colon is most active when you wake in the morning and after a meal, so choose the morning or within two hours of a meal to sit down, and it comes more easily; concentrate while you are there, and don't stay more than ten minutes at a time (official wording Guidelines for the Primary Care Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Constipation, 2019). A squatting position empties more completely than a sitting one.
    • Where this comes from: A | Diet, fluid, exercise and the habit of going are the basic treatment every guideline agrees on, and they come before medicine (official wording Chinese expert consensus on chronic constipation, 2019).
    • Who this is for: Most people with functional constipation. If the signals below appear, this is no longer a matter of changing habits — get seen soon.
    • What you get back: Independence — no longer afraid that "straining will bring something on in the toilet."
    • How this works in China: If four to eight weeks of changed habits doesn't help, you can ask the doctor to consider medicine; which type, and for how long, is the doctor's decision — don't lean on stimulant laxatives yourself long term (official wording Guidelines for the Primary Care Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Constipation, 2019).

    10.7 If constipation comes with these signals, don't put it off¶

    • What it costs: one clinic visit, possibly a stool test or a colonoscopy (money: a fair amount | time: half a day | effort: some | do you have to speak up: easy)
    • In short: The great majority of constipation is functional, and changed habits plus medicine will ease it. But in a small share there is another disease behind it, and finding it early makes a large difference.
    • What you can do: If any one signal appears, go to gastroenterology or a general practitioner; don't add laxatives on your own.
    • blood in the stool, or a stool test positive for occult blood;
    • weight loss without trying, or anemia, or marked fatigue;
    • abdominal pain that keeps getting worse, or a lump you can feel in your abdomen;
    • can't pass anything, bloated and in pain, and vomiting (think bowel obstruction).
    • Where this comes from: A | These alarm signals, and "for a first visit over forty, assessment is recommended, with colonoscopy if needed", are the referral wording written into the Chinese guideline (official wording Guidelines for the Primary Care Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Constipation, 2019).
    • Who this is for: Anyone with any one of the above. If none of these signals is present and the history is long, you can be treated empirically first — not everyone needs a colonoscopy.
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid that "I put up with everything in silence and let the time when it could be treated go by."
    • How this works in China: Register with gastroenterology; the doctor decides from the alarm signals whether to test the stool, take an image, or do a colonoscopy.

    10.8 Change four things in the bathroom, and there is a rule for grab-bar height¶

    • What it costs: grab bars and a commode chair run from a few dozen to a few hundred yuan, and fitting them needs a tradesman (money: some | time: a few days | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy)
    • In short: Leaking, nocturia, constipation — in the end they all come down to "can you use the toilet safely, and with dignity". Chapter 5 covered the bathroom as the corner of the home where most falls happen; here are the few things that bear directly on elimination.
    • What you can do: ① Grab bars: on both sides of the toilet, fit a horizontal bar about 70 cm above the floor, and on the other side a vertical bar about 1.4 m high; bars 3 to 4 cm in diameter, with the inner side no less than 4 cm from the wall (official wording the national standard GB 50763-2012, Code for Accessibility Design). Those dimensions are the standard for public buildings (accessible toilets); at home, the closer fit is the Ministry of Civil Affairs industry standard MZ/T 218—2024, General Requirements for Age-Friendly Renovation of the Home Environment for Older Adults — grab bars should be provided beside the toilet and in the shower area, with the height set by the person's height and gait, and the gap between bar and wall no less than 40 mm. ② Raise the toilet, or add a commode chair: for someone who can't squat down, or can't get up, a commode chair is the answer. ③ Light the whole way when you get up at night: fit a run of motion-sensor night lights from the bed to the toilet, not just one; take up the mats along the route. ④ For functional incontinence, put a lidded commode by the bed, so that when there is no time to walk you can go right there (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • Where this comes from: B | The grab-bar dimensions are the national standard wording, but that is a standard for public buildings; at home it has to be set to your height and your body — before it is fitted, have the workman hold the actual bar against you and check the height.
    • Who this is for: People who can still get to the toilet themselves, or half by themselves. Bars have to be anchored into the wall, so use a qualified contractor. Someone completely bedbound needs a different kind of care.
    • What you get back: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "falling on the way to the toilet in the middle of the night."
    • How this works in China: Some items in an age-friendly home renovation are subsidized by the government; go to the residents' committee or the sub-district eldercare service window and ask, "does a case like mine get the subsidy" (Minfa [2020] No. 86; see Chapter 5 for details).

    10.9 Adult diapers and underpads: not a surrender, a purchase of dignity¶

    • What it costs: depending on how much you use, a few dozen to a few hundred yuan a month (money: a fair amount | time: any time | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy — used right, no one knows)
    • In short: Wearing one is so that you still dare to go out; it has nothing to do with winning or losing. It puts a lid on the "daren't sit, daren't go far, always worried my trousers are wet" for long enough that the days go on as usual.
    • What you can do:
    • How to choose: by how much leaks. If it is an occasional drip and you can still get to the toilet, use a thin underpad; if there is more, and it is at night, a thicker diaper.
    • How to change: change on a schedule; don't wait until it is soaked through. Sitting in wet makes the skin break down quickly (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • How to protect the skin: change promptly when wet; warm water and a towel, wiping gently rather than scrubbing; let it dry before dressing; put on a layer of petroleum jelly or a similar barrier cream, to keep the skin and the urine apart; don't wash and irrigate repeatedly (official wording Mayo Clinic).
    • Where this comes from: C | This is standard nursing practice; there is no large trial behind the practice itself; but the moment the skin goes red or breaks, that is the moment to deal with it.
    • Who this is for: Everyone who needs one, and especially people who are bedbound, have dementia, or have thin skin. If the skin is already broken or infected, get a nurse or a doctor to deal with it.
    • What you get back: Dignity — no longer afraid that "I smell, and people keep away from me."
    • How this works in China: Nurses at a hospital nursing clinic or a community health center can teach you how to change and how to protect the skin. Long-term care insurance here is a social insurance scheme run by the state, not the private long-term care policies sold in some countries, and it mainly pays for "nursing services": the nationwide Catalogue of Long-Term Care Insurance Service Items (Trial) (《国家长期护理保险服务项目目录（试行）》) (Yibaofa [2025] No. 24) contains only 20 daily living care items and 16 medical nursing items, 36 in all; adult diapers and underpads, classed as "supportive assistive devices", are not in the national catalogue for now (the document says their inclusion will be "studied and explored" later). A few cities have a separate allowance for devices; whether you can claim, and how much, ask the local medical insurance bureau (official wording National Healthcare Security Administration; benefits differ from place to place, and the latest local wording governs).

    10.10 Sex and intimacy: the thing that is assumed to have disappeared¶

    • What it costs: one conversation, and possibly one clinic visit (money: the price of one registration | time: once | effort: none | do you have to speak up: hard — this is the one that takes the most courage)
    • In short: Many people assume that by this age there is no such thing any more, and so nobody mentions it. But people over fifty still think about it and still do it; the need does not vanish just because you are older (official wording Chinese Journal of Epidemiology, 2023).
    • What you can do:
    • How to start: you don't need to go round the houses. One sentence that works: "There is something I have been too embarrassed to bring up with you."
    • First work out what is in the way: it may be illness (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, a gynecological or prostate problem); it may be medicine (some blood-pressure drugs, antidepressants, and prostate drugs affect sexual function); it may be an operation you have had (prostate, gynecological); or it may simply be that mood and the relationship have changed (third-party research the UpToDate topic Sexual Dysfunction in Older Adults; a PMC review, 2024). A good many of these can be dealt with, but you have to say it first, and get it looked into first.
    • Who to talk to: geriatrics, urology or andrology, gynecology; for distress of mood and relationship, psychiatry or psychology. For problems such as dryness after menopause, gynecology has treatments that answer to it.
    • Where this comes from: C | "There is still sexual desire late in life, and the factors that shape it can be looked into and talked about" is a judgment with research behind it (third-party research, see sources); but how it can be handled for any one person has no single answer — you have to go through it point by point with a doctor.
    • Who this is for: People who want to talk about it, and are willing to see a doctor for it. Not wanting to talk about it is entirely all right too; not sorting this out does not stop you doing the other things well.
    • What you get back: Relationships + dignity — no longer afraid that "it is assumed I don't need it, and I am too embarrassed to say."
    • How this works in China: Register with geriatrics, urology, andrology or gynecology and say straight out, "I have a problem of this kind I would like you to look at"; if you can't get the words out, write it on a piece of paper and hand it over.

    10.11 The one thing you can do today¶

    Today, do one set of pelvic floor exercises.

    First find the muscle: pretend to hold back urine, hold back wind — the part that tightens is it. Then tighten for five seconds, relax for five seconds, ten times in a row.

    It costs nothing, no one can see, and you can start today. If you can't find the muscle, that is precisely the sign that this is worth one visit to a doctor.

    10.12 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    This whole chapter, there is almost nothing you can do by stepping in, because it is a matter of the body and a matter of face. There are two things you can do.

    How to get the words out to him. Don't ask "do you leak?" — that is asking him to admit to something humiliating. Say it about yourself instead: "I've been getting up at night a lot lately, so I went and had it checked; the doctor says lots of people have this, and it can be treated." Lower yourself first, and he may pick up the thread. Or leave this chapter open on the table; that does more than ten sentences from you.

    What you can still do when he won't agree. Three things that don't need his nod: ① start changing the bathroom — grab bars, night lights, take the mats up — and call it "steadier for me to use too"; ② buy a pack of underpads and put them in his room, saying they are "there just in case", and don't push him to use them; ③ when you go with him to an appointment, put the questions to the doctor for him — how many times he gets up at night, how his drinking is, what medicines he takes. He may not admit it out loud; his body will.

    10.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • Pelvic floor rehabilitation items (biofeedback, electrical stimulation and the like): the range covered by medical insurance in each place, and the reimbursement rules, differ from province to province and city to city. At the national level only the framework for bringing rehabilitation items in by batch has been set; the specific additions are for each province to decide. This chapter has not verified them one by one — ask the hospital's insurance office or the local medical insurance bureau before the appointment.
    • Whether adult diapers and underpads are covered by long-term care insurance or by a local allowance differs from place to place. The nationwide Catalogue of Long-Term Care Insurance Service Items (Trial) does not yet include "supportive assistive devices" of this kind; a few cities have a separate allowance for devices, and you need to ask the local medical insurance bureau.
    • The grab-bar dimensions in GB 50763-2012, Code for Accessibility Design, are the standard for public buildings (accessible toilets); this chapter has added the civil affairs industry standard MZ/T 218—2024, which fits the home more closely, but the final height of a grab bar at home still has to be set to the person's height and body, and matched to the wording of the home age-friendly renovation program (Minfa [2020] No. 86).
    • The list of factors in the "sex and intimacy" section rests mainly on the outline of the UpToDate topic and one review; I have not checked each item back to the original study.
    • "About 60% stress / 10% urge / 30% mixed" for incontinence is the wording of the Chinese Medical Association's popular-science article (written by Lou Wenjia of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, reviewed by Zhu Lan); the original study based on a national survey gives different proportions among middle-aged and older women (see 10.14). Different populations and different years: the numbers cannot be carried straight across.
