    Chapter 6 · More medicine is not better¶

    Medicines are meant to keep bad things from happening. Past a certain number, they start to become the bad thing themselves.

    A row of pill boxes on the table — morning ones, midday ones, evening ones, some before meals and some after. Have you ever counted how many there really are?

    6.1 What this chapter is for¶

    Your medicines may come from several places: prescribed by cardiology, by endocrinology, by the community hospital — and picked up by you or your children from pharmacies, livestreams, relatives. Each place looks only at its own patch. No one looks at all of it together.

    This chapter is about gathering the medicines scattered across several departments and several sources onto one sheet of paper, and having one person who can see the whole picture go through them.

    6.2 By the fifth drug taken at once, risk starts to climb¶

    • What it costs: the time of one clinic visit (Money: the cost of one registration fee | Time: one visit | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, and it is easy — just bring the whole pile of medicines and show the doctor)
    • In short: Drugs interact with one another. The more kinds you take, the more chances there are for a collision. By the fifth, the risk starts to climb.
    • What you can do: Count everything you are taking — medicines, supplements, Chinese herbal medicine, topical drugs. If it is more than five, put "ask the doctor to review all my medicines together" on the list for your next visit.
    • Where this comes from: B | A 2024 consensus in Chinese geriatrics defines taking 5 or more drugs at once as polypharmacy, and 10 or more as hyper-polypharmacy. Its figures: among patients in China aged 60 and over, the polypharmacy rate is 70.8%, they take an average of 8.6 drugs a day, and the rate of adverse reactions is 29%; this group is only 12% of the population yet accounts for more than 30% of all drug spending. official wording
    • Who this is for: holds for people taking several drugs for chronic conditions. If you take only one or two and your condition is stable, there is no need to be anxious. Taking many drugs is not the same as taking the wrong ones; the question to ask is whether each drug is still needed now.
    • What you get back: independence — no longer afraid of "the medicines making me sick"

    6.3 Every prescription: ask four questions first — benefit, risk, alternatives, what if I don't¶

    • What it costs: a few extra questions at the appointment (Money: none | Time: a few minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, and it is easy — ask too many and the doctor may grow impatient, but this is your business)
    • In short: The NHS in the UK has promoted a set of questions for patients, shortened to BRAN: Benefit, Risk, Alternatives, Nothing. It is for decisions like "should I do this test, should I take this drug," and you can read it straight off.
    • What you can do: Benefit — what is this drug doing: preventing something for me, or treating a discomfort I have now? Risk — taking it, which warning signs should I watch for? Alternatives — is there a route without drugs, or a different drug? Nothing — what happens if I don't take it now, or wait a while?
    • Where this comes from: C | Common practice. NHS (Scotland) puts it on its "help patients ask the right questions" page, sourced to NHS Inform and Choosing Wisely UK. It is not a research finding but a communication framework from a public health body.
    • Who this is for: people who can still get to appointments themselves and talk with the doctor. For those asking on a parent's behalf, see 6.11. These four questions are not for arguing with the doctor; they are for taking the decision back into your own hands.
    • What you get back: dignity + independence — no longer afraid of "being shuffled into decisions I don't understand"

    6.4 A new complaint caused by medicine — don't rush to treat it as a new disease¶

    • What it costs: one review (Money: none | Time: half an hour | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: no)
    • In short: There is a common mistake: a discomfort caused by a drug is taken as a new disease, and another drug is added to treat it. Drug added on drug, layer on layer — this is called a "prescribing cascade."
    • What you can do: If a new complaint has appeared recently (swollen feet, constipation, dizziness, cough, poor memory), first ask whether it is caused by the one or two drugs newly added — don't assume it is a new disease. Ask the doctor with that question: could this be a reaction to one of my drugs?
    • Where this comes from: B | The mechanism is clear, there are examples, and there are population studies. A 2022 consensus in Drugs & Aging (ThinkCascades) compiled a list of prescribing cascades. The most typical: among blood pressure drugs, calcium channel blockers (such as amlodipine) readily cause ankle swelling; some people read it as "too much water" and prescribe a diuretic to clear it; a 2020 population study (JAMA Internal Medicine, Ontario, Canada, more than 40,000 people) found that older adults newly started on such drugs were more likely to be prescribed a diuretic afterwards. third-party research
    • Who this is for: most worth checking for people who recently changed drugs and then developed symptoms. If the symptoms are sudden and severe, treat it as an emergency first; don't stop drugs at home on your own.
    • What you get back: money + independence — no longer afraid of "more and more drugs, feeling worse and worse"

    6.5 Sleeping pills, antihistamines, painkillers, stomach drugs: pull these classes out and go through them first¶

    • What it costs: going through your pill boxes with a list beside you (Money: none | Time: ten-odd minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: no)
    • In short: There are certain classes of drugs that go wrong especially easily in older people; for decades they have been on lists of "what older people should avoid." Those lists are public, and you can check your drug names against them yourself.
    • What you can do: Look at these four classes first:
    • Sleep aids, sedatives: the "-pam" and "-zolam" drugs, such as diazepam, alprazolam, estazolam, zolpidem. The American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria (2023 edition) lists this whole class as "avoid"; China's Guidelines on Drugs to Use with Caution in Older People also says not to use them for insomnia, agitation, or delirium.
    • Antihistamines: first-generation antihistamines, such as diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine (Chlor-Trimeton), promethazine. Beers 2023 also lists these as "avoid": they have strong anticholinergic effects and older people metabolize them slowly, so they easily cause poor memory, dry mouth, and constipation; long-term accumulation is linked to falls, delirium, and cognitive decline.
    • Painkillers: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen, diclofenac. Beers 2023 advises against long-term use unless there is no other option and a stomach-protecting drug can be used at the same time. It states: with use for 3–6 months, fewer than 1% of people develop a stomach ulcer, bleeding, or perforation; by one year this rises to about 2%–4%.
    • Stomach drugs: proton pump inhibitors (the "-prazole" drugs). Beers 2023 advises against using them continuously for more than 8 weeks unless there is a clear reason (such as needing long-term steroids or painkillers, or having severe esophagitis). Concerns with long-term use include gut infections, pneumonia, and fractures.
    • Where this comes from: B | These come from publicly released drug-use standards, with evidence grading, but they are not the same for everyone. Besides the US Beers 2023, Europe has STOPP/START (2023 edition, 190 items: 133 STOPP + 57 START), and China has the Criteria for Potentially Inappropriate Medication in Older Chinese People 2024 edition (154 items); they can be read alongside one another. official wording
    • Who this is for: these classes are "usually best avoided or used with caution," not "never allowed." Which drug, what dose, whether it can be stopped — only a doctor who knows your whole situation can decide. If you see a drug on a list, don't stop it yourself; take it and ask the person who prescribed it.
    • What you get back: independence — no longer afraid of "the drugs I take including ones I shouldn't"

    6.6 In geriatrics, the more common move is to cut medicine down¶

    • What it costs: one appointment (Money: the cost of one registration fee | Time: one visit | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: no)
    • In short: Other departments watch one disease and add drugs; geriatrics watches one person and often does the reverse: stops what can be stopped and cuts down what should be cut down. This is called "deprescribing."
    • What you can do: At your next appointment, take the initiative and say to the doctor: "Please help me look at all my medicines together, and see which still need to be taken and which can be tried at a lower dose or stopped." Bring the list from 6.7. Don't cut drugs yourself; some drugs (such as anti-epileptics, steroids, sedatives) can cause serious trouble if stopped suddenly.
    • Where this comes from: B | Deprescribing has research support, but the effect is modest. A systematic review plus pooled analysis found that deprescribing in primary care slightly reduces the number of drugs, falls, and hospital admissions (Aging Medicine, 2026). The 2024 Chinese polypharmacy consensus also writes "deprescribing" and regular review into routine work.
    • Who this is for: people on multiple drugs whose condition is stable. For someone in an acute illness, just out of hospital, or unstable, get the immediate problem under control first and leave deprescribing for later. But deprescribing is not "the more the better" — stopping the wrong drug also causes harm, so it must be done in a doctor's hands.
    • What you get back: independence + money — no longer afraid of "more and more drugs"
    • How this works in China: see 6.10.

    6.7 Make a medication list you can take to the clinic¶

    • What it costs: half an hour and one sheet of A4 paper (Money: none | Time: half an hour | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, easy)
    • In short: What the doctor most wants to know is everything you are taking, and you can probably not say it all. One sheet of paper solves this.
    • What you can do: Write it under these headings; when you are done, take a photo and save it on your phone, named "My medicines": drug name (write the generic name, e.g. "amlodipine"); how many times a day, how many tablets each time, before or after meals; why you take it (for which disease, prescribed by whom); which hospital, which department; don't leave out supplements, Chinese herbal medicine, topical drugs, or eye drops; and a separate column "what I want to ask": which can be stopped, which one makes me feel unwell.
    • Where this comes from: C | Common practice. The 2024 Chinese polypharmacy consensus writes that anyone on multiple drugs should have a complete, up-to-date, easy-to-understand medication list, and should have at least one medication consultation a year.
    • Who this is for: everyone taking medicines, especially people whose memory is starting to slip and whose children pick up the drugs for them. The list should show its date; rewrite a new one whenever the drugs change.
    • What you get back: dignity + independence — no longer afraid of "being unable to say what I take and being treated as muddled"

    6.8 Ginkgo plus warfarin: someone ended up in hospital with a brain bleed¶

    • What it costs: a glance at the supplement bottles (Money: none | Time: a few minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: no)
    • In short: Supplements are not "harmless sugar pills"; they can also collide with drugs. There is a set of combinations already on record where things have gone wrong.
    • What you can do: If you or a parent takes an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel and the like), pick these out separately and ask the doctor about them: ginkgo leaf, danshen, dong quai, ginseng, high-dose vitamin E.
    • Where this comes from: B | There are case reports and mechanisms, but no randomized trial. A review (2015) recorded one case: a 78-year-old woman who, five years after coronary bypass and taking warfarin throughout, developed a left-sided cerebral hemorrhage two months after adding a ginkgo-leaf preparation, while the prothrombin time — which reflects clotting — showed no clear change. A normal lab report could not hold back the bleeding. In China, too, clinical pharmacists have reported a case of gum bleeding after warfarin was combined with ginkgo leaf. On the mechanism side, ginkgo, danshen, and dong quai are thought to increase bleeding tendency. third-party research
    • Who this is for: it matters most for people taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs. If you take these supplements and also must take an anticoagulant, don't stop the anticoagulant yourself; go and ask the prescribing doctor and the pharmacist.
    • What you get back: independence — no longer afraid of "medicines and supplements together causing internal bleeding"

    6.9 In your eighties, the targets for blood sugar and blood pressure can be re-negotiated with your doctor¶

    • What it costs: one appointment, one conversation (Money: the cost of one registration fee | Time: one visit | Effort: none | Do you have to speak up: yes, easy)
    • In short: When you are young, the lower blood sugar and blood pressure are, the better; in your eighties and frail, whether they should still be pushed that low may flip the other way. Pushed too low, the risks of low blood sugar, dizziness, and falls pop up.
    • What you can do: Take these sentences to the doctor: "At my age and in my condition, what should my blood sugar and blood pressure be set at?"; "Is this current target set for a general person, or set for me?"; "To loosen it a bit, would I need to reduce my drugs?" Leave the target values to the doctor; don't change them yourself.
    • Where this comes from: B | Guidelines support stratifying by physical condition and allowing the targets to be loosened, but the specific numbers differ from person to person. The Chinese Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diabetes in the Elderly (2024 edition) stratifies older people by health status and states, for the poorer-health group, that blood sugar targets may be loosened appropriately; the Chinese Guideline for the Management of Hypertension in the Elderly (2023) also states that for frail older people, the blood pressure level at which drug treatment begins, and the target value, may be loosened appropriately. official wording
    • Who this is for: most worth discussing for people of advanced age, frail, or at risk of falls or low blood sugar. For people who are still robust and without these risks, the targets may instead need to be tightened — and don't cut your drugs yourself just because you heard the word "loosen."
    • What you get back: independence — no longer afraid of "being tied to a number and harmed by it"

    6.10 How this works in China: community pharmacists, long prescriptions, family doctors¶

    • Community pharmacists and pharmacist-run clinics: Many hospitals in mainland China have opened pharmacist clinics, and in 2024 operational standards for an "older-adult polypharmacy clinic" appeared. The national Opinions on Accelerating the High-Quality Development of Pharmaceutical Services (Guoweiyifa [2018] No. 45) requires pharmaceutical services to reach down to the community level. Go to your community health centre and ask: is there a pharmacist who can review all my medicines for me?
    • Long prescriptions for chronic conditions: The Rules for the Management of Long-Term Prescriptions (Trial) (Guoweibanyifa [2021] No. 17) states that a long-term prescription is generally within 4 weeks, and for patients with a stable chronic condition it may be extended appropriately, but never beyond 12 weeks. The benefit is not having to travel to a big hospital every month; still, the doctor should review the medicines again every few months.
    • Contracted family doctors: Since 2016 the state has promoted contracted family doctor services (Guoyigaibanfa [2016] No. 1). After you sign up, follow-up for chronic conditions, prescriptions, and referrals can go through your family doctor first. What the service includes and what it charges differ from place to place; go to the community health centre where you live and ask "what can signing up do for me?"
    • The detailed rules for these three things, and how each links to medical insurance (yibao, China's public medical insurance — a separate scheme from Medicare, the NHS, or private insurance elsewhere), differ from city to city. Go by the latest local wording.

    6.11 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    He may refuse to talk about cutting back on medicine, or he may feel you are implying he is past managing. Here are some things you can do that don't need his agreement:

    • Help him make the list (6.7) and save a photo. Even if he doesn't have it, it helps that you keep a copy on your phone.
    • When you go to the appointment with him, ask the four questions from 6.3 on his behalf, especially "what if I don't take it."
    • Don't cut his drugs for him, and don't throw medicines away in front of him. Which can be stopped — let the doctor say it in his presence.
    • Once he feels you are treating him as "a patient who can't control himself," he may push back harder. Hand the conversation over to the doctor; see Chapter 20, 20.5.

    6.12 The one thing you can do today¶

    Take everything you take today (medicines, supplements, Chinese herbal medicine, eye drops), lay it out on one table, take a photo, count how many, save it on your phone, and name it "My medicines."

    Next time you see the doctor, show it to them and say one sentence: "Please look through it all with me, and tell me which ones I still need."

    6.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • The three figures "70.8%", "8.6 drugs", and "29%" are given in the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Assessment and Management of Polypharmacy in Older Adults (2024), which cites domestic studies. I checked back to the consensus text, but I did not obtain each original study it cites one by one; here I use them as the consensus states them.
    • The drug-use standards of different years (Beers 2023, STOPP/START 2023, PIM-China 2024) differ in their items. This chapter picks out only four common classes, and does not make a complete list.
    • Deprescribing can slightly reduce falls and hospital admissions; the evidence comes from a systematic review plus pooled analysis (Aging Medicine, 2026). The effect is modest and the effect of any single item is small; different studies are not fully consistent.
    • For long prescriptions, contracted family doctors, and pharmacist clinics, this chapter gives only the national-level documents; how it works in each city has to be asked locally.
