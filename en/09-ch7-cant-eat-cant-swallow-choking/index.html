    Chapter 7 · Warning Three: Can't Eat, Can't Swallow, Choking¶

    On the eating line there are three red lights. The first two come on slowly: can't eat, edging down over months; can't swallow, one meal choking worse than the last. The third is the fastest — that one minute of choking decides life or death.

    7.1 What this chapter is for¶

    Three things, with different time scales and different people to see.

    • Choking: a few minutes of first aid. It needs hands. You should know it now.
    • Can't swallow: a problem of swallowing function. Change how eating is done; see a rehabilitation department or a swallowing specialist.
    • Can't eat: a problem of appetite and nutrition. Find the cause (teeth, mood, chronic disease, medicines); see a nutrition clinic.

    The three also join into one line: can't eat and can't swallow push a person toward malnutrition and muscle loss. That line closes in 7.5 and 7.6.

    7.2 The minute of choking: first, look at whether he can still cough¶

    The only thing in this whole chapter that you should know "right now."

    Choking: first tell the two kinds apart¶

    • What it costs: Nothing. The Red Cross public first-aid training is free.
    • In short: An airway block comes in two kinds. If he can still cough, still make a sound, the airway is not fully blocked — the most effective "tool" is his own cough. Don't slap him, don't thrust on him; the moment you act you may push the thing deeper.
    • What you can do:
    • Ask: "Are you choking?" If he can speak and cough strongly, stand beside him and watch, and encourage him to cough hard.
    • Can't speak, can't cough, can't breathe, both hands to the throat (the choking sign), face turning blue or purple — this is when you act.
    • Adults (over 1 year old): 5 back blows + 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating, until the object comes out or he becomes unresponsive. Stand behind him, slightly to one side, have him lean his upper body forward, and strike 5 times with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades; if it doesn't come out, wrap both arms around his waist, make a fist with one hand above the navel and below the lower end of the sternum, cover it with the other hand, and thrust quickly inward and upward 5 times.
    • Once he loses consciousness, stop the thrusts, lay him flat, call 120, and start CPR.
    • Where this comes from: A | official wording: the first-aid guidelines updated in 2025 by the American Heart Association and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation set "back blows first, then abdominal thrusts" as the fixed sequence for those over 1 year old; Red Cross training in China has always taught it this way.
    • Who this is for: most adults of normal weight. Pregnant, or anyone you can't reach around the waist: switch to chest thrusts. Infants under 1 year: no abdominal thrusts; use "5 back blows + 5 chest compressions." Already unconscious: go straight to CPR. Don't fish for an object you can't see with your fingers — you'll only push it deeper.
    • What you get back: independence + relationships — no longer afraid of "watching him choke and not knowing what to do."
    • How this works in China: see 7.9.

    If you live alone, learn to save yourself first¶

    • What it costs: A few minutes, learned once (money: nothing | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
    • What you can do: ① Make a fist yourself, thumb side against the spot above the navel, cover it with the other hand, and thrust quickly upward and inward; ② or press your upper belly against the back of a chair, the edge of a table, or a railing, and bend over, pushing inward and upward again and again; ③ at the same time, make a loud noise and call out.
    • Where this comes from: A | official wording: authoritative first-aid guidelines include self-rescue methods.
    • Who this is for: especially people who live alone and eat alone during the day. If family lives with him, the point is to teach the previous section to them.

    7.3 Choking on water is not a small thing¶

    One cough is aspiration; "no reaction" after the cough is worse¶

    • What it costs: What it costs is effort: watching, recording, and maybe one extra clinic visit.
    • In short: Aspiration is food, water, saliva, or even stomach contents going into the windpipe. Overt aspiration you can hear: violent coughing, face turning purple, a wet voice after eating. Silent aspiration you cannot hear: something went into the windpipe, but his cough reflex is weak, so it can't come out and there's no commotion either. Quiet aspiration more easily drags on into pneumonia.
    • What you can do: Treat the following as "aspiration signals"; when one appears, pay attention: coughing or choking while eating or drinking; a "wet" or gurgling voice after eating; a feeling that "something is stuck in the throat"; unexplained low fever, duller spirits, slower reactions, smaller portions; repeated lung infections.
    • Where this comes from: A | third-party research: Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital explains "silent aspiration" very clearly and cites a large Chinese study — from 2011 to 2017, in-hospital aspiration pneumonia was about 9.4 cases per 100,000 person-years.
    • Who this is for: advanced age; after stroke or traumatic brain injury; dementia; Parkinson's; COPD; after radiotherapy for head and neck tumours; and people taking sedatives or antipsychotics. China's Guidelines for the Rehabilitation Management of Dysphagia (2023 edition) note that about 42% of people have dysphagia in the acute phase of stroke, reaching 80% with brainstem lesions.

    A cup of warm water is enough to self-check¶

    • What it costs: One cup of warm water, a few minutes (money: nothing)
    • In short: The most common bedside screen is the water-swallowing test (Kubota): drink about 30 millilitres of warm water and see whether it can be finished in one gulp within 5 seconds and whether he chokes. There is also a questionnaire, EAT-10. Doctors at Peking Union Medical College Hospital use the two together: a total score above 3 suggests possible dysphagia; above 4, see a specialist.
    • Where this comes from: B | third-party research: both tools are common in clinical use, but some doctors warn that the water-swallowing test may "underestimate" abnormality — testing fine at home does not mean there is no silent aspiration.
    • Who this is for: people who are awake and alert and can cooperate with drinking. If he already clearly chokes when swallowing, don't keep testing — go and see someone.

    "Repeated pneumonia" and "choking while eating" should be looked at together¶

    • In short: If someone in the family has "pneumonia" several times within a year, each time after eating, don't just put it down to a "poor constitution." Dysphagia is the most common risk factor for aspiration pneumonia. A clinical report from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University notes that aspiration pneumonia makes up about 71% of community-acquired pneumonia in the elderly.
    • Where this comes from: B | second-hand: "about 71% of community-acquired pneumonia in the elderly" comes from Expert Recommendations on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Adult Aspiration Pneumonia (2022), cited by the clinical report from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University; the same recommendations also state that aspiration pneumonia is only about 5%–15% of all community-acquired pneumonia — the two figures differ by population. The direction "repeated pneumonia calls for a swallowing check" holds up.
    • Who this is for: older people with repeated lung infections.

    7.4 The older people get, the less they want meat — it's not being fussy¶

    It's several things stacked together.

    • What it costs: Nothing. Understand first, then talk about change.
    • In short:
    • Taste and smell fade: smelling and tasting get dull, with salt the least felt. The dish "has no taste" because he genuinely can't taste it.
    • Filling up fast: the stomach empties more slowly, so a little makes him feel full. A young person who gets hungry can make it up; an older person often can't.
    • Teeth: missing teeth, ill-fitting dentures — if he can't chew meat he simply stops touching it, and meat is the most direct source of protein.
    • Mood: older people eat more when they have someone to eat with; bereavement, loneliness, and no heart for cooking all pull the amount down.
    • Illness and medicine: depression, dementia; many chronic diseases and drugs suppress appetite.
    • Where this comes from: B | third-party research: each one has mechanistic studies ("anorexia of aging" already has reviews), but how much each weighs varies from person to person.
    • Who this is for: starting from "recently eating noticeably less," it holds for most families. If the amount drops suddenly over a short time and weight loss comes with it, it is more likely an illness — see a doctor first.
    • One admission: the food is all laid out and he hasn't said he won't eat — it's just that bite by bite he gets slower and slower.

    7.5 Malnutrition and muscle loss twist into a circle of their own¶

    • What it costs: Nothing (first, understand this circle)
    • In short: A circle that speeds itself up — eating too little, not enough protein → muscle drops (sarcopenia), less strength → moves less, wants to eat even less → immunity and cough strength also drop, more prone to infection and hospitalisation → hospital and infection push appetite down again, back to step one. It is slow, but it keeps turning.
    • Where this comes from: A | third-party research, figures checkable: muscle drops about 8% per decade from the thirties and forties, faster from 65 to 80; losing 5% to 10% of body weight in half a year is a main signal of malnutrition (official wording, NHS UK); a multicentre survey at Peking Union Medical College Hospital found the prevalence of sarcopenia in those over 80 is about 42% (third-party research, cited from Guidelines for Medical Nutrition Therapy for Elderly Patients in China (2026 edition)).
    • Who this is for: changes in weight and food intake hold for yourself and for your parents. Action: weigh him, write it down, look at the trend.
    • One admission: once the circle is turning, shouting "eat more" alone won't turn it back.
    • Breaking the circle: change eating and moving together. For eating see 7.6; for moving see Chapter 5.
    • What you get back: independence — no longer afraid that "once he gets thin he'll never get fat again."

    7.6 When he can't swallow, how to change the three meals a day¶

    The principle in one sentence: change the food, change the posture, change the speed, and then learn the "see a specialist" signals by heart.

    Change the food: soft, slippery, not crumbly; not too thin, not too sticky¶

    • In short: Food that swallows well has even density, doesn't fall apart, moves easily in the mouth, and doesn't stick to the mucosa. The opposite is what chokes most easily: thin liquids (plain water, clear soup), sticky things (rice cake, peanut butter), granular things (dry rice grains, nut fragments), dry and crumbly things (crackers, steamed cake), and things with bones or spines.
    • What you can do: ① Make dishes into purées, steamed egg custard, thick soups, jelly-like — easier to swallow than clear soup; ② water that is too thin will choke him — thicken it with a thickener to "like thin porridge" or "like pudding"; pharmacies and online stores sell it; ③ don't drink through a straw — it sends water too fast and is more dangerous; ④ when drinking from a cup, leave at least half a cup in it; with too little water he has to tilt his head back, and tilting back chokes most easily; ⑤ internationally there is a common graded framework (IDDSI), with drinks and foods graded in eight levels from 0 to 7. Just remember the direction: the thicker the liquid the safer; the softer the solid the safer.
    • Where this comes from: B | third-party research: texture modification and thickening are common practice (the popular-science pieces from Peking Union and the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, and the dysphagia diet consensus, all say so); which level fits him best needs a professional assessment.
    • Who this is for: people who have already choked. If he only chokes occasionally and hasn't been assessed, don't thicken everything to an extreme yourself — ask first.

    Change the posture: sit upright, chin down; don't lie down, don't tilt back¶

    • What you can do: ① If he can sit, eat sitting, sitting upright, leaning forward about 20 degrees, neck slightly down; ② if he can only lie down, raise the head of the bed at least 30 degrees, flex the neck forward, and pad the shoulder on the paralysed side with a pillow; ③ the head can swallow with a slight chin-down, which narrows the entrance to the windpipe; ④ within half an hour after a meal, don't turn him, pat his back, or suction right away.
    • Where this comes from: B | third-party research: posture management is standard practice in swallowing rehabilitation.
    • Who this is for: people who have already choked. For those fully bedbound, the carer positions them according to the assessment.

    Change the speed: small bites, slow, swallow clean before the next bite¶

    • What you can do: ① a small bite, starting from a very small spoon (a few millilitres) — too much leaks and chokes, too little doesn't trigger the swallow; ② swallow before the next bite, doing a few "dry swallows" after each; ③ alternate solids and liquids, swallowing a bite of solid then a small sip of water (also thickened); ④ don't talk, and don't watch a phone or TV, while eating.
    • Where this comes from: C | this book's own check (2026-10): feeding training is a common practice in rehabilitation; see the popular-science pieces from Peking Union and the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University.

    When these signals appear, it's time to see a swallowing specialist¶

    • In short: Repeated choking, a wet voice after eating, repeated lung infections, longer mealtimes, weight going down; an abnormal Kubota test or EAT-10 self-check; having already been hospitalised because of this. One is enough to go.
    • What you can do: Find a doctor who does swallowing assessment — rehabilitation department, geriatrics, otolaryngology (ENT), or neurology — and a speech-language therapist. The gold standard is the videofluoroscopic swallow study and the flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing.
    • Where this comes from: A | official wording: popular-science pieces from Peking Union, Beijing Tsinghua Changgung, and other hospitals all point to the path "assessment → specialist."
    • Who this is for: anyone with any of the signals above.

    7.7 Dignity in this matter of eating¶

    • What it costs: Nothing. What it takes is a few changed habits.
    • In short: Being fed, eating slowly, and food dribbling onto clothes are the three things many older people least want seen. What it hurts is not the body but dignity. Let one meal tell him "I'm useless" and he'll eat less, go out less, eat in front of others less — and get worse nourished.
    • What you can do: ① If he can eat by himself, let him eat by himself, even if it's slow, even if it's messy; ② don't rush him, don't stare, don't wipe his mouth in front of others; ③ put a suction pad under the bowl, cut food into small pieces, use less soup — reduce the "mess" at the source rather than scolding after; ④ let him decide what to eat and how much — "You can help me, but ask me." (Chapter 20)
    • Where this comes from: C | this book's own check (2026-10): caregiving experience; no trial can measure "how many points dignity is worth," but it belongs to the "dignity" of the four ledgers and can't be cut.
    • Who this is for: older people who can still express their wishes should be left to do it themselves as far as possible. For those fully fed, the dignity falls on the person feeding: steady hands, eyes not looking away, mouth not commenting.
    • What you get back: dignity — no longer afraid of "being coaxed like a child at the dinner table."

    7.8 If you are reading this for a parent¶

    First do three things that don't need his consent:

    1. Observe. At meals, watch whether he chokes, whether his voice changes after eating, how long he takes, whether he touches meat. Note it in your phone.
    2. Record weight. Weigh him once a month. If he loses 5% to 10% in half a year, take him to ask a doctor.
    3. Learn it yourself too. Learn the Heimlich manoeuvre in 7.2 — the person who knows it isn't necessarily your parents, but you may be the only one near them who does.

    When he won't agree to change the food or refuses to see a doctor, it's still the three things from Chapter 20: build a file, ask the doctor on his behalf, find resources. Down at the level of eating, that means putting his choking, his weight changes, and his history of pneumonia onto one sheet of paper and handing it to the doctor at the next appointment.

    7.9 How this works in China¶

    First weigh him, then book a nutrition clinic or a sarcopenia clinic¶

    • In short: In big hospitals, nutrition is handled by the clinical nutrition department, and the elderly by the geriatrics department. The 2024 Chinese Expert Consensus on the Outpatient Management Standards for Sarcopenia in the Elderly already recommends that hospitals at all levels open sarcopenia clinics, equipped with body-composition analysers and grip-strength meters; for a first screen a soft tape measure around the calf can be used.
    • What you can do: When booking, say three things clearly — how much weight he has lost in half a year, how long he has been eating less, and whether he has choked. After the doctor screens and assesses, they then decide whether to teach you to change his way of eating or to add oral nutritional supplements. Not every hospital has one; big-city tier-3 hospitals are easier to find.
    • Where this comes from: A | official wording: Chinese Expert Consensus on the Outpatient Management Standards for Sarcopenia in the Elderly (2024 edition), led by the Geriatrics Branch of the Chinese Medical Association (released by Peking Union Medical College Hospital, 2025-08).

    What's in the community canteens and elders' dining halls¶

    • In short: Since 2023 the state has been promoting meal assistance for the elderly. With the approval of the State Council, the Ministry of Civil Affairs and 10 other departments issued the Action Plan for Actively Developing Meal Assistance Services for the Elderly; a sample survey showed 22.1% of older people have a need for meal assistance. The name differs from place to place: Shanghai calls them "community elders' canteens," Guangdong calls them "elders' dining halls," and there are also meal-assistance points and home delivery.
    • What you can eat and what it costs: a meal is put together like ordinary food, and many places have tiered subsidies for the very old, the disabled, and those living alone, cheaper than eating out. By the end of 2025 Shanghai had built 521 community elders' canteens and 1,977 meal-assistance points; by 2024 Guangdong had built more than 3,600 elders' dining halls.
    • One pitfall: many canteens are kept going by subsidies; once the subsidy shrinks they easily run at a loss and close.
    • What you can do: Ask the residents' committee or the sub-district eldercare service centre three questions — is there a meal-assistance point in this area, can someone in my situation get a subsidy, and can it be delivered to the home.
    • Where this comes from: A | official wording: the Action Plan for Actively Developing Meal Assistance Services for the Elderly by the Ministry of Civil Affairs and 10 other departments, and public data from the Shanghai Civil Affairs Bureau.

    For the teeth, see the dental department first¶

    • In short: If he can't chew meat, fix the teeth first, not his willpower first. Fill the missing teeth and adjust the dentures, and the amount he eats often goes up with it.
    • What you can do: Go to the dental department of a general hospital, a dental hospital, or the prosthodontics department (the one that makes dentures); some places have a geriatric dental clinic.
    • What to know in advance: implants and dentures are mostly paid out of pocket, and medical insurance generally doesn't cover dentures. Ask the price and the plan first.
    • Where this comes from: C | this book's own check (2026-10): the care path is common sense; how much local medical insurance reimburses for dental care (especially dentures and implants) differs by place — go by the latest local rules.

    For swallowing rehabilitation, find the rehabilitation department and a speech-language therapist¶

    • In short: Swallowing rehabilitation is handled in the rehabilitation department, and the matching role is the speech-language therapist (sometimes called swallowing therapy). Not every hospital has one at present; big-city tier-3 hospitals are easier to find.

    7.10 The one thing you can do today¶

    Search "your city + Red Cross + first-aid training" and sign up once. The Red Cross public first-aid training is free, and one class is enough to learn what to do in the minute of choking. Once you've learned it, teach it to one other person in the family — you may be the only one near them who knows it.

    7.11 What I am not sure about in this chapter¶

    • Online it circulates that "about 45% of people over 65 and about 75% of people over 80 have dysphagia," but only hospital popular-science pieces citing it could be found, and it couldn't be traced back to the original survey, so this chapter does not use those figures.
    • "Aspiration pneumonia makes up about 71% of community-acquired pneumonia in the elderly" is second-hand from Expert Recommendations on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Adult Aspiration Pneumonia (2022); its original study wasn't checked back. The same recommendations also give about 5%–15% of all community-acquired pneumonia; the two figures differ by population.
    • How much local medical insurance reimburses for dental care (especially dentures and implants) goes by the latest local document.
    • The subsidy standards and coverage of community canteens are local policy and change quickly; this chapter only writes "where to ask" and doesn't give specific amounts.
