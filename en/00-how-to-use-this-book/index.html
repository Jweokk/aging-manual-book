    Chapter 0 · How to use this manual¶

    Who wrote this¶

    Not a doctor.

    It was put together with the help of AI: the public material was read through, the official documents were checked one by one, and what was left was sorted out. So nothing in here comes from an experiment of ours — this book contains no measurements of our own. Every number carries a grade tag saying where it came from and how strong that source is, so you can open it and check for yourself; where we could not check it, we say so.

    Nor does this book set out to teach you how to grow old. The people who wrote it are aging too. They forget things, they find things a nuisance, and there are things they would rather not face.

    This is not a checklist¶

    You may open it and feel tense: so many entries, and you can't manage one of them.

    You don't have to. Of all the entries in this book, you will use two or three in a lifetime. Pick one or two and do them, and the book has paid for itself. Leave the rest for reference: come back to them when you, or your parents, actually reach that point.

    That is why it looks like a manual and not a textbook. A manual never asks you to read it from cover to cover. It only asks that you can find the right page when something goes wrong.

    Four accounts, not one¶

    This book wants to help you keep four accounts:

    • Independence — eating, using the toilet, washing, going out on your own; and being able to get things done on a phone (booking an appointment, hailing a ride, paying)
    • Money — your own retirement money, and the money your children get pulled into
    • Relationships — your partner, your children, old friends, neighbours, the community. What runs out first in old age is often not the body but the relationships
    • Dignity — not being humoured like a child, not losing your composure in front of people, still making your own decisions

    These four accounts cannot pay for each other. One fewer fall does not buy you an extra 300 yuan a month, and an extra 300 yuan a month will not buy back a fall you didn't take. Neither stands in for the other.

    This comes first because most books about aging keep only one account: how long you live, or how much you spend. Keep one account and you end up concluding "this is better than that"; keep four, and you find that many things do not need comparing at all — they are simply different things.

    How to read each entry¶

    Most entries have a fixed shape:

    • What it costs: what this item takes out of you — money, time, effort, and whether you have to bring something up. Aging has a peculiarity: the most expensive cost is often not money but being unable to say the thing out loud. So "whether you have to bring it up" gets a line of its own.
    • In short: what this is actually about. After reading this line you should be able to explain it to your partner or your children.
    • What you can do: actions you can follow. Only the method — nothing forces you to do it.
    • Where this comes from: where the entry came from. A = a public source with a specific checkable figure (systematic review, large study, the text of a statute); B = supported by research, but no exact figure available (a single small study, for instance); C = common practice and experience, not evidence. If you are unsure, follow the letter back.
    • Who this is for: who the entry holds true for. A healthy 60-year-old and an 80-year-old with several chronic conditions need different things.
    • The last two lines: What you get back (which of the four accounts this pays into, and which fear it removes), and How this works in China (who to ask, where to go, what subsidies exist).

    Some entries have only three lines. If one sentence says it clearly, we don't pad the format.

    Two paths¶

    Path one: for yourself. Match yourself first — which stage of aging are you at?

    • Stage 1 Not old yet (forty or fifty, or you still think of yourself as young): build up your reserves. Go to Part 1.
    • Stage 2 Just starting to age (your body has begun sending signals): read the signals, get what should be checked. Go to Part 2.
    • Stage 3 Starting to need help (your strength is going, or there is someone disabled at home): sort out the home, the money, the people. Go to Part 4.
    • Stage 4 The last stretch: how to have the conversations, how to make the arrangements. Go to Part 5.

    You can also skip the stages and look things up directly: Appendix B is arranged by physical symptom (pain, leaking, forgetting, falling), Appendix C by what you are afraid of (of getting poor, of burdening your children, of loneliness, of dying in pain).

    Path two: for your parents. Start with Chapter 20, "How to start the conversation". Your hardest problem is usually not that you don't know how to prevent a fall — it is that you don't know how to say it to them. Then look things up by symptom or by fear. Finally, read the section at the end of each chapter, "If you are reading this for a parent". It covers what you can do first, when they are not in the room or will not admit to anything.

    If you have ten minutes¶

    Do these three now. They cost nothing, or very little:

    1. Take the mats out of the bathroom and the entryway. Not put them away — take them out of the house. These are the two places where older people fall most, and a mat is the most common thing to trip on.
    2. Tonight, put motion-sensor night lights along the route from bed to toilet. Thirty to eighty yuan. Most people's problem is not that they have no light, but that they have one light, not a path.
    3. Photograph everything you take — medicine and supplements both. Keep it on your phone under the name "my medicine". Next time you see a doctor, show it to them. That is the first thing a doctor wants to know, and you will usually not be able to list it all from memory.

    When this book does not apply¶

    • Emergencies: chest pain, unable to speak, one side of the body not moving, confusion. Call 120. Chapter 8 has a one-page table for judgement; don't read it now, turn to it when something happens.
    • Once you have a diagnosis: this book is about preparing and arranging. It does not replace your doctor. Which treatment to take is a question for the doctor who is treating you.
    • About medicine: this book gives no doses and no prescriptions. Only who to go to and what to ask.

    One honest thing¶

    Some things this book cannot fix. Disability, the loss of a spouse, and the last thing of all — it cannot hold any of them back.

    What it can do is turn "caught off guard" into "arranged in advance". That difference often changes a person's last years more than curing any single disease.
