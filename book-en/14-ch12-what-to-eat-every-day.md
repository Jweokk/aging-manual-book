# Chapter 12 · What to Eat Every Day: Rebuilding the Table at Sixty and Seventy

The table of a person living alone shrinks quietly.

Same bowl, half the rice. The meat comes round and the chopsticks go around it. Vegetables go from three dishes to two, then to one. Nobody ever said "I'm not eating" — it's just that each meal is a little slower than the last.

What this chapter does is lay that table again. The core of it is one counter-intuitive thing: as you get older, your body's need for a few things does not go down. It goes up.

## 12.1 What this chapter solves

Lots of people hold to one belief: when you are old, eating less and eating plain — that is what looking after yourself means.

Half right. The portion can be smaller, and the direction is not wrong, but there are a few things you have to pay closer attention to than you did when you were young: protein, so the muscle doesn't quietly go; water; and the everyday business of salt, oil, alcohol and soup, community canteens, and supplements.

## 12.2 As you get older you need more protein, not less

- **What it costs**: No extra money. What changes is the combination (money: next to nothing | time: from this meal onward | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: *The Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Elderly (2022)* (《中国老年人膳食指南（2022）》) has one sentence that matters: for older people, especially the very old, the need for energy falls, while the need for protein and calcium rises [official wording]. As you age your body gets worse at turning protein into muscle — the same chopstick-load of meat, you can't use half of it.
- **What you can do**: Make "protein at every meal" the floor. Breakfast should not be only congee and pickles: add an egg and a glass of milk. Lunch and dinner each get one "solid dish" — meat, fish, shrimp, eggs, tofu, taking turns.
- **Where this comes from**: A | the guidelines put that sentence into their core recommendations; the direction is clear.
- **Who this is for**: sixty and up; it holds for most people. The exception is people already diagnosed with kidney disease who have to limit protein — how much they eat is up to the doctor.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of "can't eat, gets thin, never puts it back on"
- **How this works in China**: see 12.12.

## 12.3 How to spread protein across three meals: an egg at breakfast, one solid dish at lunch and dinner

- **What it costs**: A few extra yuan a day (money: next to nothing | time: a few minutes per meal | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: However much protein you eat at one sitting, there is only so much the body can take at a time to build muscle; spread across three meals it works better than one big load. The guidelines' combination: 1 egg and 1 glass of milk at breakfast, and at lunch and dinner a meat dish, vegetables, and one soy product [official wording].
- **What you can do**: In the morning, 1 egg and 1 glass of milk (or soy milk); at lunch and dinner a "palm-sized" piece of meat or fish, or a block of tofu; fish or seafood at least twice a week; if digestion is poor, mince the meat and steam it with egg, pick the fish bones out clean, stew the tofu soft.
- **Where this comes from**: A | from the food-combination advice in *The Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Elderly (2022)*.
- **Who this is for**: people who can eat normally. People who can't swallow, or who choke, need a different way of doing it — see Chapter 7.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of "I'm not eating less, and the strength still drains away"
- **How this works in China**: available in any food market or supermarket; when you can no longer cook, see 12.9 on community canteens.

## 12.4 If you want to cut down on salt: low-sodium salt works, but first see whether you are one of those people

- **What it costs**: One bag of low-sodium salt, a little dearer than ordinary salt (money: next to nothing | time: once | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: The guideline's ceiling is less than 5 grams of salt a day [official wording]. Just adding less salt often doesn't help: the saltiness comes from sodium, and you can put the salt spoon away while the sodium in soy sauce and pickles stays. Low-sodium salt replaces part of the sodium chloride with potassium chloride — less sodium, the saltiness still there.
- Evidence: a study called SSaSS, run in 600 villages in northern China with 20,000 people, swapped household salt for low-sodium salt and saw 14% fewer strokes, 13% fewer cardiovascular events, and 12% fewer premature deaths (third-party research: The George Institute for Global Health, 2021).
- But low-sodium salt is not for everyone: the extra potassium has to be excreted by the kidneys. People already diagnosed with hyperkalemia must not use it — the guidelines give a "strong recommendation against"; people with impaired kidney function (eGFR<60 ml/min/1.73 m²) who want to use it must ask a specialist first — a "weak recommendation against" [official wording: *Guidelines for the Promotion and Application of Low-Sodium Salt in China* (《中国低钠盐推广应用指南》), Chinese Journal of Preventive Medicine, 2024]. People taking potassium-sparing diuretics (such as spironolactone), ACE inhibitors, ARBs for blood pressure, or potassium supplements should also be careful: these drugs raise blood potassium on their own, so low-sodium salt is not advisable, or only under a doctor's guidance [official wording: Jilin Provincial Health Commission, 2021]. If you are one of these, ask your doctor first: "With my kidneys and my blood potassium, can I use low-sodium salt?"
- **What you can do**: Switch to low-sodium salt; go easy on cured and pickled things, pickles, cured pork; add the salt just before the dish comes off the heat, or lift the flavor with scallion, ginger, garlic, vinegar.
- **Where this comes from**: B | that swapping salt lowers risk is backed by SSaSS; which people must not swap is checked against the list above — don't generalize.
- **Who this is for**: with normal kidney function and no high blood potassium found, the swap is worth it. Bad kidneys, or on those drugs: don't swap; if you really want to, ask the doctor first.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of "a pinch of salt at every meal feeding the blood pressure"
- **How this works in China**: low-sodium salt is in any supermarket; if you're unsure, bring it up at your next clinic visit.

## 12.5 The other three items on the table: oil, alcohol, soup

- **What it costs**: A graduated oil-control jug (money: next to nothing | time: once | effort: none | do you have to speak up: yes — and it's a bit awkward, refusing a drink costs face)
- **In short**: The guidelines suggest no more than 25 to 30 grams of cooking oil a day; people with chronic disease should not drink, and if an adult does drink, no more than 15 grams of alcohol a day [official wording]. As for soup, stop believing "soup builds you up": however long you stew the meat, the protein barely dissolves into the soup — most of it stays in the meat (third-party research: The Paper, popular science).
- **What you can do**:
  - **Oil**: switch to the measuring jug; steam, boil, stew and dress more, fry less; skim the oil off the top of soup; order less takeout and fewer oily ready-made dishes.
  - **Alcohol**: don't keep liquor in the house; pour the baijiu into a smaller cup, or simply drink tea instead; at the table, don't press drinks on others and don't accept them. If you are on medication, alcohol can clash with blood-pressure, blood-sugar, sleeping and pain medicines — best not to drink.
  - **Soup**: if you drink the soup, eat the meat even more, and make the soup thinner; people with gout or high uric acid should go easy on rich meat broth.
- **Where this comes from**: B | less oil, limited alcohol, no faith in soup — the directions are all standard; there is no single trial showing "switch to an oil jug and you get fewer diseases."
- **Who this is for**: most people. With gout or high uric acid, little or no rich meat broth; on the drugs above, if you can skip the alcohol, skip it.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of "putting away too much oil, salt and alcohol without noticing"
- **How this works in China**: oil jugs and salt spoons are in supermarkets and online; whether you can drink at all — ask the family doctor at the community health centre.

## 12.6 When the teeth are bad or you can't swallow, soften the food first, then talk about eating enough

- **What it costs**: One dental visit, or a change in how you cook (money: one registration fee and up | time: once | effort: none | do you have to speak up: yes — and it's a bit awkward)
- **In short**: People who can't chew meat often simply stop touching it, and the protein goes with it. But "can't chew" is mostly a matter of teeth, and has nothing to do with "should eat less meat." Fill the missing teeth, adjust the dentures, and the appetite often comes back.
- **What you can do**: Deal with the teeth first — go to the dental department or the prosthodontics department; if it can't be solved for now, change how you cook: mince the meat and steam it with egg, pick the fish bones out clean, stew the tofu soft; if you choke or can't swallow, do texture modification and eating posture properly (details in Chapter 7).
- **Where this comes from**: C | this book's own check (2026-10): fixing the teeth first and changing the cooking second is the common-sense care path; swallowing is handled in Chapter 7.
- **Who this is for**: people with missing teeth, ill-fitting dentures, or a swallowing problem. With repeated choking, meals getting slower, weight going down — don't just change the cooking, see a swallowing specialist.
- **What you get back**: independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "wanting to eat and not being able to chew"
- **How this works in China**: the dental department of a general hospital, or a dental hospital; dentures and implants are mostly out of pocket — ask the price and the plan first.

## 12.7 Don't believe "thin in old age is worth more than gold": first work out whether you're losing muscle or fat

- **What it costs**: One bathroom scale, a few minutes (money: next to nothing | time: one minute | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: "Thin in old age is worth more than gold" has done real harm. What you actually need to guard against as you get older is muscle quietly going. Losing weight is not necessarily good — what you lost may be muscle, which means throwing away your strength and your independence along with it.
- **What you can do**: Same time, same scale, once a month; watch the trend, not the single number; a drop of more than 5% in half a year means ask a doctor [official wording: NHS UK lists losing 5% to 10% of body weight in half a year as a main sign of malnutrition]; if the weight is falling but the waist hasn't changed much, or strength is clearly down (can't open a bottle cap, can't lift things), what you're losing is more likely muscle.
- **Where this comes from**: B | weight trend, calf circumference and grip strength are the usual screening measures for sarcopenia; how to read the numbers is for a doctor to judge.
- **Who this is for**: sixty and up, especially anyone who has visibly got thinner in the past half year without dieting. A sudden drop over a short period, with feeling unwell, is more likely an illness — see a doctor first. More in Chapter 2.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of the illusion that "thin is good"
- **How this works in China**: weigh yourself at home and write it down; whether more tests are needed — ask the family doctor at the community health centre.
- One admission: muscle loss never hurts, which is exactly why it gets taken for "just getting old."

## 12.8 How to make sure of your water for the day: not thirsty does not mean not short of water

- **What it costs**: A graduated water bottle (money: next to nothing | time: all day | effort: none | do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: As you get older the thirst "reminder bell" gets dull; you may not feel thirsty while the body is already short of water. *Clinical Hydration Practice Guidelines for Older Adults in China (2024 edition)* (《中国老年人临床水化实践指南（2024版）》) lists older people as a high-risk group for dehydration, and notes that as many as 73.3% of older people in China don't reach the recommended daily water intake [official wording].
- How to do it (no fixed number of milliliters given): don't wait until you're thirsty — drink at fixed points: after getting up, after each meal, before bed; keep the bottle where you can see it and reach it; use urine colour as a ruler — dark means drink; the water in soup, congee, milk and fruit counts too; go easy on sweet drinks.
- **Where this comes from**: C | this book's own check (2026-10): the hydration guidelines recommend keeping a "drinking diary," and list fixed times, watching urine colour, and choosing plain water or weak tea as the methods.
- **Who this is for**: most people. The guideline's intake recommendation comes with a condition attached — "unless in special circumstances"; with heart failure, kidney failure, edema or hyponatremia, how much you drink is up to the doctor, and more is not better [official wording: that guideline].
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid of "that dizzy spell in summer was really dehydration and heat stroke"
- **How this works in China**: a graduated cup is enough to start; with heart or kidney disease, take "how much should I drink a day" to the clinic and ask.

## 12.9 The community canteen: how to find one, what you eat there, and why some shut down

- **What it costs**: The price of a meal, usually cheaper than eating out (money: next to nothing | time: one meal | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: Cooking for one and eating alone is where people most easily make do, and after a while of making do the protein, the vegetables and the water all drop off with it. The community canteen solves exactly this: company, a hot meal, and a price someone keeps an eye on. A sample survey found that 22.1% of older people have a need for meal assistance [official wording: Ministry of Civil Affairs policy interpretation].
- How to find one: ask at the residents' committee or the sub-district eldercare service centre: "Is there a meal-assistance point in this area?" Shanghai calls them "community elders' canteens," Guangdong calls them "elders' dining halls."
- What you can eat: ordinary food with meat and vegetables; many places have tiered subsidies for the very old, the disabled and those living alone, cheaper than eating out [official wording: the policy interpretation above]; by the end of 2025 Shanghai had built 521 community elders' canteens and 1977 meal-assistance points [official wording: Shanghai Civil Affairs Bureau].
- Why some can't keep going: the policy interpretation itself points it out — everywhere reports that "meal-assistance services are hard to sustain": the margin on a meal is thin to begin with, it is held up by subsidy, and if the subsidy is small and not enough people come, it slides from thin profit into loss and shuts after a while. The funding formula is "individuals put in a little, enterprises give up a little, government subsidizes a little, collectives add a little, society donates a little," with the aim of being "profitable and sustainable" [official wording].
- **What you can do**: Ask three questions: is there a meal-assistance point, can I get a subsidy, and can it be delivered to my home when I can't walk.
- **Where this comes from**: A | the policy and the local figures can both be checked; but subsidy standards and coverage differ by place and change fast.
- **Who this is for**: people who get around easily and live near a canteen get the most out of it. Rural and remote areas are still poorly covered.
- **What you get back**: relationships + independence — no longer afraid of "eating alone, and making do a little more each time"
- **How this works in China**: ask at the residents' committee, the sub-district eldercare service centre, or the community eldercare service station; subsidies depend on the locality.

## 12.10 When the community canteen can't hold up, what you do yourself

- **What it costs**: An hour or two at the weekend (money: next to nothing | time: once a week | effort: a bit tiring | do you have to speak up: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: Not everyone has a canteen nearby, and not every canteen survives. Rather than waiting for a table, build one yourself.
- **What you can do**: Batch cooking — cook once at the weekend, portion and freeze, three days' worth; ready-made components — washed vegetables, frozen dumplings, ready-to-eat chicken breast, unsweetened yogurt, throw together a meal with protein in it; cook with neighbors — two or three households take turns cooking and share; home delivery — ask the community whether there is any.
- **Where this comes from**: C | this book's own check (2026-10): this is caregiving and living experience, with no trial to measure it; but it holds up the very solid problem of "not eating enough."
- **Who this is for**: people living alone, and people for whom cooking is a strain. If family can lend a hand, the point is to share the cooking out, not to carry it alone.
- **What you get back**: independence + relationships — no longer afraid of "from the day I can't cook, I start eating too little"
- **How this works in China**: first ask the community about meal delivery, then talk to neighbors you know about cooking together.

## 12.11 Supplements and "nutrition products": when you really need them, and when you're buying peace of mind

- **What it costs**: Don't rush to spend (money: can be high or low | time: one consultation | effort: none | do you have to speak up: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: If you can eat, and eat enough, protein powder, calcium tablets and vitamins are mostly unnecessary — the money buys peace of mind. The people who genuinely need supplements are, first, those who don't eat enough (illness, recovery, poor appetite), and second, those a doctor has found to be deficient; in that case they can be used under a doctor's or dietitian's guidance [official wording].
- One iron rule: health foods are not medicine. The State Administration for Market Regulation requires the packaging to carry the line "health food is not medicine and cannot replace medicine in the treatment of disease" [official wording: *Guidelines on Warning Wording on Health Food Labels* (《保健食品标注警示用语指南》), in force since 2020].
- **What you can do**: Before buying, ask three questions: am I actually short of this, has a doctor said so, and could it stand in for my medicine; keep a list of the supplements you take and hand it to the doctor along with your medicines at the next visit, to see whether they collide (Chapter 6); when it's a sales lecture, or someone calling it a "sure cure," walk out.
- **Where this comes from**: B | "food first, supplements under a doctor's or dietitian's guidance" is the standard line in both the guidelines and market regulation.
- **Who this is for**: if you can eat, spend the money on food first. If you genuinely don't eat enough, or you're recovering, supplements may help; which one and for how long — ask a doctor.
- **What you get back**: money + dignity — no longer afraid of "being talked into buying a cupboard full of useless things by a lecture and an acquaintance"
- **How this works in China**: for medicines look for "Guoyao Zhunzi" (the national drug approval number), for health foods look for the "blue hat" logo — only the former is medicine; if you're unsure, take it to a doctor.

## 12.12 How this works in China

Everything in this section is the mainland-China system: the people you ask are the residents' committee, the sub-district office and the civil affairs department — roughly the counterpart of a local senior-services or eldercare office in the US or UK, with the names differing from city to city.

- **Nutrition clinic**: In big hospitals nutrition is handled by the clinical nutrition department, and older people by the geriatrics department; the 2024 *Chinese Expert Consensus on the Outpatient Management Standards for Sarcopenia in the Elderly* (《老年人肌少症门诊管理规范中国专家共识》) already recommends that hospitals at all levels open sarcopenia clinics. When you register, say three things clearly: how much weight has gone in half a year, how long you have been eating less, and whether you choke.
- **Community canteens and meal subsidies**: Ask at the residents' committee, the sub-district eldercare service centre or the community eldercare service station whether there is a meal-assistance point in this area, whether you can get a subsidy, and whether it can be delivered to your home. Standards differ by place; go by the latest local wording.

## 12.13 If you can only do one thing today

Go and weigh yourself once now, and put it in the notes app on your phone. Then at tonight's dinner, add one "solid dish" to the table — an egg, a glass of milk, a palm of meat or fish, or a block of tofu, just one, set it in the middle.

Weighing yourself gives you a baseline; every weigh-in after that is compared against it. Adding one solid dish gives your body that bit of protein we just talked about. Neither takes more than a few minutes.

## 12.14 If you are reading this for your parents

**How to say it out loud.** Don't say "you eat too little, too plainly" — in his ears that means "you're old, you're finished." Put it another way, and put yourself in it too: "I've been learning a new way of eating — an egg at breakfast, a bit of meat or tofu at lunch and dinner. Shall we try it together?" Or come at it from the cooking end: you cook, put the steamed minced meat with egg and the soft-stewed tofu on the table, don't comment, and he'll pick up his chopsticks himself.

**What you can still do when he disagrees.** Three things that don't need his agreement: every time you visit, bring a meat dish he can chew, or cook one in front of him; keep a record in your phone of his weight each time, how much he eats, and whether he chokes, and hand it to the doctor at the next appointment; go to the community once and ask about meal assistance — is there a canteen in this area, can it be delivered to the home, is there a subsidy for someone in his situation. The more concrete your preparation, the less you have to rely on persuading him.

## 12.15 What I am not sure about in this chapter

- The names and addresses of each city's "elders' canteen map" and "meal-assistance point finder" differ a lot, and I have not checked them one by one; asking at the residents' committee or the sub-district eldercare service centre is more reliable than hunting for a web portal.
- Subsidy standards and coverage for community canteens and elderly meal assistance are local policy and change fast; this chapter only writes "where to ask," not specific amounts.
- For *The Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Elderly (2022)* this chapter quotes the "Core Recommendations" and the "Illustrated Explanation of the Balanced Diet Pagoda" published on the Chinese Nutrition Society website; I have not checked each point against the page numbers of the printed book.
- This chapter gives no doses and makes no diagnoses. Whether to switch to low-sodium salt, how much water to drink, whether to take supplements — all of it depends on your own illnesses and medicines. Ask a doctor or dietitian.
