# Chapter 16 · Where the Money Comes From: How the System Pays Your Bills

Once you retire you find that the bills don't stop just because you stopped working. Utilities, food, medicine — small bills that arrive every day. What actually turns a household over is the other kind: one hospital stay, one serious illness, one person who goes down and never gets up again.

Chapter 4 said the money account has to be built early. This chapter asks a different question: on the day the bill actually arrives, which of these bills will the state's system pay for you, and which ones do you carry yourself. These bills have edges, and they come in an order.

## 16.1 What this chapter settles

The old saying is that you save for old age to guard against old age. In money terms it comes down to three different pots: **the pension pays the cost of living while you're alive, medical insurance pays the doctor's bill, and long-term care insurance pays for care once you can no longer manage.**

Each pot covers one stretch. The trouble is that a lot of people treat the three as one — "I have a pension, surely that covers seeing a doctor" — and only find out, on the day someone has to be turned and washed, that the earlier pot does not stretch to the later need.

This chapter sets out where each pot ends, how reimbursement is calculated, where to go, and what fills the gap. If you remember one thing, remember this: which pot pays for which thing, and where to ask when something happens. For an English reader, the three pots are roughly the shape of Social Security, Medicare and long-term care insurance — but these are mainland China's own contributory public schemes, with their own rules, and the differences matter more than the resemblance.

## 16.2 Three pots pay for three different things — don't merge them into one

| This pot | What it covers | What it does not cover |
|---|---|---|
| Basic pension | Cash paid into your account every month; covers food and living while you're alive | Not doctor's bills, not the carer's wages |
| Basic medical insurance | The medical cost of being ill: hospital, outpatient, medicines | Not food, not day-to-day care after you become disabled |
| Long-term care insurance | Care services after you become disabled | Pays out no cash, and covers no doctor's bills |

Medical insurance does not put money in your hand; it pays the doctor's bill for you. Long-term care insurance doesn't hand out money either; it buys care services for you. **Once you separate "paying out cash" from "paying the bill", you know which account to watch.**

Between what the system will pay and what you actually take out of your pocket there is always a stretch, and the household fills that stretch itself.

## 16.3 Medical insurance reimbursement: learn the three catalogs and the three lines first

- **What it costs**: work it out once, and it keeps paying off (Money: none | Time: half an hour | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: medical insurance does not reimburse whatever you spent. It looks first at whether the medicine or the test you used is on the reimbursement list, then at how much you spent: what's off the list isn't covered, and neither is too little or too much. In mainland China this scheme is *yibao*, a contributory public scheme — the rough counterpart of Medicare. Medicare pays a defined share of covered services, with a deductible and coinsurance. *yibao* passes the same bill through a fixed list of what it will pay for, plus three limits of its own.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Learn the three catalogs: medicines, medical services, medical consumables. Only what is inside them is reimbursed; what is outside you pay in full.
  - ② Learn the three lines: the **deductible** (below it, you pay yourself), the **reimbursement rate** (at community level usually higher than at a big hospital), and the **annual cap** (above it, nothing more is paid).
  - ③ *yibao* only reimburses costs "within the policy scope". It also leaves alone the "self-paid" and "own-share" lines on the bill.
- **Where this comes from**: A | National averages are only a reference: within policy scope the employee scheme's fund pays about 80% and the resident scheme about 70% (National Healthcare Security Administration reading of Guobanfa [2021] No. 42). Deductible, reimbursement rate and cap all vary with where you are enrolled, which scheme you are in, and the tier of hospital — the place you are enrolled governs. Ask the medical insurance bureau there, or look it up in the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: everyone who is insured; first work out whether you pay employee medical insurance or resident medical insurance.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "one serious illness and the household collapses, and I have no idea how much medical insurance will actually pay"
- **How this works in China**: the medicine catalog is the latest edition (Yibaofa [2025] No. 33, in force from 2026-01-01). Look up the details in the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app, or ask the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.4 Before you travel for treatment, do the out-of-area filing on your phone

- **What it costs**: a few taps on the phone, a few minutes (Money: none | Time: a few minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: if you go to the city where your children live for treatment without filing, you may be reimbursed less and may have to pay the hospital first and claim the money back by hand. With the filing done, you can settle on the spot by scanning your code. The reason a filing is needed at all is that in mainland China medical insurance is run city by city, not as one national plan: the city where you are enrolled is the one that holds the money, and the hospital in the other city has to be told in advance.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Pick the right category: **long-term residence outside your province** (moving somewhere else to live, moving to be near children, being posted there for work); **temporary treatment away from home** (referral, emergency rescue, seeing a doctor while visiting family or traveling).
  - ② Two entrances: the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app, or the "out-of-area treatment filing" service in the National Healthcare Security Administration's official account.
  - ③ After identity verification, choose the filing type and the place you will be treated, and upload the documents; some places accept a personal declaration in place of proof.
  - ④ Before you leave, check the progress of the filing and confirm that the hospital where you will be treated is set up for direct settlement.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The rules come from Yibaofa [2022] No. 22 and Yibaofa [2024] No. 21; but the documents, the time limits and the size of the reduction differ from place to place — one place may cut reimbursement by 10 points, another by 20. The place you are enrolled governs: ask the medical insurance bureau there.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: people who live outside their home city long-term, or who go to a big hospital in another province. If you only ever see doctors in your own city, you don't need this.
- **What you get back**: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "taking Mom and Dad to a big-city hospital means laying out tens of thousands first and then not getting it back"
- **How this works in China**: the National Medical Insurance Service Platform app; the medical insurance bureau window where you are enrolled. For the filing type, the time limits and any reduction, ask that bureau. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.5 On long-term medication for a chronic disease, get the outpatient chronic-and-special-disease designation first

- **What it costs**: one trip to a hospital or a medical insurance window (Money: none, or the cost of one registration fee | Time: one visit | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: with illnesses like high blood pressure and diabetes you take medicine every day, and the money goes out at the outpatient window. Get the condition designated an "outpatient chronic and special disease" and outpatient medicine is reimbursed at a higher rate; without the designation it is reimbursed against the ordinary outpatient quota.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① First ask the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled, or your designated hospital: is my condition on the local list of outpatient chronic and special diseases?
  - ② If it is, apply for the designation: take your medical records, the diagnosis certificate and the test results to the medical insurance agency or the designated hospital.
  - ③ Once it is approved, prescriptions and follow-up visits are settled at the chronic-and-special-disease rate, and a long-term prescription can be written to cover a longer period at one time.
  - ④ If you need treatment across provinces, first confirm that this condition can be settled directly across provinces both where you are enrolled and where you will be treated.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The conditions that can be settled directly across provinces have gone from 5 to 10: high blood pressure, diabetes, outpatient radiotherapy and chemotherapy for malignant tumors, dialysis for uremia, and anti-rejection treatment after organ transplant, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, rheumatoid arthritis, coronary heart disease, viral hepatitis and ankylosing spondylitis added in 2024 (Yibaobanfa [2024] No. 19). But the list of conditions that qualify for the outpatient chronic-and-special-disease *benefit*, and the reimbursement rates, are set by each pooling area; there is no national standard. The place you are enrolled governs — ask its medical insurance bureau or the insurance office of your designated hospital.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: people on long-term medication with high outpatient costs get the most out of it; if you only see an ordinary outpatient clinic now and then, you don't need it.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "the money for medicine every day all disappears into the outpatient quota"
- **How this works in China**: the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled, the insurance office of your designated hospital, the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app (which shows whether the chronic-and-special-disease designation is held). The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.6 Critical illness insurance is automatic; medical assistance you have to apply for yourself

- **What it costs**: applying for assistance means one trip to a civil affairs or medical insurance window (Money: none | Time: one visit | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: yes — and it is a little embarrassing)
- **In short**: one hospital stay runs to hundreds of thousands. After basic medical insurance has paid, a second layer — **critical illness insurance** — pays on top; if you are on the resident scheme you pay nothing extra for it, it comes automatically. If that still isn't enough and the household is genuinely in difficulty, **medical assistance** is the floor underneath, but you have to go and apply for it.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Critical illness insurance: it is generally calculated together with the discharge settlement, with no separate application. If you are unsure, ask the bureau where you are enrolled: "Did this stay go through critical illness insurance?"
  - ② Medical assistance: first see whether you fall into one of these groups — dibao recipients, people in extreme difficulty, members of households on the edge of dibao, rural populations at risk of falling back into poverty, and people pushed into poverty by serious illness.
  - ③ Where to apply: start at the village committee, the residents' committee, or the civil affairs window of the township (sub-district) office; many places already handle it as a one-stop application.
  - ④ Bring the diagnosis, the hospital receipts and the household's income situation; if you are already registered as dibao or in extreme difficulty, the assistance is granted directly.
- **Where this comes from**: A | From Guobanfa [2021] No. 42: critical illness insurance pays automatically after basic medical insurance; on medical assistance, for dibao and extreme-difficulty households the assistance deductible is in principle abolished and the assistance rate is no less than 70%, while for households on the edge of dibao and for people pushed into poverty by serious illness the deductible is about 10% and 25% of the previous year's per-capita disposable income of residents. The place you are enrolled governs; ask the civil affairs or medical insurance window.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: households genuinely in difficulty, where the out-of-pocket cost of a serious illness is heavy. Applying is not a loss of face.
- **What you get back**: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "one serious illness drags the whole family down, and I am too ashamed to ask for help"
- **How this works in China**: the village committee, the residents' committee, the civil affairs window of the township (sub-district) office, the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled. Ask three things: which group we fall into, what the deductible and the rate are, and how to apply. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.7 Supplementary policies like huiminbao: small money against a big bill — check three things first

- **What it costs**: a few dozen to just over a hundred yuan a year (Money: hardly anything | Time: one enrollment | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: huiminbao is supplementary medical insurance that is "guided by the government, sold by insurers, bought by you voluntarily": a few dozen to just over a hundred yuan a year, cover of several million yuan, mainly filling in costs outside the medical insurance catalogs, your own share, and specialty drugs. The deductible is usually not low, so minor illness never reaches it; what it guards against is serious illness.
- Whether it is worth it comes down to three things:
  - ① **Who runs it**: is it an official product guided by the local government and underwritten by a licensed insurer, or a look-alike with a similar name?
  - ② **How high the deductible is**: the higher the deductible, the less it does for minor illness, and the more its whole point concentrates on serious illness.
  - ③ **What it pays and how**: are costs outside the catalogs covered, how are pre-existing conditions handled, and can it be settled directly?
- **Where this comes from**: B | In July 2025 the National Financial Regulatory Administration issued a notice regulating it. The controversy is that enrollment is low nationwide: healthy people drop out, sick people buy in, and it can slide into a "death spiral".
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: people who are older, have pre-existing conditions, and cannot buy ordinary commercial health insurance — for them "no health gate" may be the only policy still open; for people who already have good cover, it is mostly a top-up for specialty drugs and off-catalog costs.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "the off-catalog costs and the astronomically priced drugs are beyond what medical insurance can do"
- **How this works in China**: the official enrollment entrance guided by the local government (in some places the premium can be paid from the balance in your medical insurance personal account). Before you enroll, ask clearly about the deductible and what is covered. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.8 Long-term care insurance: the third pot, not yet in every city

- **What it costs**: one look-up, one question (Money: none | Time: one trip to a window or one phone call | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: long-term care insurance covers the care bill after you lose the ability to manage. It is the youngest of the three pots, and it generally does not pay you cash — it uses the money to buy care services for you. In mainland China this is a public social-insurance scheme rather than a policy you buy from an insurer; the rough counterpart is long-term care insurance in the US, except that there the cover is mostly a private policy you buy and pass medical underwriting for, and Medicare does not pay for custodial care.
- Where it has got to: 15 cities piloted it in 2016, and in 2020 it expanded to 49. In September 2025 the National Healthcare Security Administration reported coverage of 190 million people and spending of more than 85 billion yuan. By the end of 2025 the number insured had reached 308.54 million (Ministry of Civil Affairs and National Office on Aging, "2025 National Bulletin on the Development of Aging-Related Undertakings") — the two figures are counted differently: the first is coverage by the system, the second is people actually insured. In March 2026 the General Office of the CPC Central Committee and the General Office of the State Council issued "Opinions on Accelerating the Establishment of the Long-Term Care Insurance System", with the goal of "about 3 years, near-universal coverage nationwide by the end of 2028", and the contribution rate held at about 0.3%.
- **What you can do**: ① first check whether your city runs long-term care insurance; ② if it does, the disabled state has to last more than 6 months and be assessed and confirmed before you can receive benefits; ③ benefits mainly pay service fees, with no deductible.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The timeline, the coverage figures, the contribution rate and the goal come from official documents (National Healthcare Security Administration, 2025-09-25; General Office of the CPC Central Committee and General Office of the State Council, 2026-03-25); but benefits differ widely from place to place, and the pilot cities still need about 3 years to align with the national catalog.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: people already in a city that runs long-term care insurance. Another city's standards do not necessarily apply — check locally first.
- **What you get back**: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "one person becomes disabled, the whole family loses its balance, and the carer's bills drag the household under"
- **How this works in China**: the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled, a medical insurance service window, or the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app. Ask three things: "Does our place run long-term care insurance? How do I apply for the disability assessment? Which services are covered, and how much is paid?" The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.9 When one person goes down, these are the three bills the household actually pays

- **What it costs**: one careful pass through the accounts (Money: none | Time: half an hour | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: what long-term care insurance pays out is services, not cash. What really flows out of the household's wallet is the carer's wages, the income your children lose, and care supplies and transport.
- **What you can do**: list these separately and you can see how big the gap is.
  - ① **The carer's wages**: a live-in carer, an hourly carer, a bed in an institution — add it up monthly. This is the largest fixed cost after disability.
  - ② **The income your children lose**: if one child quits or takes long leave to provide care, that is tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of yuan a year in lost income — a bill with no receipt and no one to reimburse it.
- **Where this comes from**: C | These are the ordinary costs of family care, and there is no nationwide statistic. How much is reimbursed depends on the local catalog and the assessment grade.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: households with a disabled older person who is already being cared for by someone. Ask about local long-term care insurance early in the disability rather than after they are bedbound — it is simpler.
- **What you get back**: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "the carer's wages and the lost income drag the family's savings under"
- **How this works in China**: for which services local long-term care insurance covers, and what a carer costs, ask the medical insurance bureau where you are enrolled and the elder-care service providers. Agree with the family in advance "who provides the care, and for how long" (see Chapter 21).

## 16.10 The order to build it in: fill the foundation first, then the gaps

Money is limited, so there is an order to what you protect first and what you leave for later. Buying something fancy before the foundation is paid up is putting the cart before the horse. Work down this order:

1. **Get basic medical insurance in place and paid up**: the employee scheme or the resident scheme, never a year lapsed. This is the foundation under every reimbursement.
2. **Confirm whether critical illness insurance is there**: the resident scheme includes it; on the employee scheme it depends on local arrangements. While you are at it, find out how to apply for local medical assistance.
3. **Check long-term care insurance**: if your place has it, learn how the assessment works and which services are covered; if it doesn't, register that this risk is yours to carry.
4. **Fill the gaps**: supplementary policies like huiminbao for the people they suit; enterprise annuities and the private pension (see Chapter 4) come later still.

- **Where this comes from**: C | This order is the book's own, sorted on "protect the frequent first, the extreme later": first the likely cost of seeing a doctor, then the low-probability, high-cost serious illness, and only then the long account that is care after disability.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: people with spare money who want one more layer for later life; if your basic medical insurance is still lapsed and you are borrowing to see a doctor, reconnect the foundation first.
- **How this works in China**: for lapsed payments, back payments and enrollment, call 12333 or ask the social insurance agency where you are enrolled; for medical insurance, assistance and long-term care insurance, ask the medical insurance bureau there. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.11 These "retirement products" — don't hand over money yet

- **What it costs**: a few minutes of judgment, and a lifetime's savings spared (Money: none | Time: a few minutes | Effort: none | Do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: these categories go after people in later life specifically, and the tricks are always the same few.
- How to tell, three ways:
  - ① **The "house-for-pension" scam**: the real thing is a reverse-mortgage pension insurance policy — an insurance product, not an investment. The scam borrows the name, has you mortgage your home and put the money into its "high-interest investment", and you end up with neither the money nor the house.
  - ② **"High-interest wealth management"**: anyone promising "capital guaranteed, high interest, money back" and pressing you to sign on the spot should be treated as a scam. Regulated investment products are not allowed to promise your capital back.
  - ③ **Prepaid elder-care schemes**: cards that pay rebates, membership cards, prepaid cards, large membership fees — in substance they are using the money you paid in to keep their cash flow turning. The Ministry of Civil Affairs has issued a risk warning, and there is also a document regulating prepaid fees at elder-care institutions.
- Three brakes: for a large transfer, wait 24 hours before you do it; if someone wants you to mortgage property or move savings into "a certain account", stop; if you are not sure, ask your children, the community office, or the bank counter.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The scam types and methods come from warnings by the consumer protection bureau of the China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission and the Ministry of Civil Affairs' "Risk Warning on Illegal Fundraising in the Elder-Care Service Sector" (2023-06). Nothing here is investment advice.
- **Who this is for**: **Who it applies to**: older people who own a home or hold a lump sum. If you have already put money in, don't be ashamed — call the police first and keep the evidence.
- **What you get back**: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "a lifetime's savings get taken and I don't dare let anyone know"
- **How this works in China**: if you think you have been scammed, call 110; for the rules on prepaid fees at elder-care institutions, ask the local civil affairs bureau; to check whether a financial product is real, ask the bank counter or the financial regulator. The latest local wording prevails.

## 16.12 If you can only do one thing today

Open the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app, claim your personal enrollment record and your electronic medical insurance credential, and while you are in there glance at whether you pay employee medical insurance or resident medical insurance.

This step doesn't cost a single yuan and takes ten minutes. But every question you have later — how much is covered, where to claim — begins its answer here.

## 16.13 If you are reading this for your parents

In our parents' generation there are a lot of things they would rather carry alone than bring up, "money" and "illness" most of all. If you are reading this chapter for them, the most useful help you can give is to turn "ask them" into "look it up".

**How to say it out loud**: don't ask "will your medical insurance cover this", say "let me put your electronic medical insurance credential on your phone, so you can just scan the code when you see a doctor". You are installing a tool for them, not auditing their finances.

**Three things you can do without their agreement**: ① use the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app to look up their enrollment status and whether they hold the outpatient chronic-and-special-disease designation; ② if they live outside their home city long-term, or are going to another province for treatment, do the out-of-area filing in advance; ③ go to the medical insurance bureau where they are enrolled, or to the community office, and ask whether there is long-term care insurance locally, how the chronic-and-special-disease designation is granted, and how to apply for assistance when things are hard. None of the three costs much.

**What you can still do if they won't agree**: get the paperwork together first — diagnosis, hospital receipts, medicine list — into one bag. When the day comes, one bag of documents is worth more than ten conversations.

## 16.14 Where I am not sure

- Outpatient chronic and special disease: the 10 conditions that can be settled directly across provinces are nationally uniform, but the list of conditions that get the *benefit*, and the reimbursement rates, are set by each pooling area. This chapter cannot give one rate that works everywhere, only how to apply and where to ask.
- The documents, the time limits and the reimbursement reductions for out-of-area filing differ from place to place; the range given in this chapter is a summary and was not traced back place by place. The place you are enrolled governs.
- There is no single official national publication of huiminbao's payout ratio or enrollment rate ("payout ratio above 90%" comes from a media report quoting a local regulator; "overall enrollment about 5%" comes from an industry research article). Use the local product terms and official notices.
- Long-term care insurance benefits differ widely from place to place; this chapter gives the national timeline and targets. For what a particular city pays and how it assesses, go back and ask the local medical insurance bureau.
- There is no nationwide statistic for the income family carers lose or for the price of a carer; this chapter only sets out which bills to count.
