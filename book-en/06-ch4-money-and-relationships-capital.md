# Chapter 4 · Money and Relationships as Capital

On the day you retire, someone throws you a send-off and hands you a keepsake printed with the work unit's name. Three months later, all that's left of the place where you worked for thirty years is a number that lands in your salary card every month — a notch lower than your wage. The last message in the former-colleagues group is a forwarded health article. No one replies.

Those two things are the "capital" of your later life: a pension you have to bank early, and a network of relationships you have to bank early. This chapter is about how to bank those two — while there is still time.

## 4.1 What this chapter is for

Part One is called "Building Your Capital". The earlier chapters are about the body, and those all count as "self-reliance" among the four accounts.

This chapter is about the other two: money and relationships.

For the money account, the state has built you a three-tier frame; the top tier is the one you have to put together yourself, and if you never put a hand to it, it stays empty. The relationship account is trickier. It will not sit there and wait for you; it only depreciates. On the day you really need someone to knock on your door, who is still on the list depends on what you have put in now.

The two accounts share one thing: they only pay interest if you start early. Open them when you already need them and it's too late.

## 4.2 Pensions come in three tiers — first work out which tier you sit on

- **What it costs**: half an hour, one clear look (money: none | time: half an hour | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: pensions have three tiers. Tier one is the basic old-age insurance the state provides; as of the end of 2025 it covered 1.076 billion people nationwide, and most people's retirement money depends on it [official wording: MOHRSS 2025 statistical bulletin]. Tier two is the enterprise annuity or occupational annuity the employer provides; few employers have one. Tier three is the private pension you save yourself. Work out which tiers you're on first, and only then can you tell how solid the ground is.
- **What you can do**:
  - **Check tier one**: how many years you contributed and how much is in your individual account. Look it up in the "Zhangshen 12333" app, the "electronic social security card", or the National Social Insurance Public Service Platform.
  - **Check tier two**: ask your former employer whether it has an enterprise annuity; government agencies and public institutions generally have an occupational annuity.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The three tiers are an institutional frame spelled out in national documents. The outline of the 15th Five-Year Plan says to "accelerate the development of a multi-tiered, multi-pillar old-age insurance system", and the private pension comes from Guobanfa [2022] No. 7.
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: people who have contributed to social insurance and can find their enrolment record. If you have never enrolled, lapsed for years, or work in flexible employment, start by phoning the social insurance agency where you're enrolled: "How do I enrol in my situation, can I make up the gap, and at what standard?"
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "I don't have enough money, and I don't even know how much I have"
- **How this works in China**: to look up social insurance, use the "Zhangshen 12333" app or the "electronic social security card" (you can claim it inside common payment and chat apps); for social insurance questions call 12333, or go to the social insurance service hall where you're enrolled. Medical insurance is a separate entrance — use the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app. The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.3 The private pension account — if you don't open it, no one opens it for you

- **What it costs**: one account opening, then a deposit each year (money: voluntary, the cap is set by the state | time: half an hour to open | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: the private pension is "the retirement account you open for yourself": voluntary, you pay in, you buy financial products the state recognises, contributions are deductible against personal income tax, and you can only withdraw at retirement or when you meet the conditions. It sits outside social insurance — an extra tier you add on your own.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① First confirm whether you're in the urban-employee or the urban-and-rural-resident basic old-age insurance; either one qualifies you to open the account.
  - ② Open two accounts: the private pension account, through the National Social Insurance Public Service Platform, the "electronic social security card", or the "Zhangshen 12333" app; and the fund account, at a compliant commercial bank.
  - ③ Pay in each year. The current cap is 12,000 yuan a year (set by Guobanfa [2022] No. 7, which states it may be adjusted in time; as of October 2026 it is still this figure) [official wording].
  - ④ Choose the products yourself: savings, wealth-management products, commercial old-age insurance, and public mutual funds are all allowed, and since 2024 government bonds are in scope too [official wording].
- **Where this comes from**: A | The account channels, the contribution cap, the product range and the withdrawal conditions are all written in national documents (Guobanfa [2022] No. 7; Renshebufa [2024] No. 87).
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: people still contributing to social insurance, who have spare cash, and who already pay income tax. **But** it is not a good deal for everyone: the tax you save depends on your bracket, so if your income is low and you don't pay income tax to begin with, there's little to save; and the money runs in a closed account and cannot be taken out early except in the specified cases. Whether to open it, and how much to put in each year, is your own calculation.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "I have only one pension, and no one fills the gap"
- **How this works in China**: for opening an account and looking things up, go through the National Social Insurance Public Service Platform, the "electronic social security card", or the "Zhangshen 12333" app; for tax-break and withdrawal questions, ask the bank where you opened it, call 12333, or call the tax hotline 12366. The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.4 Long-term care insurance covers the care bill after you lose the ability to manage

- **What it costs**: one look-up, one question (money: none | time: one trip to a service window or one phone call | effort: none | do you have to ask: no)
- **In short**: the pension covers food on the table while you're alive; medical insurance — China's public health scheme, the rough counterpart of Medicare — covers seeing a doctor. But if one person becomes disabled and the whole family is knocked off balance, who pays that care bill? Long-term care insurance (LTCI) does. It generally doesn't hand you cash; it buys care services for you. In China this is a public social-insurance scheme rather than a private policy you buy from an insurer, and it pays care providers instead of sending money to the family.
- **What you can do**: ① First check whether your city runs LTCI. ② If it does, the disabled state generally has to last more than 6 months, and you must apply and be assessed before you can receive benefits [official wording]. ③ Benefits have no deductible and mainly pay the service fees of home, community, and institutional care [official wording].
- Where this one has got to: it has followed a "pilot first, then rollout" path. The first 15 cities piloted it in 2016, and in 2020 it expanded to 49 cities [official wording]. By the end of 2025 the number covered had passed 300 million [official wording: Ministry of Civil Affairs and National Office on Aging, "2025 National Bulletin on the Development of Aging-Related Undertakings"]. In March 2026 the General Office of the CPC Central Committee and the General Office of the State Council issued "Opinions on Accelerating the Establishment of the Long-Term Care Insurance System": within about 3 years, by the end of 2028, near-universal coverage nationwide, with the contribution rate unified at about 0.3% [official wording].
- **Where this comes from**: A | The timeline, the coverage numbers, the contribution rate and the benefit rules all come from official documents (National Healthcare Security Administration, 2025-09-25; Ministry of Civil Affairs and National Office on Aging, "2025 National Bulletin on the Development of Aging-Related Undertakings"; General Office of the CPC Central Committee and General Office of the State Council, 2026-03-25).
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: people already in a city that runs LTCI. **Benefits differ from place to place**: the national catalogue of service items was only issued in 2025, and the pilot cities will take about 3 more years to align [official wording]. Another city's rates and services don't necessarily hold where you are.
- **What you get back**: money + relationships — no longer afraid that "one person becomes disabled, the whole family falls apart, and the carer's bills drag the household under"
- **How this works in China**: where to ask — the medical insurance bureau where you're enrolled, a medical insurance service window, or the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app. Ask three questions: "Does our place run LTCI? How do I apply for a disability assessment? Which services are covered, and how much?" The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.5 What runs out first when you get old is relationships

- **What it costs**: a little time, one honest look at one thing (money: none | time: one round of asking yourself | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: when the money runs out there is still the state underneath you; when relationships break there is no one to make it up for you. A partner, children, old friends, neighbours, former colleagues, and a family doctor — these six groups are the real "capital" you can draw on on the day you become disabled. That list never grows by itself; it only shrinks.
- Why treat it as an account: loneliness is not just "being in a bad mood". A WHO report says about 1 in 6 people worldwide is affected by loneliness, and loneliness is linked to about 871,000 deaths a year [official wording]. A meta-analysis pooling data on several hundred thousand people found that people with strong social relationships have a survival rate about 50% higher than those with weak ones — the same order as quitting smoking or controlling weight [third-party research].
- **What you can do**: the ones on this list that genuinely depreciate and need you to top up are the four below.
- **Where this comes from**: B | The link between loneliness and health and death risk is supported by a large body of cohort studies; "social connection" is now a WHO public-health theme. **But** correlation is not causation, and intervention studies on "whether keeping up social ties extends life" are still inconsistent. If you start now, I don't promise you more years; what it protects is this: on the day you need someone, you can still call them out.
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: living alone, empty-nest, and people whose social circle shrank sharply after retirement matter most. If your social life is already full, don't stake all your relationships on one group, say only on former colleagues.
- **What you get back**: relationships — no longer afraid that "living alone and empty-nested, no one would know, and one old friend after another is gone"
- **How this works in China**: at the community level, the places you can use include the residents' committee, the sub-district elderly care service centre, the community elderly care service station, and the university of the third age; what exactly they offer and how to join, one visit and you'll know.

Relationships slip away; you can't stop it. All you can do is slow it down.

## 4.6 The old-friend relationship needs its own top-up

- **What it costs**: one phone call and one meeting a month (money: costs almost nothing | time: a few hours a month | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: colleague relationships are "fed" by the work unit; the moment you retire, the power cuts. Old-friend relationships you have to top up yourself. The older you get, the fewer people you can really talk to, and the few left are worth a lot.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Pick 3 to 5 old friends you can really talk to and fix a set day to meet or call. "Let's get together when we can" means never getting together.
  - ② Phone calls work better than messages — at least one a week.
  - ③ Find something you do together: walking, chess, fishing, strolling in the park all work; a shared activity is what holds up over time.
  - ④ Don't keep it only in a chat app. Put their phone number and which neighbourhood they live in into your phone's contacts, so that if you lose your phone or can't get back into the chat app, you can still reach them.
- **Where this comes from**: C | This is common practice and compiled experience; there is no randomised "old-friend maintenance trial" in older people. **But** the link between social participation and mood and cognition is supported by cohort studies — see the previous item.
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: people who can still get out and are still up for meeting. If you have trouble getting around or live far away, swap "meeting" for a set call and video. One day an old friend will need you to visit them too; relationships run both ways.
- **What you get back**: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid that "after retirement no one looks me up any more"
- **How this works in China**: community university of the third age, day-care centre, seniors' activity room, tai-chi group, square-dance team, cards room. Go to the community residents' committee or the community elderly care service station and ask: "What activities for older people are there here, and how do I join?" The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.7 Get to know three neighbours first — in a crisis they're closer than your children

- **What it costs**: a few nods, one phone call (money: none | time: a few greetings | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: however devoted your children are, they're a city away. When something really happens — a fall, a sudden illness, a leak, an unlit stove — the one who can knock on your door within minutes is a neighbour. This account is unremarkable day to day and most expensive when something goes wrong.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Start with the people across the hall and two or three households above and below you; know their surnames.
  - ② Leave each other a phone number, not just a chat app.
  - ③ Set a "little signal": curtains open in the morning, lights on at night means all is well; a day with no movement, and you knock.
  - ④ You be the one who knocks too. Take in a neighbour's delivery, lend a hand — relationships are saved up, not handed to you.
- **Where this comes from**: C | Neighbourly mutual help is common practice; eldercare in China has long leaned on this informal network of family and neighbours. **But** not every community has usable neighbourly ties, and new complexes and places with lots of renters are weaker.
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: living alone, empty-nest, and people in older complexes (where neighbours still know each other) find it easier. If you live in a new complex with many renters, the more realistic route is to build it through the community, property management, or the building head.
- **What you get back**: relationships + self-reliance — no longer afraid that "I'll have an accident alone at night and no one will know"
- **How this works in China**: the residents' committee, the building head, the grid worker, property management. Many cities are running visits to seniors living alone and "door-knock" drives; ask the community whether there is one. The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.8 After retirement, rewire the colleague relationship

- **What it costs**: one round of tidying up your contacts (money: none | time: one-off | effort: none | do you have to ask: you do have to bring it up, and it's a bit awkward — you fear being seen as "still angling for connections")
- **In short**: on the day you retire, half your social identity goes. Colleagues you saw every day turn into "former colleagues" overnight. This isn't fickleness; it's that the shared thing is gone. If you want to keep them, wire a new shared thing in.
- **What you can do**:
  - ① Around retirement, save the contact details of 3 to 5 colleagues you still want to be in touch with; save the phone numbers, don't leave them only in the work group chat.
  - ② Find something tied to your old trade but not to a time clock: re-employment, mentoring an apprentice, a hobby group, an industry old-friends group — let the identity continue in another form.
  - ③ The former employer's events, the retirees' branch, or the retired-staff management office — go once and see if you fit.
- **Where this comes from**: C | Compiled experience. "Identity and social-circle contraction after retirement" is a phenomenon geriatric sociology discusses over and over; there is no trial support.
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: people who spent a lifetime in a work unit, a government office, or a school find it most pronounced. If you're self-employed, a sole trader, or a farmer, there was no "workplace relationship" to break anyway — for you this item becomes peers, old customers, people from your home town.
- **What you get back**: relationships + dignity — no longer afraid that "after retirement I have no identity and have been left behind by the times"
- **How this works in China**: the former employer's retired-staff management office, the labour union; an industry old-colleagues group; the university of the third age; some places run re-employment and re-hiring programmes for older people. Go to your former employer's HR and ask what activities there are for retirees. The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.9 Sign up for a family doctor in advance, and bank "who to see when I'm ill"

- **What it costs**: one signing, then on demand (money: in most places the basic package is free or low-cost | time: half an hour to sign | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy to bring up)
- **In short**: when you feel unwell at midnight, what panics you most is usually not knowing who to turn to, with no one knowing your background. Signing up for a family doctor is like banking in advance a doctor who "knows you".
- How to sign up: ① Go to the community health centre or township health centre where you live. ② You can sign with a team or with an individual — allowed under a 2022 policy. ③ The agreement can run 1 to 3 years, renewable on expiry [official wording]. ④ Bring your ID card and medical insurance card, and ask clearly about the service list and the fee.
- What it covers: diagnosis and treatment of common and frequently occurring illnesses, and chronic-disease management and follow-up; a referral through your family doctor gives you priority for consultation, tests, and hospital admission; for people with limited mobility or dementia, home visits and a home hospital bed; for eligible chronic-disease patients, a long-term prescription of 4 to 12 weeks [official wording].
- Is it worth it: yes, especially for people with chronic illness, limited mobility, or living alone — it's your "front door" into the hospital. **But**: the competence of family doctors and the experience after signing vary a lot from place to place, and some places are criticised for being "signed but not served". Before signing, ask: how do I contact this team, how often for chronic-disease follow-up, and how does a referral work?
- **Where this comes from**: A | The signing arrangements, the service content, referrals, and long-term prescriptions are all written in national documents (Guoweijicengfa [2022] No. 10). The goal is for contracted-service coverage to reach more than 75% by 2035, and more than 85% of key groups [official wording].
- Who this is for: **Who it applies to**: most worth it for people with chronic illness, limited mobility, or living alone. If you're in good health and always see specialists at a big hospital, the benefit is mainly convenient chronic-disease follow-up and referral. If you're already disabled or have dementia, use it mainly for home visits and a home hospital bed.
- **What you get back**: self-reliance + relationships — no longer afraid that "no one goes to the doctor with me, and I don't know who to call"
- **How this works in China**: where to sign — the community health centre or township health centre where you live. Ask three questions: "Who am I signing with? What does it cover? How much does it cost?" For reimbursement when seeing a doctor away from home, ask the medical insurance bureau where you're enrolled, or use the "National Medical Insurance Service Platform" app. The latest local wording prevails.

## 4.10 The one thing you can do today

Pick up the phone and call the one old friend you most want to keep, and settle on a fixed day right there: next Wednesday at three in the afternoon, a walk together, or a pot of tea.

You can look up your pension any time; that account won't vanish overnight. Relationships are different — they depreciate every day, and today's phone call is the first deposit you make into it.

## 4.11 If you are reading this for a parent

Every "do it for them" in this chapter works best when it becomes "do it together with them".

**How to say it**: don't say "I'll look up your pension for you" — say "I've been trying to work out these tiers myself lately and can't get my head round it; help me pull up your contribution record so I can learn from it." Swap "I'll do it for you" for "teach me through it once", and they'll go along. Same idea for asking a neighbour or a family doctor — let them watch you ask.

**What you can still do when they refuse**: some things don't need their nod. ① You look up their contribution years for them using the "Zhangshen 12333" app or the "electronic social security card". ② Go to the social insurance bureau where they're enrolled and ask how lapses and transfers are handled. ③ Go to their community and ask one question: is LTCI running, and have they signed up a family doctor? ④ If you're not often by their side, help them get to know their neighbours — when you're not there, the neighbours are. These four don't need their agreement; do what you can first.

## 4.12 What I am not sure about in this chapter

- "Basic old-age insurance covers 1.076 billion people nationwide" has been traced back to MOHRSS's original release (the "2025 Statistical Bulletin on the Development of Human Resources and Social Security" and MOHRSS's routine press conference of 2026-01-27), and no longer rests on second-hand commentary. "Over 150 million private-pension accounts opened" could not be pinned to an original MOHRSS figure; the only official number is the "cumulative contributions of 145.2 billion yuan" in the "2025 National Bulletin on the Development of Aging-Related Undertakings". The account figures come from several financial media relaying MOHRSS's figures (about 150 million, 2025); when you use them, go by the latest official wording.
- LTCI coverage moves forward with time and the figures are not fully consistent: the National Healthcare Security Administration said on 2025-09-25 that it covered 190 million people with cumulative spending above 85 billion yuan; Xinhua and the aging bulletin, by the end of 2025, put it at about 300 million. This chapter uses the latter. Implementation timing and benefits differ a lot from place to place; go by the medical insurance bureau where you're enrolled.
- The link between loneliness and death risk is supported by cohort studies, but on "whether keeping up relationships from now on can extend life" there is a lack of intervention studies targeting older Chinese people.
