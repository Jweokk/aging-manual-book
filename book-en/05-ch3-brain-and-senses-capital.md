# Chapter 3 · Brain and Senses Capital

The TV volume goes up a notch every year. The family says it's too loud; to you it sounds just right. At the market the vendor has to say it twice before you catch it, and you wave your hand and say, "that's just what getting old is like."

This chapter covers five things: brain, ears, eyes, teeth, feet. They're like five switches in a row on the wall — when one comes loose, it's never only that one circuit that goes bad. If your ears can't hear clearly, you pull back from the dinner table, the card game, the people around you. If your teeth can't bite, you eat less meat and vegetables. If your feet hurt, you can't be bothered to go out the door.

## 3.1 What this chapter is for

As you age, the first thing to come loose is often not your legs — it's these five. They look as if each runs its own business, but in fact one leads into the next.

Being hard of hearing, blurry eyes, bad teeth, foot trouble — you tend to write these off as "normal for old age." Some of them can be stopped early; and some changes are your body telling you to go to the hospital that very day, not to wait until next month.

This chapter looks at three things: what can be fixed ahead of time, whether fixing it is worth it, and which kind of change cannot wait.

## 3.2 Hearing loss and dementia share a hard link

Research shows the link between hearing decline and dementia is solid; but how far hearing loss itself causes dementia is not fully settled.

The Lancet Commission on dementia prevention's 2024 report lists 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia, and hearing loss is one of them. The report models this with population-attributable fractions: push all these factors down, and in theory you could delay or stop nearly half of dementia — that's a modelled expectation, not an observed drop in incidence ([third-party research: Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, 2024 report]). The World Health Organization also states that untreated hearing loss raises the risk of dementia and speeds cognitive decline ([official wording: WHO *Deafness and hearing loss* fact sheet]). The number-one cause of hearing disability in China is age-related hearing loss, at 51.61% ([third-party research: *China Hearing Health Report (2021)* blue book, using data from the second national sampling survey of disabled persons in 2006]).

One distinction to keep clear: correlation is not causation. People with poor hearing have more dementia — that is observed over and over; but the report itself writes that some of these associations "may only be partly causal."

So does managing it early lower the risk? The hardest evidence is the ACHIEVE trial (2023, *The Lancet*): 977 people aged 70 to 84 with untreated hearing loss, split into a group given hearing aids plus hearing rehabilitation and a group given only health education, followed for 3 years ([third-party research: Lin FR et al., Lancet 2023]). Taken together, hearing aids did not clearly slow cognitive decline; among the subset at higher cognitive risk, decline was 48% slower.

One more point to flag: a few years ago an observational study said "hearing aids can lower dementia risk" and it was passed around everywhere; the paper was later retracted by *The Lancet Public Health* ([third-party research: retraction notice, 2023-12]). Don't treat "hearing aids prevent dementia" as settled.

Are hearing aids worth it? The first thing they buy back is "being able to hear, to chat, not to be walled off from the sound of people talking" — that alone is worth it; and the dementia benefit, if any, most likely lands on people who already carry other risk factors (the group in ACHIEVE).

## 3.3 Get your hearing tested once first, and get hearing aids if you need them

- **What it costs**: a pure-tone audiometry test runs a few dozen to one or two hundred yuan (varies by place); a hearing aid runs several hundred to several thousand or over ten thousand yuan a pair (money: not cheap | effort: half a day for the test, fitting takes several trips | strength: not tiring | do you have to speak up?: the hardest step — this is where many people get stuck)
- **In short**: hearing mostly declines slowly, hard to notice yourself, but the family notices first. Get tested once, get a chart, and then you have something to go on.
- **What you can do**: ① register with otolaryngology (ENT) and say you "want to check your hearing," and get pure-tone audiometry; ② have the doctor sort out which type it is — some cases are not aging but middle-ear infection, earwax blockage, or sudden deafness, and need handling separately; ③ when it really reaches the point where you need one, get fitted at a proper hospital hearing centre or a fitting facility with the right qualifications — don't just buy one online; ④ after fitting you need to go back for adjustment and practise using it — discomfort in the first month is common.
- **Where this comes from**: B | pure-tone audiometry with proper fitting is standard practice; but the evidence for "hearing aids prevent dementia" only goes as far as "people at higher risk may benefit" — don't treat it as insurance.
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: people who can hear but can't make out speech, and whose family keep saying you're hard of hearing. If your ear canal is draining pus, you have ear pain, or one side suddenly goes deaf, see 3.8 and go to hospital the same day.
- **What you get back**: relationships + self-care — no more fearing "being walled off from the sound of people talking, until eventually nobody talks to me"
- **How this works in China**: the free annual health management for people aged 65 and over includes a rough hearing check ([official wording: National Basic Public Health Service Standard, 3rd edition]); for precise hearing testing and hearing-aid fitting, go to the ENT department of a tier-2 or higher hospital. Hearing aids mostly go through the Disabled Persons' Federation's assistive-device fitting subsidy — see 3.9.

## 3.4 Once cataract affects your daily life, stop putting it off

- **What it costs**: one outpatient visit plus one operation; the surgical fee is reimbursed against the local medical-insurance catalogue, and the out-of-pocket share varies by place (effort: mostly day surgery or outpatient surgery | strength: not tiring | do you have to speak up?: easy to ask)
- **In short**: cataract is the world's number-one cause of blindness, and the most common cause of vision loss in older people. It can't be treated with pills or eye drops — the clouded lens has to be removed and replaced with an artificial lens.
- **When to do it**: not when it "ripens." The National Health Commission's 2026 operational standard says: when the visual function lost to the lens no longer meets the needs of daily life and work, and surgery offers a chance of improvement, it can be done ([official wording: NHC *Operational Standard for Adult Cataract Surgery (2026 edition)*]). Not being able to see the road and being afraid to step down a stair — you've reached that point.
- **What you can do**: ① register with ophthalmology; check visual acuity, slit lamp and eye pressure, and check the fundus if needed; ② ask the doctor three questions: is it appropriate to do it now, which eye, and whether blood pressure and blood sugar need controlling first; ③ if you have high blood pressure or diabetes, get blood pressure and blood sugar steady before surgery, as the doctor says; ④ go back for follow-up after surgery as instructed.
- **Where this comes from**: A | surgery is the only treatment for cataract; whether you've reached the point for it and how to do it are decided by the ophthalmologist together with your general condition.
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: people diagnosed with cataract whose vision is declining. A sudden change in vision, or severe eye pain with redness, may be a different emergency — see 3.8 first.
- **What you get back**: self-care + dignity — no more fearing "not seeing the road clearly, afraid to go out, afraid of falling"
- **How this works in China**: cataract surgery is a diagnosis-and-treatment item covered by medical insurance; how much is reimbursed, whether it can be done as an outpatient, and whether the artificial lens is chosen from inside or outside the catalogue all vary by place — go by the local medical-insurance scheme's latest wording. Where to ask: the hospital's medical-insurance office, or the local medical-insurance administration.

## 3.5 When your teeth can't bite, it runs all the way to nutrition and the dinner table

- **What it costs**: one dental check-up; fillings, dentures and implants differ widely in price, from a few hundred to over ten thousand (money: not cheap | effort: several trips | strength: not tiring | do you have to speak up?: easy to ask)
- **In short**: once teeth are missing, you can't chew meat, vegetables or nuts, so you pick only soft things to eat, until your diet grows narrower and narrower. Worse, you start hiding from people when you smile and talk.
- **The current numbers**: in the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey, people aged 65 to 74 had on average only 22.5 teeth left, and 36.8% of those missing teeth had not been restored ([official wording: Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey, released by the National Health and Family Planning Commission on 2017-09-20; the figure of 22.5 retained teeth is on the release page, while the 36.8% missing-teeth-not-restored figure is survey detail, second-hand from *China Population News*, 2024-10-24 — see 3.13]).
- **What you can do**: ① see a dentist every six months to a year, don't wait for pain; ② if you have calculus or bleeding gums, get periodontal treatment first, to save the teeth you still have; ③ restore missing teeth promptly: fix them in place if you can; if many are missing and your health is average, a removable denture is also a proper option, and a full denture needs time to adapt and several adjustments ([official wording: Chinese Stomatological Association, *Guidelines for Oral Restoration in Elderly Patients* T/CHSA 001-2021]); ④ brush twice a day and use floss; take dentures out daily to clean them, and don't sleep with them in.
- **Where this comes from**: B | timely restoration, periodontal maintenance and regular denture review are standard in oral-health guidelines; which type of restoration to choose is decided by the dentist together with your general condition and your wishes.
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: people starting to lose teeth, who can't chew, whose dentures are loose. If you've had a cardiac stent, take anticoagulants, have used bisphosphonate drugs, or have poorly controlled blood sugar, you must tell the dentist before extraction or an implant.
- **What you get back**: self-care + relationships + dignity — no more fearing "not being able to eat, eating badly, and not daring to open my mouth to smile"
- **How this works in China**: the medical-insurance rules for dentistry vary by place; generally extractions, fillings and periodontal treatment are partly reimbursed, while dentures and implants are mostly out-of-pocket — go by the local medical-insurance catalogue and the hospital you attend. Whom to see: the dental department of a general hospital, a dental hospital, or a prosthodontics department; if your community health centre has a dental department, ask there first. Where to ask about reimbursement: the hospital's medical-insurance office, or the local medical-insurance administration.

## 3.6 Not being able to cut your toenails is a different matter from diabetic foot

- **What it costs**: a suitable pair of nail clippers; or one foot-care session, a few dozen yuan (money: costs almost nothing | effort: low | strength: not tiring | do you have to speak up?: easy to ask)
- **In short**: as you age, your eyes blur, your back can't bend, your hands can't reach your feet, and cutting your toenails gets hard. Cut into the flesh and it gets infected and fills with pus. If you have diabetes, that one cut can turn into big trouble.
- **If you cut them yourself**: ① use large nail clippers with a straight blade; ② cut straight across, leave a little white edge, don't dig the corners in at the sides; ③ soak them soft first, use good light, sit steady, don't perch on one foot; ④ if you can't reach or your hands shake, switch to long-handled tools or have a family member cut them, and if it really comes to it, go to a foot-care clinic at a community or hospital service.
- **If you have diabetes, remember one more thing**: your feet are not the same as other people's feet. The *Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetic Foot (2019 edition)* state it clearly: diabetic foot is destruction of the skin and deep tissue below the ankle, often with infection and vascular disease; ingrown nails, blisters, bleeding and fungal infections are all its precursor lesions; trimming toenails, nail avulsion and ingrown-nail removal must all be done by trained medical staff, not at a bathhouse or a nail salon ([official wording: Chinese Diabetes Society, *Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetic Foot (2019 edition)*]). The same task that's a minor nuisance for an ordinary person can, for you, run all the way to ulceration and gangrene.
- **Shoes and socks**: shoes should fit, support the foot and not squeeze at the front; don't go out in open-toed slippers; choose light-coloured cotton socks so a break shows up; check your feet once a day, including the soles and between the toes.
- **Where this comes from**: A | the diabetic-foot guideline makes "check your feet daily" and "toenails and calluses handled by professionals" explicit recommendations; for ordinary people, cutting as above carries little risk, but there is no randomised trial specifically on "how to cut your toenails."
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: people who can't reach or cut them well; people with diabetes, lower-limb vascular problems, deformed feet or numb feet should not do it themselves — go to endocrinology or a foot clinic. If there's a break in the foot, blackening, redness, swelling, heat, pain, or fever, go to hospital the same day.
- **What you get back**: self-care — no more fearing "a small wound on the foot that ends up costing me the leg"
- **How this works in China**: high-risk diabetic feet need regular foot checks per the guideline, more often the more severe. Where to ask: a community health centre or a hospital endocrinology department.

## 3.7 Brain capital is saved day by day: move your body, use your mind, control blood pressure and blood sugar, sleep well, have people to talk to

- **What it costs**: ten-plus minutes of movement a day; the rest are mostly habits that cost nothing (money: costs almost nothing | effort: daily | strength: not too tiring | do you have to speak up?: no need to speak up)
- **In short**: there's no drug that lets you deposit into this "brain capital" all at once — it comes from a string of ordinary things: move your body, use your mind, keep blood pressure and blood sugar under control, sleep well, have people to talk to, and don't leave your hearing and eyesight unattended.
- **How to do it** (pick one you can start today):
  - **Move your body**: brisk walking, cycling, dancing, tai chi all work — accumulate enough each week, don't sit all day (see Chapter 13).
  - **Use your mind**: read books and papers, play chess, learn something new — this works better than repeating things you already know.
  - **Control blood pressure, blood sugar and blood lipids**: take your medicine and check your blood pressure as the doctor says, don't stop on your own. This is the most solid evidence, and high blood pressure is among those 14 factors.
  - **Sleep well**: keep a fixed wake-up time; if you snore loudly, feel sleepy all day, or wake up choking at night, get checked once for sleep apnoea. (Sleep is not among those 14 factors; the evidence is weaker.)
  - **Have people to talk to**: eat, chat and do things together with people often. Being all alone is also among those 14 factors.
- **Where this comes from**: B | that managing several factors together can lower dementia risk is the core conclusion of this report; but which one helps you most, and by how much, can't be guaranteed.
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: people not yet diagnosed with dementia who want to push it off. If you're already clearly forgetting things, or suspect a brain problem, see a doctor first (Chapter 9).
- **What you get back**: self-care + relationships — no more fearing "my mind running shorter every day, and in the end not knowing anyone"
- **How this works in China**: communities, universities of the third age and senior activity centres often have ready-made classes and teams (tai chi, choir, walking groups). Where to ask: a community health centre, or a university of the third age.

## 3.8 These changes are signals to go to hospital the same day

- **What it costs**: one trip out, possibly to the emergency department (money: the cost of one registration | effort: same day | strength: not tiring | do you have to speak up?: easy to ask)
- **In short**: some changes in hearing and eyesight are not "slow aging" but emergencies: sudden, one-sided, or with dizziness and unsteadiness. Wait a night, and you may lose something that could have been saved.
- **Go the same day**:
  - One ear suddenly can't hear clearly, or suddenly goes deaf. This may be sudden deafness — sensorineural hearing loss coming on suddenly within 72 hours, an ENT emergency where the earlier the treatment the better ([official wording: Chinese Medical Association, *Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Sudden Deafness (2015)*]).
  - One eye suddenly goes blind, or a black cloth seems to block your vision, or there's a sudden burst of flashes and a marked increase in floaters.
  - An eye suddenly becomes severely painful and red, with vision declining at the same time.
  - A change in hearing or vision together with dizziness, unsteady walking, slurred speech or weakness on one side of the body — treat it as a stroke and call 120 immediately (see Chapter 8 and Appendix D).
- **Where this comes from**: A | the treatment window for sudden deafness and the emergency nature of sudden blindness in one eye are both written in the guidelines; remember "sudden, one-sided, with dizziness."
- **Who this is for**: **who it applies to**: everyone. Don't put in a couple of eye drops or dig at your ear yourself and wait for tomorrow. Changes that come on slowly, over months — handle them per the sections above; the moment the word "sudden" appears, treat it as an emergency.
- **What you get back**: self-care — no more fearing "something that could have been saved, and I dragged past it"
- **How this works in China**: for sudden deafness, register with ENT — some hospitals can add you the same day; for eye emergencies, register with the ophthalmology emergency service; for slurred speech or weakness on one side, call 120. If you're not sure, go to the hospital's emergency-department triage first.

## 3.9 Where to get these things done in China

- **Rough hearing and vision checks in the free health check-up**: people aged 65 and over who have lived in the community for at least half a year get one check a year, done at a community health centre, a township health centre or a village clinic; the physical examination includes rough checks of mouth, vision and hearing ([official wording: National Basic Public Health Service Standard, 3rd edition, "Elderly Health Management"]). This is a rough check, not a diagnosis; if problems are found, you're referred to a higher-level hospital to investigate.
- **Rehabilitation assistive-device fitting**: basic assistive devices such as hearing aids and walking aids go through the Disabled Persons' Federation's "assistive-device fitting subsidy." All 31 provinces (autonomous regions and municipalities) and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps have issued policies ([official wording: China Disabled Persons' Federation / Xinhua News Agency, 2024-02]). In most provinces the target group is local certificate-holding disabled persons and eligible disabled children. How much is subsidised, whether you need a disability certificate first, and where to apply all vary by place — go by the local latest wording. Where to ask: the Disabled Persons' Federation at the community (village) or township (sub-district) level, or the county (district) Disabled Persons' Federation.
- **Medical-insurance reimbursement**: whether cataract surgery, dental treatment and diabetic-foot treatment are reimbursed, and how much, depends on the local catalogue and the hospital's tier. Where to ask: the hospital's medical-insurance office, or the local medical-insurance administration.

One honest word: of these five, some will keep getting worse all the way. Ears can be partly made up for with hearing aids, teeth can be partly made up for with restoration, and the brain and blood vessels can only be managed bit by bit, day by day. What this chapter can do is stop what can be stopped early, and get the same-day things done the same day.

## 3.10 The one thing you can do today

Remember roughly what notch the TV volume is on at home now, and look again next month. The volume creeping up one notch at a time is one of the easiest signals to miss after midlife.

Then, go for a pure-tone audiometry test.

## 3.11 If you are reading this for a parent

**How to say it out loud**: don't say "you should get a hearing aid" at the moment when he can't hear you and you've repeated the same sentence a third time — that's pointing it out to his face. Try another way, putting yourself into it: "My hearing's been off lately too — I'm going to get it checked, come with me?" After the test, mention his situation to the doctor in passing. Or leave the hearing aid alone for now and just say "the TV's too loud, the neighbours will complain soon" — giving him a way to save face about turning it down.

**What you can still do when he disagrees**: what usually trips older people up is "face" and "what's the use." There are three things you can do without his agreement. First, book the appointment and fix the time for him, turning "whether to go" into "which day to go." Second, help him find out how the local Disabled Persons' Federation's assistive-device subsidy works (see 3.9) — lower that money hurdle and the resistance drops too. Third, whether he's willing to wear it or not, start with the "being able to hear" situations: set him up with wireless headphones for the TV, put phone calls on speaker, seat him with his back to the wall at the dinner table and face him when you talk. Once he hears a little better, he'll know for himself that the thing is useful.

## 3.12 What I am not sure about in this chapter

The items in this chapter that were marked for verification have all been traced back to source where possible: the two oral-health figures go back to the survey release page and *China Population News*; the Lancet and hearing-aid passage goes back to the full report and the ACHIEVE paper; the Disabled Persons' Federation assistive-device subsidy goes back to the Xinhua piece on the Chinese government website. The remaining two are limits of the data itself, not things I failed to find, and I leave them as they are:

- The randomised evidence on hearing aids and dementia is currently mainly just one trial, ACHIEVE, with 3-year follow-up; whether they can really delay dementia over the long term is still unanswered. The observational study a few years ago that said "hearing aids lower dementia risk" has been retracted by *The Lancet Public Health* and can no longer be used as a basis.
- Among the causes of hearing disability in China, age-related deafness accounts for 51.61%, from the second national sampling survey of disabled persons in 2006; *China Hearing Health Report (2021)* was still using this older data when it was published in 2021, and there has been no new round of national sampling since, so this proportion is on the old side.
