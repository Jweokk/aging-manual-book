# Chapter 9 · Forgetting: how far from dementia

You are clutching your keys, hunting through every room for your keys. Then you find them — you had put them down in the wrong place. This happens to people of forty and to people of eighty.

It may have happened to your mother. What's different is that she burns the rice, leaves a sentence half said and hanging in the air, and can no longer think of something she set down a moment ago. The question in your mind you don't dare ask out loud: is this old age, or is this illness.

This chapter does not answer "is she demented." It answers one thing only: **when should you go and get it checked.**

## 9.1 What this chapter is for

Normal old age and dementia have a wide stretch of ground between them. Forgetting where you put the keys happens to nearly everyone; forgetting what keys are for, so that you can't open the door of your own home, is another matter.

At one end, carrying everything off as "he's old, it's normal" means missing the years when something could still be done. At the other end, two searches online turn you into a patient in your own mind. There is still no drug that turns this disease around, so this chapter gives you one thing only: a ruler.

## 9.2 Forgetting where the keys are and forgetting how keys work are two different things

- **What it costs**: nothing, just your eyes and your memory (money: nothing to pay | time: as you go about your day | effort: none | do you have to bring it up? easy)
- **In short**: Memory and function are two different things. In normal aging, what is forgotten is "things": where the keys went, what you were about to say — a reminder brings it back. Dementia eats "function": you can't use a key to open a door, you can't make a phone call, you can't work out the change from the grocery shopping, and a reminder doesn't bring it back.
- **What you can do**: Check him against these three; the more that match, the more it is worth getting checked. ① Things or function: is what's forgotten a "thing" (which can be filled back in), or "function" (something he could do and now can't)? This is the dividing line. ② Repetition: the same question asked several times, the same story told several times, insisting that things have been taken by someone. ③ The person has changed: someone who loved company stops going out, talks less and less, turns suspicious and quick to anger.
- **Where this comes from**: B | The line between dementia and ordinary forgetfulness is "it affects daily life"; clinically, the criterion for mild cognitive impairment is that the more complicated everyday tasks (managing money, shopping, going out to run errands) go wrong, while eating, bathing and using the toilet can still be managed [third-party research].
- **Who this is for**: It holds for older people who can still get about on their own and are still living with family. For wheelchair users and people who have been bedbound for a long time, the "function" line is drawn differently and the focus of judgment has to change.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "I can still tell the difference, I just don't dare think the worst."
- **How this works in China**: These three can be checked at home, with no equipment at all; if none of them match and you are still uneasy, book one outpatient visit as in 9.6.

## 9.3 The first to notice something is wrong is usually the family, not a scale

- **What it costs**: paying attention as you go about your day, plus one sheet of paper you can write on (money: nothing to pay | time: a few minutes each time | effort: none | do you have to bring it up? easy)
- **In short**: Ask him to his face "is your memory getting worse" and you mostly won't get the truth. Either he won't admit it, or he can't explain it himself either. The first to see that something is wrong is the person who lives with him day after day.
- **What you can do**: Don't write down one line, "bad memory." Write down three concrete things: ① things he used to be able to do and now can't (does he still remember to pay the bills, can he use the washing machine, can he make an appointment for himself); ② repetition (how many times the same question, how many times the same story); ③ the person has changed (someone who loved company no longer comes out, talks less and less). For each, write down: when it started, how often, who noticed. This sheet of paper is a clue for the doctor.
- **Where this comes from**: B | The 2018 Chinese guideline put "report by the patient or by a knowledgeable informant" into the diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment (grade A recommendation); the informant questionnaire (IQCODE) tells normal older people apart from mild cognitive impairment with an accuracy of about 79.9% [third-party research].
- **Who this is for**: It holds for older people and families who still live together. **For someone living alone**, the first to notice is often not family but a community follow-up visit, a neighbor, or an accident (a fall, wandering off).
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "by the time I notice, it's already too late."
- **How this works in China**: Write these three onto one page and hand it to the doctor at the next accompanied visit; it works better than saying on the spot "my mother's memory is bad."

## 9.4 "It's just old age, normal" may be blocking a batch of diseases that can be treated

- **What it costs**: one outpatient visit, plus a few tubes of blood (money: the price of one registration | time: half a day | effort: none | do you have to bring it up? easy)
- **In short**: There is a batch of cases that look like dementia but come from other causes, and when the cause is removed the person may well recover. Treat it as "old age" and you never get the chance to check.
- **What you can do**: Take this list along and ask the doctor, "have these been checked?"
  - **Hypothyroidism**: intolerant of cold, slow to react.
  - **Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency**: often with anemia, a sore tongue, numb hands and feet.
  - **Abnormal blood sugar or electrolytes (sodium, calcium)**.
  - **Depression**: in someone whose mood is low, memory and reactions both slide; when the depression is treated, cognition often comes back with it.
  - **Infection, abnormal liver or kidney function**.
  - **Drug side effects**: sleeping pills, some antihistamines, antidepressants, antipsychotics.
  - **Normal-pressure hydrocephalus, subdural hematoma**: growing muddled slowly after a blow to the head — think of these two.
- **Where this comes from**: B | Professional manuals and clinical guidelines all list these as potentially reversible causes of dementia-like symptoms, of which depression, thyroid, B12 deficiency and drugs can be treated or adjusted. **But** reversible does not mean it will necessarily reverse: a disease of brain degeneration does not turn around because one tablet was added. And "mild cognitive impairment" itself does not always lead on to dementia: in some studies about a quarter to 30% of people go back to normal cognition, though the studies differ widely [third-party research].
- **Who this is for**: It holds most of all for people who have only recently become muddled, whose change is fairly fast, or who have a clear trigger (a new drug, a blow to the head). People who have been sliding slowly for years with no trigger should not skip this step either.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "it could have been treated, and one word, 'old age,' cost us that."
- **How this works in China**: When you register, say "his memory is getting worse and I want to rule out the treatable causes." The doctor will usually order a complete blood count, thyroid function, blood sugar, liver and kidney function, vitamin B12; most of it is covered by medical insurance (*yibao*).

## 9.5 Checking early does not buy you a cure, it buys you the extra years

- **What it costs**: the time for one assessment (money: registration plus tests | time: half a day | effort: none)
- **In short**: The value of checking early is not in "curing" — it is in four concrete things, and all of them start with knowing early: getting onto medicine sooner, arranging care sooner, deciding the things that are yours to decide yourself.
- **What you can do**: ① **Medication**: in the early stage (mild cognitive impairment, and mild to moderate dementia) some drugs help a little, slowing the drop in function; whether to take them and which one is for the memory clinic or the neurologist to judge. ② **Non-drug measures**: moving, keeping blood pressure and blood sugar under control, getting hearing and vision fitted, keeping up social contact and using your brain — these are written into standard dementia-prevention practice, mild in effect but real. ③ **Care arrangements**: while you are still clear-headed, you can decide for yourself whether to go into a facility, who lends a hand, how the work is divided at home. ④ **Taking stock of money and authority**: accounts, the flat, insurance, who manages the money — sort it out while you can still explain it.
- **Where this comes from**: B | The World Health Organization's position is that dementia cannot be cured, but drug and non-drug interventions can help maintain life and function; doctors at public hospitals say the same, that intervening early at the mild cognitive impairment stage can delay the onset. **But** the drugs have only a limited effect on mild cognitive impairment itself.
- **Who this is for**: It holds for people who can still decide things and speak for themselves. For someone who can no longer say what he wants, this step can only rest on documents prepared ahead of time (designated guardianship, an advance care plan — see Chapter 22).
- **What you get back**: Dignity + money — no longer afraid of "in the end everyone else decides for me."
- **How this works in China**: See 9.10; for how to write a designated guardianship and advance care instructions, see Chapter 22.

## 9.6 Go to a memory clinic, do the four kinds of test, and don't take a checkup package for a diagnosis

- **What it costs**: one to several outpatient visits (money: registration plus tests | time: half a day each | effort: none)
- **In short**: Memory problems belong to the memory clinic, neurology, geriatrics, psychiatry (psychology). The doctor puts the history, the scales, the blood and the imaging together to judge — it is not one question on a test and then a verdict.
- **What you can do**: Register with the right department, then match the kinds of test to it. ① **History and scales**: the doctor asks the person and also asks the family, uses a cognitive scale as a baseline, then tests again after a while to see the difference. ② **Blood**: the treatable causes in 9.4. ③ **Imaging**: usually one brain MRI, to look for atrophy, infarction, hydrocephalus, bleeding. ④ **Added as needed**: cerebrospinal fluid, brain PET, genetics — only when the doctor thinks it necessary and it would change the judgment.
- **Where this comes from**: B | The 2018 Chinese guideline recommends a structural brain MRI for patients with mild cognitive impairment at their first visit (grade A recommendation), with blood tests as in 9.4; a brief scale alone is not sensitive enough, and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) screens for mild cognitive impairment more accurately [third-party research]. Brain PET and cerebrospinal fluid are done only for people whose cause is unclear or whose plan needs adjusting.
- **Who this is for**: It holds for people who can cooperate with a scale. For someone who can no longer cooperate, the tests have to be done another way and the focus moves to care. Don't conclude from one scale: the comparison between two of them matters more than a single score.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "not knowing where to start, being led along by a checkup package."
- **How this works in China**: First ask whether there is a memory clinic near you (neurology or geriatrics at a tier-3 hospital, some psychiatric hospitals). When you register, ask directly for the "memory clinic" or the "cognitive disorders clinic." For the community route, see 9.10.

## 9.7 Don't buy these three yet: brain supplements, one drop of blood, whole-body PET-CT

- **What it costs**: a few hundred to tens of thousands (money: a lot | time: one go | effort: none)
- **In short**: The moment word gets around that "it might be dementia," the most expensive things start arriving at the door. There are three where the price and the usefulness are furthest apart.
  1. **Brain supplements, memory supplements**: no supplement has been shown to prevent dementia or to make memory better. What points the right way is moving, managing chronic disease, and seeing more of people. What you buy is reassurance, not effect.
  2. **"One drop of blood to test for dementia"**: blood markers for Alzheimer's disease really are moving forward, and in 2025 the US regulator approved the first blood test for use in supporting a diagnosis [official wording]; but its place is for people who already have memory symptoms, to help judge — **not as a screening test for people with no symptoms**. Someone without symptoms who spends this money may just buy himself a "positive" result and six months of fear.
  3. **Whole-body PET-CT**: it is not a checkup item. Whole-body PET-CT in healthy people carries a large radiation dose, and its yield for early cancer is limited. The National Health Commission sets out the basic items of a health checkup as a short list, and whole-body PET-CT is not on it; the deeper tests are left to people at risk. In the cancer screening protocol, the official guidance is more direct: "it is not recommended to use chest X-ray, MRI, PET-CT or biomarker testing for lung cancer screening." The brain PET done when dementia is suspected is something the doctor orders to establish the cause, and it is a different thing from a "whole-body cancer-hunting package."
- **Where this comes from**: C | These points are a sorting-out of "go by the evidence and the official wording, don't spend money at random"; they are not saying some test is useless. When there are symptoms, each test has its own place, and the doctor decides it.
- **Who this is for**: It is most worth reading for families who want to "buy peace of mind with money." When symptoms really do appear, go through the memory clinic, not the checkup center and not a supplement livestream.
- **What you get back**: Money — no longer afraid of "spending a great deal and buying back a false reassurance."
- **How this works in China**: The words to refuse can be: "did a doctor order this test for me? If not, I'll leave it for now and go ask the memory clinic."

## 9.8 After a diagnosis, give the family a calendar for the first year

- **What it costs**: time, plus a checklist (money: depends | time: done bit by bit across the year | effort: a little tiring | do you have to bring it up? easy)
- **In short**: The day of diagnosis is the most panicked, but the things that really have to be done can be put into an order. In the first three months, do three of them.
- **What you can do**:
  - **The first three months**: ① **Set up two files**: a medical file (diagnosis, medication, scale results, the treating doctor's name and phone number, test sheets), and a family file (ID card, medical insurance, pension, insurance policies, bank cards, property deed). ② **While he is still clear, settle two things**: who speaks for you when you are not clear (designated guardianship, done at the notary office), and which resuscitations you do or don't want (an advance care plan, see Chapter 22). ③ **Change four places at home**: a lock or a tracker against wandering, a timer or an alarm on the gas stove, grab bars in the bathroom and beside the bed, and the dangerous things put away.
  - **Over the first year, bit by bit**: **Care arrangements** (who is in charge by day, who by night, whether you can use community day care or short-stay residential care to catch your breath); **money** (ask clearly whether long-term care insurance will pay, whether you can get a disability certificate and draw the two subsidies, see 9.10); **support for the carer** (the person doing the caring is the one more likely to break down; arrange shifts and respite); **thinking ahead** (driving, using fire, going out alone, large transfers).
- **Where this comes from**: B | Care, guardianship, respite, long-term care insurance — all of these are written into the national action plan on dementia; exactly what you can get and how to apply differs from place to place.
- **Who this is for**: It holds for people just diagnosed, still in the early or middle stage. For someone who can no longer manage at all, the focus moves from "arranging" to "hands-on care" (see Chapter 23).
- **What you get back**: Dignity + relationships — no longer afraid that "one diagnosis and the whole family comes apart."
- **How this works in China**: See 9.10.

## 9.9 This chapter is about when to get checked; Chapter 8 is about calling 120 now

- **What it costs**: the few seconds it takes to think it through (money: nothing to pay | time: a few minutes | effort: none)
- **In short**: Memory getting worse, forgetting things slowly, is an outpatient matter. But a few things are urgent, and when they appear, don't wait for a clinic appointment: becoming muddled, not recognizing people, talking nonsense over a few hours to a day or two; suddenly unable to speak clearly, with weakness down one side of the body; muddled after a fever or after a blow to the head.
- **What you can do**: One sentence to remember: **confusion that comes on over hours or days is an emergency; forgetting that comes on slowly over months or years is an outpatient matter.** If you can't tell, call 120.
- **Where this comes from**: B | Sudden confusion is mostly caused by infection, lack of water, or muddled use of medicine, and people who already have dementia are more likely to have it.
- **Who this is for**: It holds for all older people. For someone living alone nobody sees these things, which makes it all the more important to tape the table in 8.10 next to the phone.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "the ones who should have waited for a clinic went to the emergency room, and the ones who should have called 120 sat waiting for an appointment."

## 9.10 How this works in China: memory clinics, community screening, disability assessment

This section is about the mainland-China system. Where a term has a counterpart you may know from another country, the counterpart is named alongside it.

- **Memory clinics.** The state is pushing memory clinics and specialist cognitive-disorder services (starting at county level and above in general hospitals), linking screening, diagnosis and treatment, rehabilitation and follow-up into a single line [official wording] (*National Action Plan on Responding to Senile Dementia*, 国卫老龄发〔2024〕42 号). By May 2024, 602 cognitive centers of various levels had been built nationwide, of which 298 were memory disorder prevention and treatment centers [official wording]. When you register, ask for the "memory clinic" or the "cognitive disorders clinic"; where there is no clinic under that name, register with neurology or geriatrics and say "my memory is getting worse and I would like a cognitive assessment."
- **The community gate.** Everyone over 65 already has one free health-management check a year, and initial cognitive screening is being folded into it: the community screens first, and abnormal results are referred to a higher-level hospital for further assessment [official wording] (same plan). Go to the community health centre — the primary-care layer, the rough equivalent of a GP practice — and ask one question: "do you do the cognitive screening for older people, and how is it done?"
- **Disability assessment and subsidies.** When dementia reaches the point of affecting daily life, you can apply for a disability certificate (intellectual disability or psychiatric disability category). China issues one national certificate for this; elsewhere the comparable step is registering for disability benefits, which comes with no card. Apply at the disabled persons' federation of the county or district, or the sub-district (township), where your household registration (*hukou*) is; collect the Disability Assessment Form; be assessed at the psychiatric (appraisal) department of the designated hospital; then the result is posted publicly and the certificate is made. A legal guardian generally handles it. Those who qualify can apply for the "two subsidies for disabled persons" (a living allowance for disabled people in hardship, and a nursing subsidy for people with severe disability); the severe-disability nursing subsidy generally does not look at household income, and some localities have brought grade 3 and grade 4 intellectual and psychiatric disability into it; it can be applied for online [official wording] (the "support for the vulnerable" section of the national government service platform).
- **Where to ask**: the disabled persons' federation at the community or sub-district where you are registered, the district or county civil affairs bureau, the medical insurance bureau, plus the memory clinic at the hospital you usually go to. **The specific standards are whatever the latest local wording says.**

## 9.11 If you can only do one thing today

Run the person you are worried about through the line in 9.2: is what he forgets a "thing" or a "function"?

If you can't tell, or he has already fallen on the "function" side, do one thing: next time you register, don't book a checkup — book the memory clinic or neurology, walk in and say this sentence:

> "His (my) memory has been getting worse lately, and I want to find out whether there is a treatable cause."

## 9.12 If you are reading this for your parents

**How to say it out loud.** Don't say "I suspect you have dementia" — say that and it turns into a fight. Put it on yourself instead: "my memory's been bad lately, I want to get it checked, come with me, we'll both do the test." Put him in the position of "coming with" and "both of us." Once it's booked, just go to the hospital; don't hold a debate at the dinner table first.

**If he won't agree, what you can still do (without his nod).** ① Start the files: one medical, one family, and keep a copy yourself. ② Keep an observation record as in 9.3 for three months, and next time say nothing else — hand the doctor that page first. ③ Talk to the doctor on your own once first, and ask clearly "if he comes, how would you check him." ④ At home, fix the dangerous spots first — the fire, the door, the floor.

What he wants is often not to stay away — it is the fear of being stamped "dementia." Keep that door open for the day he feels for himself that something is wrong.

## 9.13 What I am not sure about in this chapter

- How far Alzheimer's blood markers ("one drop of blood" tests) actually reach in Chinese outpatient clinics, whether they are self-paid, and what they cost have been changing fast in recent years; this chapter writes only the one piece of wording that has been checked — its place is for people with symptoms, to help judge, not as routine screening for people without symptoms. For how a particular hospital does it, ask the memory clinic.
- The assessment process for disability certificates (intellectual and psychiatric) and the standards and scope of the two subsidies vary from place to place; this chapter gives the national-level wording, and what happens on the ground differs — ask the disabled persons' federation, civil affairs and medical insurance bureau where you are registered.
- How far community health centres have actually got with initial cognitive screening varies in speed from place to place; the national action plan sets its target for 2030.
