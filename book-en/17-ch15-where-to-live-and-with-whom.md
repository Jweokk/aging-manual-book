# Chapter 15 · Where to live, and who to grow old with

You have lived in this flat for thirty years. You know where the light is with your eyes shut, and how high the step at the doorway is.

Two things have slowed you down lately. The stairs in the corridor — even holding the handrail, you take them more carefully now. Getting up in the night, you don't quite dare put the big light on. You haven't told anyone — saying it out loud would feel like frightening yourself.

When the time really comes to decide where to live, it usually isn't now. It's after a fall and a discharge from hospital, or after your other half goes first. By then, more often than not, someone else makes that decision for you. This chapter wants to move it forward a little.

## 15.1 What this chapter settles

Where you live, and who you grow old with, is the most expensive choice of your later life — expensive in money, and more expensive still in the time lost. It is almost always made passively, after something has gone wrong: you fall, and only on discharge do you find you can't get up the stairs; you sell the flat and move to your children's city, and only then find your medical insurance (*yibao*) is still registered back home and you can't follow what people say.

Each way of living is set out with four things made clear — who it suits, what it really costs (money / time / energy / face), how to try it once first, and when it doesn't hold.

## 15.2 Home: pass three gates first, then talk about moving the institution's bed into your house

- **What it costs**: a few hundred yuan for small changes, several thousand to tens of thousands for big ones, subsidy depending on where you live; the service fee for a family eldercare bed is billed monthly on top (money: a fair amount | time: days to weeks | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy)
- **In short**: whether the old flat can still be lived in comes down to three things first — whether the building can take a lift; how many steps there are at the door and inside; and whether the toilet is a squat one or a seat.
- **What you can do**: ① Lift: find out whether your building can have one added; for multi-storey old blocks there is now legal backing for adding lifts (Article 22 of the Law on Building a Barrier-Free Environment). ② Steps: for the height differences at the entrance, in the corridor and in the bathroom, add a ramp or a handrail. ③ Squat toilet: change it for a seated one, or add a commode chair. Where the money is best spent: the bathroom, the entryway, and the path from the bed to the toilet (see Chapter 5). Try it once first — start with the bathroom only.
- Family eldercare beds: this isn't buying a bed, it's moving visiting care, age-friendly renovation and smart devices into your home. Do the ability assessment first (GB/T 42195-2022); you qualify only at mild disability or above. Then ask at the community how to apply and who pays.
- **Where this comes from**: A + B | the World Health Organization lists home assessment and modification as one of the ways to prevent falls, and the direction of subsidies is in Minfa [2020] No. 86; family eldercare beds have a Ministry of Civil Affairs standard (MZ/T 234-2024), and by the end of 2024 the national program had supported 184 areas in setting up 358,000 of them. **But** which single change helps how much varies from home to home.
- **Who this is for**: People who can still walk on their own and whose home is their own or a long lease; the beds are only for people at mild disability or above. A healthy 60-year-old should deal with steps and lighting first; at 75 with several conditions, start with bathroom handrails, non-slip surfaces and a night light; if you are already disabled, renovation alone isn't enough — you need a bed or home visits lined up.
- **What you get back**: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid of "everywhere in the house is a trap" and "I want to stay home, but I'm afraid no one will be looking after me."
- **How this works in China**: ask the community, the sub-district office, the district civil affairs bureau — how to apply for renovation and a family eldercare bed, and whether there's a subsidy. Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.3 Community care: which jobs day care and home visits can take off your hands

- **What it costs**: a few dozen yuan a time, or covered by government purchase or subsidy (money: barely any | time: per visit | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy)
- **In short**: what the community can do for you is mostly the small things — somewhere to go in the daytime, someone to sit with, one meal taken care of; and at home, help with meals, bathing, cleaning, medical visits and getting about. It can't stand in for the night, or for a sudden acute illness.
- **What you can do**: go to the community eldercare service station or the sub-district eldercare service centre and ask for a service list. Ask three things clearly: what they can do in your home, how they charge, and from what hour to what hour. A day-care centre is "checked in by day, home at night". Try it once first — book one home-delivered meal or one assisted bath, and see whether anyone actually turns up.
- **Where this comes from**: B | home-visit eldercare services have a national standard (GB/T 43153-2023), and day-care centres have their own national standard for basic service requirements. **But** the density of stations and the quality of service differ a lot from place to place; having a station doesn't mean you can use it, or afford it.
- **Who this is for**: People still living in their own home whose mobility isn't too bad. Most healthy 60-year-olds won't need it; at 75 with several conditions it's most useful, especially meals, medical visits and company in the daytime; if you are already disabled you need professional care, and the community usually can't take on the severe end.
- **What you get back**: Independence + relationships — no longer afraid of "alone at home, no one in the daytime, and I can't be bothered to cook."
- **How this works in China**: ask at the community eldercare service centre; for food, ask about the community canteen (see Chapter 12). Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.4 Institutions: when these signals appear, it is time to go and look

- **What it costs**: private ones start at several thousand yuan a month, and the higher the care grade the more you pay; public ones are cheaper but you queue (money: a lot | time: look first, decide later | effort: none | do you have to ask: yes, and it's a little embarrassing)
- **In short**: the reason to consider an institution isn't the vague sentence "you're old". It's a few signals: living alone; a fall at night; no longer able to walk, and no longer going out; the person caring for you has given way first; needing professional nursing. Two of these together and you should go and look — don't wait for something to happen.
- **What you can do**: look at it the way you'd look at a flat, and ask three things: ① is there a grade rating from the civil affairs department (GB/T 37276-2018)? ② how many residents does each care worker look after, is anyone on duty at night, how far is the nearest hospital? ③ the itemized fees, how leaving early is settled, and whether prepaid fees go into a bank-supervised account. Try it once first — ask whether you can stay a few days on trial. Public institutions mostly take people in extreme difficulty as a backstop: cheap, but you wait your turn; private ones admit you quickly, with wide differences in price and service.
- **Where this comes from**: C | "try before you decide" is common practice, but there is no unified national rule on "trial stays / short stays"; policies and fees differ by area, so you can only ask your local civil affairs office or the institution itself. Public institutions have assessment and waiting-list rules (Shanghai, for example — see the sources); private ones have no common yardstick.
- **Who this is for**: People whose family care can't hold, or who need professional nursing. For most healthy 60-year-olds it's early; at 75 with several conditions, first look at whether anyone at home can cover 24 hours; once disabled it is often a hard necessity. Some older people end up in an institution because a family member decided it for them.
- **What you get back**: Relationships + dignity — no longer afraid of "the day I go down, no one at home can catch me."
- **How this works in China**: ask the district civil affairs bureau or your city's eldercare service website; before signing a contract, look at the prepaid fees. Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.5 Group eldercare among friends: the villa in Hangzhou, and the lessons that came later

- **What it costs**: sharing the rent (one house split among several people) and splitting living costs (money: some money | time: long-term | effort: a bit tiring | do you have to ask: hard to bring up — this is where people most easily fall out over money)
- **In short**: group eldercare (*baotuan yanglao*) is a few older people choosing to live together, looking out for each other and splitting the costs. In Hangzhou, 13 older people shared one villa, and for a while it was held up as a model. Behind the model there was a price — the first reason these arrangements break up is that people can't agree about money.
- **What you can do**: try it once, don't sign up for a long stretch at the start, and above all don't sell your own house to fund it. Live together for a month first, or for one season, and test out three things: who buys the food and cooks, how the costs are split, and who looks after someone who falls ill — write it down before you move in.
- **Where this comes from**: B | the Hangzhou house is real (2017, Yuhang, 13 older people, about 500 square metres), and both Xinhua Net and CCTV reported it in 2018. Why this kind of arrangement struggles to last was put more bluntly in a 2021 report (Beijing Evening News, reposted by CCTV.com): the people interviewed said "money is what stops this group holding together", and the report also points out that medical services on the outskirts can't keep up; Wu Danxing, a member of the expert committee on eldercare services at the Ministry of Civil Affairs, said self-organized groups have "nothing binding".
- **Who this is for**: People who can still manage on their own, whose temperaments get along, and whose means are roughly similar. Fairly feasible for a healthy 60-year-old; at 75 with several conditions, be careful — a group on the outskirts has no professional medical care and the risk is higher; once disabled, it essentially doesn't hold.
- **What you get back**: Relationships + money — no longer afraid of "being too lonely on my own" and of "falling out in the end".
- **How this works in China**: there is no official protection for these arrangements, so disputes are yours to guard against — money put in, cost sharing, how someone leaves, who cares for the sick: all written down in black and white. Cizhou Nursing Home in Jiading, Shanghai, ran as a crowdfunded group arrangement for more than ten years before its members moved away one after another; that is one example.

## 15.6 Mutual-aid eldercare and time banks: Nanjing's ledger, and why it can't carry the weight

- **What it costs**: volunteering costs nothing; what you trade is future service hours (money: barely any | time: per session | effort: a bit tiring | do you have to ask: easy)
- **In short**: a time bank is "today I help you and the hours go into the book; later someone helps me for the same number of hours". The difficulty isn't the bookkeeping. It's whether the hours you deposit can actually be withdrawn later.
- **What you can do**: ask at the community whether there is a mutual-aid eldercare site locally, how to register, and how service is recorded and redeemed. Try it once first — do one short session and see whether the platform records it, and whether it pays out. Nanjing can serve as a reference: the civil affairs figures for June 2026 were more than 1,300 sites, covering over 99% of urban and rural communities, 94,000 registered volunteers, 1.47 million orders in total and 920,000 hours of service time stored; in mid-2024 it was 61,000 volunteers and 132,000 service recipients.
- **Where this comes from**: A + B | Nanjing's is a citywide platform led by the government (piloted in 2019, renamed in 2023); Shanghai also rolled out an eldercare service time bank citywide in 2021 (Hu Min Yanglao Fa [2021] No. 28). In 2026 the Ministry of Civil Affairs and 10 other departments issued the Opinions on Advancing the Development of Mutual-Aid Eldercare Services (Minfa [2026] No. 11), the first national-level definition of mutual-aid eldercare, setting coverage of urban and rural community eldercare facilities with mutual-aid functions at no less than 70% by 2030. **But** research generally points to two reasons it can't carry the weight — weak law and supervision, and low participation (see the note at the end of this chapter).
- **Who this is for**: The younger old (in their sixties) who are in good health and have time to spare are the best providers; at 75 with several conditions you can do light service and are also most in need of it; if you are already disabled you are a recipient. Don't stake acute nursing care on it.
- **What you get back**: Relationships + dignity — no longer afraid of "being useless now that I'm old" and "having no one at hand".
- **How this works in China**: ask at the community or the civil affairs office whether there is a mutual-aid eldercare pilot platform locally. Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.7 Seasonal migration: first work out whether this is eldercare or an investment

- **What it costs**: ordinary sojourn living means rent or a bed fee by the month or by the season; if someone asks you to "pay a large sum up front and promises to return the principal with interest", that isn't eldercare (money: a fair amount | time: by the season | effort: a bit tiring | do you have to ask: easy)
- **In short**: for an older person in reasonable health, wintering in the south and summering in the north is fine — sojourn eldercare (*lüju*, living somewhere for a season), and what people here call migratory-bird retirement (*houniao*, the bird that follows the seasons). What you need to guard against is something else — people using the labels "sojourn eldercare" and "migratory-bird retirement" to get older people to put in a large sum of money up front.
- **What you can do**: hold three bottom lines. ① Pay only by the month or the season; no large prepayment, no membership fee, VIP card or deposit. ② Don't sign a contract that promises to return your principal with interest. ③ Before any large outlay, leave it a day, and ask your children, the community, the civil affairs office. Try it once first — stay a season at the walk-up price; don't buy a card the moment you arrive.
- **Where this comes from**: A | in 2023 the Ministry of Civil Affairs issued a specific risk notice on illegal fundraising in the eldercare sector; in 2025 a Supreme People's Procuratorate "eldercare finance" case, using the offer of retirement flats and sojourn bases as the hook, took in more than 5 billion yuan in illegal fundraising. **But** legitimate sojourn and health-retreat services do exist; the difference is exactly whether you have to hand over a large sum first.
- **Who this is for**: People still in good health, with spare money, who can arrange their own travel. Fairly feasible for a healthy 60-year-old; at 75 with several conditions, be careful, because treatment away from home is the weak point; if already disabled, long-distance sojourns don't suit. If someone wants a large sum from you first, it doesn't hold at any age.
- **What you get back**: Money — no longer afraid of "having money taken off me in the name of a sojourn".
- **How this works in China**: for treatment away from home, file the out-of-area treatment record first; if you meet "pay a lot up front, get returns", call 110 or 12315 first. Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.8 The drifting old: following your children, and the bills to settle first

- **What it costs**: no extra money, but medical insurance, familiar faces and your own place in things all have to be built again from scratch (money: barely any | time: long-term | effort: a bit tiring, more so if you're minding grandchildren | do you have to ask: yes, and it's a little embarrassing — you feel like a guest in your child's home)
- **In short**: the drifting old (*laopiao zu*) are older people who leave their hometown to live in the city where their children are, most of them to look after grandchildren. The difficulties are reimbursing medical costs away from home, not understanding the local speech, losing every old friend, and local benefits not reaching you.
- **What you can do**: do three things first. ① File the out-of-area treatment record; do it properly, so that outpatient and inpatient costs can be settled directly. ② Ask the local community eldercare service station what you can use without local household registration (*hukou*). ③ Find one person or one circle you can talk to; don't tie your whole life to the grandchildren. Try it once first — live there a quarter first, and don't rush to deal with the old family house.
- **Where this comes from**: B | there is no single official wording for the scale: the National Health Commission's *Report on China's Migrant Population Development 2018* (《中国流动人口发展报告 2018》) said nearly 18 million older people had moved to be with family, 7.2% of the 247 million migrants at the time; after the seventh national census in 2020, media calculations based on census data put the migrant population aged 60 and over at about 33.27 million. The years and the definitions differ, and this book doesn't merge them into one number; read it as "tens of millions".
- **Who this is for**: People in reasonable health who are willing to go, and whose relationship with their children is workable. Feasible for a healthy 60-year-old; at 75 with several conditions, be careful, since long-term treatment away from home is the hard part; if already disabled and moving with them, the care all lands on the children.
- **What you get back**: Relationships — no longer afraid of "still drifting at my age" and "never at ease in my child's home".
- **How this works in China**: file the out-of-area treatment record first (the National Healthcare Security Administration app, or the medical insurance office where you're enrolled); whether community services are open to you, ask the community. Go by the latest local wording.

## 15.9 Moving house, and living alone: arrange two things first

- **Moving house** (money: some money | time: a few weeks | effort: quite tiring, especially for the very old | do you have to ask: yes, and it's a little embarrassing)
  - **In short**: for a very old person, moving isn't changing address; it's pulling the person out of a place they know. The older you are, the more likely something goes wrong in the six months after the move.
  - **What you can do**: if you really are moving, go in steps. ① Separate what can be separated — don't cut off all your old ties on the same day. ② Keep the layout of the new place as familiar as you can. ③ For someone who already can't find their way, ask the doctor before the move.
  - **Where this comes from**: B | research suggests moving is linked to health outcomes, and to how well the new home fits old habits (see the sources). **But** the findings don't agree.
  - **Who this is for**: Everyone, with higher risk for people over 75, with several conditions, or who already can't find their way. For a healthy 60-year-old the risk of moving is small.
  - **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid of "moving somewhere else and coming apart, or getting confused".
- **Living alone: agree a check-in** (money: nothing | time: ten minutes to agree | effort: none | do you have to ask: easy)
  - **In short**: the worst thing about living alone isn't that no one looks after you; it's that if something happens, no one knows. Fixing that is small: agree a way of making contact with your children, a neighbour or a friend.
  - **What you can do**: ① check in at a fixed time every day — one phone call or one message, either will do. ② agree how long with no word before someone phones you, and how much longer before someone comes to the door. Try it once first — agree a morning check starting today, and keep it up for a week.
  - **Where this comes from**: C | common practice. Localities have visiting arrangements for older people living alone, in empty nests, or left behind (Minfa [2022] No. 73). **But** visits can't cover an emergency.
  - **Who this is for**: People living alone, in an empty nest, or left behind, especially the very old and those with several conditions. For a healthy 60-year-old keep the arrangement light; at 75 with several conditions, make it tighter; disabled and living alone is the highest risk.
  - **How this works in China**: register as living alone or in an empty nest with the community or sub-district so you are on the visiting list; a one-button call device or fall-detection equipment can be installed.

## 15.10 How this works in China: four places that can give you an answer

- **Sub-district and community eldercare service centres (stations)**: family eldercare beds, day care, home visits, mutual-aid sites, visits to those living alone — start by asking here.
- **Civil affairs departments (district civil affairs bureau, city eldercare service website)**: the list of institutions and their grade ratings, waiting-list rules for public institutions, subsidies for age-friendly renovation.
- **Long-term care insurance (LTCI, *zhanghuxian*)**: in March 2026 the General Office of the CPC Central Committee and the General Office of the State Council issued the Opinions on Accelerating the Establishment of the Long-Term Care Insurance System, to reach near-universal coverage nationwide by the end of 2028 over about 3 years, at a contribution rate of about 0.3%. This is a social insurance scheme that contributors pay into, not a private policy you take out for yourself. How to apply for the disability assessment, and how much your local scheme pays: ask the local medical insurance department or the body that administers it.
- **Meals for older people**: under Minfa [2023] No. 58, local areas are rolling out meal points, and many subsidize by category — the very old, the disabled, those living alone. Ask the community whether there is a meal point in your area and whether they will bring it up to your door.

Projects, subsidies and thresholds differ from place to place. Go by the latest local wording.

## The one thing you can do today

Stand at your front door, or out in the corridor, and count: from the door to your chair, from the bed to the toilet, how many steps are there.

Then, for those steps, put up — or borrow — something to hold on to: a handrail on the wall, a post by the door, either will do. Today. Just this one place.

Steps are the thing many older people walk every day and have never once counted. Once you've counted them, you'll know whether this flat can still be lived in.

## If you are reading this for your parents

There is one thing you have to bring up first. He almost certainly won't say "it's not easy living here" on his own; you have to hand him that sentence — but not in the tone of "you should move somewhere else". The moves you can make for him are these:

- **Walk through the flat for him first**: next time you're home, look at three places — how many steps in the corridor, whether the toilet is squat or seated, whether there is a light on the path from bed to toilet at night. Don't lecture once you've seen it; fix one of them yourself first, and you pay for it.
- **Ask at the community once**: whether there is a meal point in the area, home-visit services, visits for people living alone, and how to apply for age-friendly renovation. Once you know, put the options in front of him — he chooses, you don't decide.
- **Arrange one thing that can be tried**: whether the institution does short stays, whether the community has a taster, whether a family eldercare bed can begin with an ability assessment. Try first; make no decision yet.
- **If he is moving to your city, do these first**: if he is going to drift along with you, file the out-of-area treatment record first; then tape the community's phone number beside his bed.
- **Leave one channel open for a check-in**: agree a fixed time each day when he checks in, and say plainly that it is "I want to know you're all right", not "I'm watching you".

Do the one thing you can actually do first; it works better than waiting for him to agree.

## What I am not sure about in this chapter

- Trial stays and short stays at eldercare institutions: there is no unified national rule, policies and fees differ by area, and this chapter only says "ask whether you can stay on trial" — it has not checked specific standards place by place.
- There is no single official figure for the size of the drifting-old population: the National Health Commission's 2018 report said nearly 18 million older people had moved to be with family, while media calculations from the 2020 census put the migrant population aged 60 and over at about 33.27 million. The years and the definitions differ, so this chapter gives a range and does not merge them into one number.
- Claims about the "break-up rate" of group eldercare (the "over 60%", "90%", "34.2%" that circulate online) can't be traced to an authoritative source, and none of them is used.
- "How moving affects the health of very old people" is treated here only as a direction; the studies I read don't agree with one another, and I haven't checked page numbers and data tables in each.
- "Weak law and supervision, low participation" in time banks is the common ground of several research abstracts; this chapter hasn't checked the page numbers and data tables of any single full text.
- This chapter gives no doses, makes no diagnoses, and doesn't decide for anyone where to live. These choices have no standard answer: the same way of living can hold for one family and not for another.
