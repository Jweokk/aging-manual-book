# Chapter 14 · Vaccines, screening and checkup reports: only do what is worth doing

Late in the year the phone rings: "Add two tumor markers, it's only a few hundred yuan." You spend close to two thousand. The report comes back with seven or eight arrows pointing up, and one small line at the end: "further examination recommended."

## 14.1 What this chapter settles

When it comes to checkups, many people carry a default formula in their heads: **the more you test, the safer you feel; the more you spend, the more covered you are.**

That formula is wrong. With a 2,800-yuan package, the more items get added, the more likely they are to be things you don't need; and a free national checkup is the one many people can't be bothered to go to.

Boiled down to one sentence: **which vaccines, which screenings, which items on the report — that depends on your age and your risk, not on the price of the package.** The old rule was "more testing can't hurt." At your age one word has to change: **some testing does hurt.**

## 14.2 Flu vaccine: one shot a year, given in September–October

- **What it costs**: a few dozen to one or two hundred yuan, once a year (money: next to nothing | time: once | effort: none | speaking up: not needed); in many cities it is free for people 60 and over.
- **In short**: For a young person flu is a miserable week; for you it can be pneumonia and a hospital stay. This shot has to be given every year, and it is the cheapest shot there is.
- **What you can do**: Get it every year (the virus changes every year); put it in September–October, before the season; if you miss that window don't give up — you can still get it at any point in the flu season.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The Chinese CDC's *Technical Guidelines for Influenza Vaccination in China (2026–2027)* (《中国流感疫苗预防接种技术指南（2026—2027）》) lists people 60 and over as a priority group for vaccination [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: Almost everyone over 60; if you have a fever right now, or have had a severe allergic reaction to a vaccine ingredient, ask a doctor first.
- **What you get back**: Independence + money — no longer afraid that "one bout of flu will knock my body down"
- **How this works in China**: Go to a community health centre or a vaccination clinic; in Beijing it is free for Beijing-registered older people 60 and over (Beijing Municipal Health Commission, 2025-09-11) [official wording].

## 14.3 Pneumococcal vaccine: the 23-valent one after 60

- **What it costs**: one or two hundred to three or four hundred yuan, usually once in a lifetime (money: next to nothing | time: once | effort: none | speaking up: not needed); in some cities it is free for people 65 and over.
- **In short**: Streptococcus pneumoniae is one of the most common culprits in pneumonia in older people. This shot does not prevent an ordinary cold; it prevents the kind of pneumonia that puts you in a hospital bed.
- **What you can do**: In China older people usually get the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23), one primary dose; people with normal immune function usually do not repeat it; if you have high-risk factors and your last dose was more than 5 years ago, you can discuss one more dose with a doctor. The 13-valent conjugate vaccine (PCV13) also protects older people, and the 2024 consensus on pneumonia in older adults recommends the 23-valent and the 13-valent side by side; but in China the 13-valent label's approved population is still children, so use in older people is off-label and has to be assessed by a doctor.
- **Where this comes from**: A | *Chinese Expert Recommendations on Influenza and Pneumococcal Vaccination in Older Adults* (《老年人流感和肺炎链球菌疫苗接种中国专家建议》) (Chinese Journal of Geriatrics, 2018) recommends PPSV23 for people 60 and over [official wording]; *Expert Consensus on the Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment of Pneumonia in Older Adults (2024 Edition)* (《老年肺炎临床诊断与治疗专家共识（2024 年版）》) recommends that older people receive PPV23 or PCV13, evidence level ⅠB [third-party research].
- **Who this is for**: People over 60, especially those with chronic heart or lung disease or diabetes. Which one to get and how many doses — ask the doctor at the vaccination clinic.
- **What you get back**: Independence + money — no longer afraid that "one bout of pneumonia puts me in intensive care"
- **How this works in China**: Go to the vaccination clinic at a community health centre; in Beijing, Beijing-registered older people 65 and over who have never had the 23-valent pneumococcal vaccine can book, be assessed, and get one dose free (Beijing Municipal Health Commission, 2025-09-11) [official wording].

## 14.4 Shingles vaccine: worth it after 50, but ask about the price and local offers first

- **What it costs**: two doses, paid out of pocket, list price in the thousand-yuan range; many places are running a time-limited "get one dose free" scheme, with clear start and end dates (money: a fair amount | time: two doses, 2–6 months apart | effort: none | speaking up: easy).
- **In short**: Shingles is nicknamed "the snake around the waist" in Chinese. The torment is not the string of blisters; it is the nerve pain that goes on for months after the blisters have healed. The older you are, the more likely you are to get it, and the worse it hurts.
- **What you can do**: People over 50 with normal immune function can choose to get the recombinant shingles vaccine, 2 doses 2–6 months apart; people who have had chickenpox or shingles can still get it; it is a self-paid vaccine, so ask about the price and the local offer before you go.
- **Where this comes from**: A | The eligible age and the dosing schedule for the two approved vaccines are given in Chinese CDC public information (2025-07-14) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: People over 50 with normal immune function benefit most. If your immune function is deficient or you are on immunosuppressants, the live attenuated vaccine is not recommended, and whether you can have the recombinant one has to be weighed with a doctor.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid that "it burns like fire and stabs like needles, and hurts for months"
- **How this works in China**: Go to your local vaccination clinic and pay for it yourself; Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and some districts of Shanghai have run time-limited "get one dose free" schemes (Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region Disease Control and Prevention Administration, 2025-05-09) [official wording]. Take Inner Mongolia as an example: for people 50 and over who completed dose 1 between 2025-05-06 and 2025-08-31 and completed the full two-dose course before 2025-12-31, dose 2 was free (not including the per-dose vaccination service fee). Whether your area has such a scheme, and until when, depends on the latest local notice.

## 14.5 Before and after the shot: anything you are unsure about, ask the doctor at the vaccination clinic

- **What it costs**: a few extra questions (money: nothing | time: a few minutes | effort: none | speaking up: easy).
- **In short**: A sore arm, a bit of fever and tiredness after the shot is mostly normal and passes in a day or two. What you actually need to ask about is what you are unsure of: can I have it with this condition of mine, do the medicines I take conflict with it.
- **What you can do**: Before the shot, tell the doctor which medicines you are taking, which illnesses you have, and any past allergies; if you have a fever or an acute illness is flaring up, wait; after the shot, sit there a full 30 minutes before you leave; if you get a high fever that won't come down, a widespread rash, or can't catch your breath, go to hospital at once and say which vaccine you have just had.
- **Where this comes from**: B | This is the standard way every vaccination is handled; the specific contraindications depend on that vaccine's label and on your own condition.
- **Who this is for**: Everyone. Whether you can have it, and when, only the doctor assessing you on the spot can decide.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid that "the shot was wrong and something went wrong with me"
- **How this works in China**: The doctor at the vaccination clinic assesses you on the spot and asks you to sign an informed-consent form; if you are unsure about anything, ask.

## 14.6 Colorectal cancer: from 40, scored by risk, stool test first

- **What it costs**: a stool test is a few dozen yuan, a colonoscopy several hundred to over a thousand (money: some money | time: once | effort: none | speaking up: you have to, and it's a bit embarrassing; a colonoscopy is a bit embarrassing).
- **In short**: Polyps in the bowel take many years to turn into cancer, and that window — the time it leaves you — is the best value in all of screening. If you are afraid of a colonoscopy, you can do the stool test first, and have the colonoscopy if it comes back positive.
- **What you can do**: The national plan scores you on age, sex, smoking, body-mass index and history among first-degree relatives; a cumulative score of 4 or more counts as high risk; for high-risk people who have never had the disease, screening is recommended from age 40 to 74; colonoscopy is first choice, and if you won't accept it you can choose alternatives such as an annual stool test; if no lesion is found, the next colonoscopy can be 5–10 years later.
- **Where this comes from**: A | National Health Commission, *Colorectal Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis and Early Treatment Plan (2024 Edition)* (《结直肠癌筛查与早诊早治方案（2024 年版）》) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: People with a family history, long-term smokers and people carrying extra weight should be tested sooner. If you already have blood in the stool, a change in bowel habit, or unexplained weight loss, that is not screening — that is a doctor visit, straight to gastroenterology.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid that "by the time I feel something it is already advanced"
- **How this works in China**: A community health centre can do the risk assessment and the fecal occult-blood test; for a colonoscopy you register with gastroenterology. Some cities have free places under the "urban cancer early diagnosis and early treatment programme" — ask at your community health centre.

## 14.7 Breast and cervical cancer: free screening in many places, ages 35 to 64

- **What it costs**: free within the government programme (money: next to nothing | time: half a day | effort: none | speaking up: you have to, and it's a bit embarrassing).
- **In short**: These are two of the few cancers where screening really does bring the death rate down. The state has provided a free channel, with particular care for rural women and urban women on the minimum living guarantee.
- **What you can do**: In many places the free screening is for women aged 35–64, with priority for rural women and urban women on dibao; cervical cancer screening means a gynecological examination plus a primary screen (cytology or HPV testing), and those with a positive result go on to colposcopy and a tissue sample; breast cancer screening means a breast examination plus breast ultrasound, with mammography added if needed.
- **Where this comes from**: A | National Health Commission, official interpretation of the *Cervical Cancer Screening Work Plan* and the *Breast Cancer Screening Work Plan* (《宫颈癌筛查工作方案》《乳腺癌筛查工作方案》) (2022-01-18) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: Women aged 35–64. Outside that age range, or if you already have symptoms (a lump you can feel, abnormal bleeding), book an appointment and get checked directly — don't wait for the free round.
- **What you get back**: Independence + dignity — no longer afraid that "it is found too late and I suffer for nothing"
- **How this works in China**: Go to a community health centre, a township health centre or the women's federation and ask how to sign up for the local free "two cancers" screening; the latest local wording governs.

## 14.8 Low-dose CT for lung cancer: for high-risk people only, not everyone

- **What it costs**: a few hundred yuan a time, generally once a year (money: some money | time: once | effort: none | speaking up: not needed).
- **In short**: Low-dose CT is useful, but only for some people. If you don't smoke and have no other risk factors and you have one every year, most of what it finds is a false alarm, while the radiation is real.
- **What you can do**: First match yourself against the state's definition of "high-risk": **age 50 or over**, and any one of the following: a smoking history of 20 pack-years (including people who have quit but for less than 15 years); living with a smoker or working in the same room as one for more than 20 years; COPD; occupational exposure to asbestos, radon or the like for more than 1 year; a parent, child or sibling who has had lung cancer. If you match, in principle one scan a year from 50 to 74.
- **Where this comes from**: A | National Health Commission, *Lung Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis and Early Treatment Plan (2024 Edition)* (《肺癌筛查与早诊早治方案（2024 年版）》) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: Anyone who meets one of the five items above. If you are not high-risk, **tumor markers, chest X-ray and PET-CT are not recommended for lung cancer screening** — the plan says so in writing.
- **What you get back**: Independence + money — no longer afraid that "I skip the scan I need and get one every year that I don't"
- **How this works in China**: Most hospitals and checkup centres can do low-dose CT; whether you are high-risk and whether to scan every year is for a respiratory or thoracic-surgery doctor to decide.

## 14.9 Prostate and thyroid: not recommended as screening for everyone

- **What it costs**: one tube of blood for PSA is a few dozen yuan, an ultrasound a few dozen to just over a hundred (money: next to nothing | time: once | effort: none | speaking up: easy).
- **In short**: These two are practically thrown into a package for free, but "it can be tested" is not the same as "it should be tested". You find a pile of nodules and one number slightly high, then come round after round of unnecessary tests and surgery, and the person is frightened to pieces first.
- **What you can do**: **Prostate (PSA)** is not a matter of everyone getting a tube of blood drawn; domestic guidelines limit it to people with "a life expectancy of more than 10 years and high risk": age 60 or over, or over 45 with a family history of prostate cancer, or over 40 carrying a BRCA2 mutation — and the decision comes only after "fully understanding the pros and cons, together with the specialist's advice". **Thyroid**: the National Health Commission's diagnosis and treatment guideline states in writing that "screening the general population for thyroid tumors is not recommended"; only people with high-risk factors such as childhood radiation to the head and neck or a history of whole-body radiotherapy need earlier testing.
- **Where this comes from**: A | National Cancer Center, *Chinese Guidelines for Prostate Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis and Early Treatment (2022, Beijing)* (《中国前列腺癌筛查与早诊早治指南（2022，北京）》); National Health Commission, *Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Thyroid Cancer (2022 Edition)* (《甲状腺癌诊疗指南（2022 年版）》) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: If you have no family history, no symptoms and no doctor's advice, you can skip both. An abnormal result (a high PSA, a thyroid nodule) does not mean cancer; have a specialist read it first, and don't rush into surgery.
- **What you get back**: Dignity + money — no longer afraid of "being led along by a package into a pile of unnecessary tests"
- **How this works in China**: Being "given" these two in a package doesn't mean you should have them tested; whether to test, register with urology or endocrinology and ask the doctor.

## 14.10 How to read a checkup report: what to manage, what to watch, and what you get pushed to investigate

- **What it costs**: one sheet of paper, read slowly (money: nothing | time: half an hour | effort: none | speaking up: not needed).
- **In short**: Seven or eight arrows on one report does not mean you have seven or eight diseases. What a package sells is "lots of items", not "the right items".
- How to do it (start by sorting into three groups): **what you should manage** is blood pressure, blood sugar, blood lipids, waist circumference and weight — the cardiovascular and metabolic problems you can actually act on, and the ones most likely to kill you; if they are abnormal, manage them the way the doctor says. **What you can watch** is mild fluctuation in liver and kidney function, a single thyroid nodule, a small cyst; many of these can be rechecked in six months or a year, and there is no need to rush off for a specialist appointment the same day. **What you most easily get pushed to investigate further** is a raised tumor marker added on along the way, an unexplained small shadow; these often lead to a long chain of "further examinations" that mostly end in nothing, with the money spent and the fright taken.
- How to spot the pitch: when you hear "add two tumor markers for a few hundred yuan", "big discount on whole-body CT", "limited places, decide today" — slow down a step. Tumor markers are not cancer screening, and the national plan explicitly does not recommend them for lung cancer screening; a whole-body CT with no symptoms mostly finds false alarms, and the radiation is real.
- **Where this comes from**: B | "Tumor markers are not used for general cancer screening" is in the national plan; the tiering of indicators and the package sales talk are put together from common practice and public warnings.
- **Who this is for**: Anyone who has a checkup report in hand, or who often gets sales calls about checkups. An arrow is not a diagnosis; take the whole sheet to a general practitioner or your family doctor and have it read through, and don't look up single items online.
- **What you get back**: Money + dignity — no longer afraid of "lying awake at night over an arrow, or being led along by a package"
- **How this works in China**: The general-practice clinic at a community health centre, and your contracted family doctor, can both help you read a checkup report, and can help you judge which items in a package you don't need to add.

## 14.11 Free health management after 65: the one that costs you nothing

- **What it costs**: free, the state pays (money: nothing | time: half a day | effort: none | speaking up: not needed).
- **In short**: This is the free annual checkup the state gives permanent residents 65 and over. Many people don't know about it, or can't be bothered to go. What you are entitled to, don't drop.
- **What you can do**: Go to the community health centre or township health centre for your area, once a year: an assessment of lifestyle and health status; a physical examination including blood pressure and weight, with a rough check of the mouth, vision, hearing and movement; auxiliary tests including complete blood count, urinalysis, liver function, kidney function, fasting blood glucose, blood lipids, ECG and abdominal ultrasound; and health guidance. From 2025 it also adds a frontal chest DR and HbA1c testing, and continues the cognitive-function primary screen.
- **Where this comes from**: A | *National Basic Public Health Service Standards (Third Edition)* (《国家基本公共卫生服务规范（第三版）》), older-adult health management service; the items added in 2025 are in the *Notice on Doing the 2025 Basic Public Health Service Work Well* (《关于做好 2025 年基本公共卫生服务工作的通知》) (Guowei Jicengfa [2025] No. 7) [official wording].
- **Who this is for**: Permanent residents 65 and over in the area (whether your household registration is local follows local rules). If you went once and haven't been back for years, you are throwing this state checkup away every year.
- **What you get back**: Independence + money — no longer afraid that "I skip the free test I am entitled to and pay for a package instead"
- **How this works in China**: Just go to your community health centre or township health centre and ask "how do I book the free checkup for older people, and when will you be in our neighbourhood". The results go into your electronic health record, and places are working on opening that record to you directly; the latest local wording governs.

## 14.12 If your body is already raising the alarm, don't wait for the next checkup

- **What it costs**: one doctor visit (money: the cost of one registration | time: half a day | effort: none | speaking up: easy).
- **In short**: Screening is for people who feel nothing. If your body is already raising a clear alarm, this is not a screening matter — it is a matter of seeing a doctor as soon as possible.
- **What you can do**: If any of the following appears, don't delay and don't wait for next year's checkup: unexplained weight loss — you haven't been starving yourself or dieting, and you have lost several jin over a few months; pain you can't explain, getting worse, worse at night; bleeding — blood in the stool, blood coughed up, blood in the urine, or bleeding again after menopause; a clear drop in weight over a short period, or not being able to eat or swallow; a change in bowel habit that shows no sign of settling.
- **Where this comes from**: B | These are signals that recur across countries' "cancer alarm symptoms"; what they actually mean can only be judged by seeing a doctor.
- **Who this is for**: Anyone with any one of the above. This is not a "watch it a while longer" situation — especially bleeding and short-term weight loss; register with the right department as soon as possible.
- **What you get back**: Independence — no longer afraid that "my body is signalling and I am still waiting for the next package"
- **How this works in China**: Register with the relevant specialty, and write on a sheet of paper when the symptom started and how it has changed, and take it to the doctor; if you have a contracted family doctor, call first and ask which department to register with.

## 14.13 In one sentence: what to test depends on your age and risk, not the price of the package

Keep just one line: **whether a test is worth doing depends on your age + your risk factors + the guidelines, not on the price of the package.**

Used the other way round it is also a ruler: if the same test is sold to everyone at the same price with the same reason, it was not calculated for you.

So work it out yourself: **on your last checkup report, how many items can you say were aimed at which risk of yours?** The ones you can't answer for, cross off the next one.

## 14.14 If you can only do one thing today

Open your phone, look up the number of your community health centre, call it, and ask three questions:

> "How do I sign up for the free checkup for people over 65?"
> "When can I get this year's flu vaccine, and is it free?"
> "How do I take part in the local 'two cancers' screening and the urban cancer early diagnosis and early treatment programme?"

Three questions, one phone call. The benefits you are entitled to and the shots you should have start with this call.

## 14.15 If you are reading this for your parents

The hard part of this chapter for you is not not knowing; it is how to get your parents to agree to go.

**How to say it out loud.** Don't say "you should get the flu vaccine" — that is an order. Switch to a version that saves him trouble: "The community health centre is giving flu vaccines this week. I'll go with you, and we'll get the free checkup for people over 65 done at the same time — half a day and it's done." Turn "you should" into "it's convenient, it's free, and someone goes with you", and the chance he nods is much higher — and the flu shot and the free checkup can often be done in one trip.

**What you can still do when he says no.** Three things that don't need his agreement: go to the community health centre first and find out exactly how to sign up for the free checkup, the two-cancers screening and the urban cancer early diagnosis and early treatment programme, and write it out on one sheet of paper; collect the family vaccination booklet and all the past checkup reports in one place, and you keep an eye on when the next one is due; when the report comes, don't let him read it alone — sit with him and have a general practitioner read the whole sheet through. If he won't go, you lay the road first; on the day he softens, you won't have to start from zero.

## 14.16 What I am not sure about in this chapter

- The vaccine guidelines and plans cited in this chapter are written as of the version at the time of publication. The flu vaccine guideline is updated year by year (this chapter uses the 2026–2027 version), and the range of pneumococcal vaccine products is also changing; before you are vaccinated, the vaccination clinic's latest wording for that year governs.
- The shingles "get one dose free" scheme, the free two-cancers screening and the urban cancer early diagnosis and early treatment programme all vary by place: start and end dates, participating clinics, ages and screening intervals are not identical everywhere. This chapter follows the national plan's 35–64 age range and its priority for rural women and urban women on dibao; locally, the latest notice governs.
