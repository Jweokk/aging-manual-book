# Chapter 1 · Growing Old Does Not Happen in One Day

You are probably somewhere like this. In your forties, and for the first time your checkup report has an extra arrow or two next to it, and you laugh it off with "I've reached that age"; or in your fifties, and when you squat down and stand back up, you have to pause in place for half a second before you dare take a step.

You have not yet counted yourself among the "old". But you are already counting, in your head: how many more years can I go downstairs on my own, go out on my own, make my own decisions.

Where you stand in the process of getting old right now decides what you should be saving up over the next ten years.

| Stage | Where you are now | What to do at this stage | Which part to turn to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage one: not old yet** | In your forties or fifties, or you still consider yourself young | Save up capital: muscle, bone, heart and brain, and also money and people | Part One |
| **Stage two: just starting to get old** | Your body begins to send signals: blood pressure is up, stairs leave you short of breath, your back gives out | Read the signals; get checked for what should be checked; prevent what should be prevented | Part Two |
| **Stage three: starting to need help** | Your strength is failing, or there is someone disabled at home | Sort out the home, the money, the people, the authority | Part Four |
| **Stage four: the last stretch** | The days have gone into countdown | Open up the conversation; put the final decisions in writing | Part Five |

This book is written stage by stage. You are most likely in Stage One, or just stepping out of Stage One into Stage Two. This chapter is written for people in Stage One.

## 1.1 What this chapter is for

Most people only start thinking about their later years after an accident: a fall, a hospital stay, a family member noticing that you can't remember things.

By the time that day comes, half of what you could have done is already out of reach.

This chapter, while nothing has gone wrong yet, sets three things out clearly. First, how getting old happens: it starts at thirty, you just can't feel it. Second, "old" is not one thing but four different things, and many people use these four words interchangeably — and once they are mixed up, what should have been prevented hasn't been prevented, and what should have been checked hasn't been checked. Third, which of the things called "just age" are actually illnesses to be treated, and which fears are being sold to you by someone.

## 1.2 The body has a lid, and it can hold things down for years

Growing old does not happen in one day. That sentence has two layers.

One layer is that the downward slope starts very early, a little each year, not suddenly on some day.

The other layer is that the body has a set of compensatory mechanisms: when one function declines, other places take on more of the load, and they can hold a hidden problem down for many years. So you don't notice that it is declining.

Until one day the lid can no longer hold: a fall, an infection, a hospital stay. That moment is not the starting point of getting old; it is the day the lid is lifted off.

This is why you should read this book now. Not because something will happen to you today, but because if you wait until the day it happens to open it, the notebook you open should have been in someone else's hands.

## 1.3 The slide starts at thirty

Three things were already going downhill before you felt old.

**Muscle.** Muscle mass peaks between twenty and the late thirties, then starts to decline. One set of studies gives the numbers: after adulthood, men lose about 4.7% per decade and women about 3.7% (note: these figures come from research cited by the Hong Kong government's Department of Health, not a mainland population; for the mainland there is still no unified set of curves by age and by sex) [official wording: Hong Kong SAR Government, Department of Health]. What falls faster than muscle mass is muscle strength: in the same set of studies, men lose 14.5% per decade and women lose 11.8% [official wording: Hong Kong SAR Government, Department of Health]. In other words, you don't first get "thin" — you first run out of strength.

**Bone.** Bone keeps growing until around thirty, when bone mass reaches its highest point, called peak bone mass in medicine; after thirty to thirty-five, bone mass starts to decline [official wording: Beijing Municipal Health Commission]. The higher your peak bone mass, the later problems appear. Among people over fifty in China, the prevalence of osteoporosis is 19.2% — 32.1% in women and 6.9% in men [official wording: Beijing Municipal Health Commission].

**Heart and lungs.** The body's ability to use oxygen is usually measured by maximal oxygen uptake. It falls with age, and falls faster and faster: in your twenties and thirties it drops 3% to 6% per decade, and after seventy it drops more than 20% per decade [third-party research: Fleg et al., 2005].

Put the three together and they are the same sentence: it is not "getting old suddenly one day" but "a little less every year", accumulating to some number before it becomes visible.

## 1.4 Getting old is not one thing; it comes in four models

"Old" is too broad a word. In geriatric medicine, four things each have their own name; they do different things and take different paths.

- **Frailty**: the whole person's reserves get thin, and the capacity to withstand a blow is poor.
- **Sarcopenia**: the muscle, as an organ, is wasting away.
- **Multimorbidity**: not one disease, but a string of diseases existing at the same time.
- **Disability**: function has already been lost; help is needed.

These four push each other along, but if they are bundled into one "getting old", a signal that could still be caught and intervened on early gets taken for an unsalvageable outcome.

### Frailty: one small illness floors you

- **What it costs**: nothing in normal times; to get it properly checked you add one outpatient visit (Money: barely any money | Time: a few minutes | Effort: nothing | Do you have to speak up: no)
- **In short**: the same cold, the same minor operation — others ride it out, you don't, and you recover very slowly. This is frailty: not that some organ has broken, but that the whole person's margin has got thin.
- How to judge (against the numbers): ① without dieting, you have lost 5% of your weight in six months (someone weighing 100 jin loses 5 jin); ② you often feel weak and tired; ③ your grip strength has dropped — you can't twist open a bottle cap or carry groceries; ④ you walk more slowly than people your age; ⑤ your activity has clearly fallen off, and you don't want to move anything. Three of these five usually count as frailty; one or two of them is called "pre-frailty" [third-party research: Fried frailty phenotype].
- **Who this is for**: it holds for you if you can still get by on your own, just with a thin margin. Frailty does not equal being bedridden; it is the stretch before being bedridden, and part of that stretch can be walked back.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid that "one small illness and I never get up again"
### Sarcopenia: first you lose strength, then you lose power

- **What it costs**: nothing in normal times; if you want a confirmed diagnosis, add one outpatient visit plus tests
- **In short**: sarcopenia is not "thin". It means the amount and the strength of your muscle have fallen to a point where they already affect your walking, standing up, and carrying things.
- How to work it out: the Asian diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia (AWGS 2019) give several lines. Grip strength, below 28 kg for men and below 18 kg for women; walking speed, slower than 1.0 m/s; standing up from a chair five times in a row, over 12 seconds; calf circumference, below 34 cm for men and below 33 cm for women [third-party research: AWGS 2019]. What you can do at home first: measure your calf circumference, see whether twisting off a bottle cap is hard work, and count whether you need to push off with your hands to get up out of a chair.
- One number: the prevalence of sarcopenia among older people in China, pooling 22 papers, is about 14.6% [third-party research: meta-analysis, 2022]; looking at individual studies on their own, because the diagnostic criteria and the sampled populations differ, figures from single digits to thirty percent have been reported (8.9%–38.8% in community studies, reaching 67.1% among those over eighty) [third-party research: Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Sarcopenia in Older Adults, 2021].
- **Who this is for**: it holds for you if — your weight isn't necessarily low, you may look normal or even a bit heavy, but your muscle is quietly shrinking (this is called "sarcopenic obesity"). So don't judge by the scale.
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid that "my legs have no strength, I can't walk far or stand for long"
- **How this works in China**: to get assessed, register with geriatrics, rehabilitation medicine, or general practice; a contracted family doctor at a community health centre can also do an initial screening. Programmes and fees differ from place to place, so go by the latest local rules.

### Multimorbidity: not one disease but a string of them

- **What it costs**: no money, just the time for one extra visit (Do you have to speak up: easy enough, you just have to admit your illnesses aren't few)
- **In short**: having two or more chronic diseases at the same time is called multimorbidity. The trouble is not how severe any one disease is, but that they and their respective medicines fight each other.
- How to work it out: count the chronic diseases you have already been diagnosed with; two or more, and that is multimorbidity. Among people over sixty in China, the self-reported multimorbidity rate is about 43.6% (CHARLS 2015, 11,707 people) [third-party research]; if you also count the results of checkups and lab tests, the proportion is much higher — across the same dataset the 2011 and 2015 waves are 85.96% and 92.24% respectively [third-party research]. The two figures differ so widely only on "which diseases count".
- **Who this is for**: it holds for people with more than one illness and more than one medicine. The most practical single move for multimorbidity: each time you see a doctor, remind them once that "I'm still taking medicine prescribed for another condition" (see Chapter 6).
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid that "the more the illnesses are treated, the more there are, and the more medicine I take, the more muddled it gets"
### Disability: function is already gone

- **What it costs**: no money, just one conversation about care
- **In short**: among these six things — eating, dressing, bathing, using the toilet, getting in and out of bed, moving about indoors — if one of them needs help over the long term, that is disability; the more of them need help, the more severe it is.
- How to work it out: count how many of those six need help. Then go on to count the "instrumental" ones: going out to buy groceries, taking transport, paying bills, using a phone to get things done. In China, "being able to get things done on a phone" is also counted as part of independence (see Chapter 0).
- One number: according to the National Health Commission, China has more than 45 million older people who are disabled or have dementia, roughly one in every six older people needing long-term care [official wording: National Health Commission]. (A note on the scope: the 45 million is **disability plus dementia**; counting only disabled older people, there is another figure of about 35 million — see the sources for Chapter 4.)
- **Who this is for**: it holds for people who already need a hand. Once you reach this point, the focus of the conversation changes: it is no longer "how to prevent it", but "who will care for me, where the money comes from, how to keep my dignity" (turn to Part Four).
- **What you get back**: independence — no longer afraid that "one day I'll even need to be fed"
Remember the one distinction between the four words: **frailty is a thin margin; sarcopenia is muscle shrinking; multimorbidity is many diseases; disability is function lost.**

## 1.5 "Compression of morbidity": making the hard years shorter

In geriatric medicine there is a term called **compression of morbidity**. In 1980 the American physician James Fries proposed it [third-party research: Fries, 1980]: total lifespan has a ceiling; what you can do is push the period of illness and disability later and compress it shorter.

**Living long is lifespan; living well is health span** — these are two different numbers.

China's average life expectancy was 79 years in 2024, and 79.25 years at the end of 2025 [official wording: National Health Commission]. As for "the years you can get by on your own, without depending on others", the National Health Commission's annual bulletin does not carry that figure — the bulletin publishes only average life expectancy, and does not list "healthy life expectancy" separately; the World Health Organization has its own estimates for each country, but it uses a different method, so you cannot simply subtract one from the other. So this chapter does not lay out two numbers for you to subtract; it states one certain thing: for the extra years you live, whether you can get by on your own or are lying there needing care — the difference is right there.

If the extra years cannot be lived independently, then "longevity" becomes "a longer stretch of suffering". What compression of morbidity wants is for "two or three hard years" to take the place of "ten hard years".

## 1.6 Five misconceptions, the first is the most dangerous

### Misconception one: "It's just age, it's normal"

This sentence is the most expensive one. Some changes are aging, some are illness; sweeping them all under "getting old" amounts to letting a treatable illness drag on until it is untreatable.

The following things are often taken for "just age" when they should be investigated.

- Getting weaker and weaker, feeling cold, constipation, poor memory, low mood: could be hypothyroidism. In a survey of 78,470 cases across 31 provinces in China, subclinical hypothyroidism was the most common among older people, with a prevalence close to 20% [third-party research: Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Thyroid Disease in Older Adults, 2021].
- Dizziness, a pale face, breathlessness at the slightest movement: could be anaemia. Anaemia is common in older people, yet it is often taken for normal aging, or hidden behind other chronic diseases [official wording: Beijing Municipal Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine].
- Breathless after two steps, waking at night gasping, swollen ankles: could be heart failure.
- No energy, not wanting to do anything, waking early: could be depression, not something that "cheering up" will fix. Among middle-aged and older people aged 45 and over in China, the detection rate of depressive symptoms is 23.61% (CFPS, 2018) [third-party research: Journal of Sichuan University (Medical Sciences), 2021].
- Losing weight over six months, no appetite: get it checked; don't treat it as "getting thin in old age".

Remember a formulation that is hard to get wrong: **a single change that has appeared only in the last few months, and is still moving in the wrong direction, is not "slow aging" — it is something to be looked at.** Conversely, something that appears suddenly within days (can't speak clearly, weakness on one side, chest pain) is not a matter of "having it looked at", but a matter of calling 120 (see Chapter 8).

- **Who this is for**: everyone. This one does not depend on age.

### Misconception two: "You can't buy a thin old age for a thousand pieces of gold"

- **In short**: once you are old, losing weight is not necessarily a good thing — what you lose is mostly muscle. What you should watch is muscle, not the number on the scale.
- **What you can do**: stop looking only at weight. If you have lost more than 5% of your weight in six months (the threshold is in the frailty item in 1.4), see a doctor about why, rather than being pleased.

### Misconception three: brain supplements prevent dementia

- **In short**: so far, no health product has been proven to prevent dementia or improve memory. What actually helps the brain is controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, exercise and social contact, and fixing hearing problems.
- **What you can do**: if you're willing to spend money, spend it on these first; hold off on the supplements.

### Misconception four: "When you're old you should move less and rest"

- **In short**: the less you move, the faster muscle and heart-lung function fall away, and the more likely you are to fall. Deciding to stop moving because you're afraid of falling is the worst option (see Chapter 5).
- **What you can do**: start with what you can do, even if it's lifting your legs while sitting, or practising standing up while holding on to something.

### Misconception five: "After eighty, checkups are pointless"

- **In short**: it's neither do everything nor do nothing. What you should do is the handful of screenings picked out for your age, physical condition, and risk. Picking out that handful is exactly the job geriatrics and the checkup report should do (see Chapter 14).
- **What you can do**: replace "should I have this test" with "will this change how I'm treated"; if the answer is no, don't have it.

## 1.7 Who makes money off your fear

Aging is the topic easiest to harvest, and some people call it the "anxiety business". Why it is fertile ground: there are many people who are afraid, you don't understand, the seller does, and people's judgement drops when they are frightened; and people living alone have one fewer person to talk things over with.

To deal with it, this book has only one dumb method: **write a source for every item; and mark out separately every "fear that is exploited by sellers".**

### Expensive checkup packages, and "one drop of blood"

- **In short**: proper cancer screening is done separately, by risk, one cancer one test. Low-dose CT for the lungs, colonoscopy for the bowel, mammography or ultrasound for the breast. Anything that claims in one sentence to "screen for all cancers" or "screen for a hundred diseases" is selling a gimmick. The claim "one drop of blood tests for cancer" has been debunked repeatedly; the American company that made it its headline product, its founder was ultimately sentenced to 11 years 3 months in prison for fraud [official wording: U.S. Department of Justice announcement, 2022-11-18].
- How to spot it: when words like "test everything at once", "predict", "premium", "genetic" appear, put a question mark on them first. Renaming routine items and marking up the price is standard practice in this trade.
- What happens if you don't buy: you save a few thousand yuan and are missing nothing. But remember: don't save on the screenings you should have along with it. The few that really should be done are set by a doctor according to your situation.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "spending a bit buys peace of mind"
### Brain supplements and health-product seminars

- **In short**: in the cases published by the State Administration for Market Regulation, the chain of scams in older people's health products is a fixed four steps: free trials, manufacturing anxiety, false advertising, high-price sales [official wording: State Administration for Market Regulation].
- How to spot it: be on your guard against anyone who has you collect something free, sit through a seminar, join a group chat, or watch a livestream first. Many of those "the effect is really good, I'll order two more months" buyers in the group are shills. A common food or health product claiming to cure disease is itself illegal advertising.
- What happens if you don't buy: you save money and time. The treatment you should have won't be any less for it, and you were never relying on it for treatment in the first place.
- **What you get back**: money — no longer afraid that "I have to do something for my health"
### Housing-for-pension

- **In short**: the thing being piloted by the state is properly called "reverse mortgage pension insurance", an insurance product. The scam borrows the policy's name to get you to mortgage your home and take the money to invest in "high-interest wealth products" [official wording: National Financial Regulatory Administration risk notice].
- How to spot it: whenever someone wants you to **sign to mortgage your property in order to invest**, however much the name looks like a policy, it is a scam. In the legitimate version the house is still yours, you draw money monthly and it is dealt with after your death; the terms and rights must be asked through point by point.
- What happens if you don't buy: the house is still there, and you are still living in it. For most families this matters far more than the extra bit of interest.
- **What you get back**: money + dignity — no longer afraid that "there isn't enough money and I'll lose my life savings"
One general rule: **whenever someone wants you to "pay now, it's gone after today", stop, wait a day, and say it out loud to one person.** What a scam fears most is exactly that one day and that one person (see Chapter 18).

## 1.8 The one thing you can do today

Today, take a sheet of paper and write down one question, for yourself or for your parents:

> In the last three months, is there anything about me that has changed for the worse? Weight, strength, walking, memory, sleep, whichever.

If there is, make an appointment and get it checked; don't say to yourself "it's just age, it's normal". If there isn't, keep this sheet of paper, and ask yourself again three months later.

Ask yourself this one question, and you turn "getting old suddenly one day" into "I've been watching it all along".

## 1.9 If you are reading this for a parent

Of everything in this chapter about "how to say it", the line with the best chance of getting through is to state your own worry, not to tell them they can't cope.

- **How to say this out loud to them**: don't start from the fact that they are "old" — they will shut the door at once. Start from one small thing you have seen, and put it as your own worry: "I've noticed you need to hold the wall going up the stairs lately, and it makes me uneasy; I'd like to go with you to get it checked." Say what I see, not what you shouldn't be. You can also use this book as an opening: "I'm reading a book about preparing ahead, and there's one place I'd like to go through with you."
- **When they disagree, what else you can do**: there are three things that don't need their nod. ① Write down the "should-be-checked" items from 1.6 (no energy, feeling cold, dizziness, breathlessness, weight loss), and next time you have a chance to go with them to the doctor, tell the doctor about these. ② If they say checkups are pointless, don't push them to do everything; instead, ask the doctor which items are genuinely worth doing for their age and sex. ③ If they have bought an expensive package or health products, don't tear it up in front of them; first ask "who recommended this", then calmly tell them the "starts with a free trial" spotting method.

## 1.10 What I am not sure about in this chapter

- **There is no routine official figure for healthy life expectancy.** The National Health Commission's annual bulletin publishes only average life expectancy (79 years in 2024, 79.25 years at the end of 2025) and does not list separately "the years you can get by on your own, without depending on others"; the World Health Organization has estimates of healthy life expectancy for each country, but they use a different scope and method from the domestic bulletin, so they cannot be directly subtracted. This chapter therefore does not use that difference. Next step: if the National Health Commission brings it into its regular annual publication, add it then.
- **Prevalence varies with the diagnostic criteria, so only a range can be given.** Frailty: using the Fried phenotype, on the CHARLS 2011—2012 baseline, it is 7.0% among people over 60 [third-party research: Wu et al., 2018]; using the frailty index (FI) instead, for older people in China it was 18.7%, 20.6%, and 28.4% in 2011, 2013, and 2015 [third-party research: Chinese Journal of Epidemiology, 2018]. Sarcopenia: the meta-analysis gives 14.6%, while individual studies range from single digits to thirty percent. The difference lies in the diagnostic criteria, the sampled population, and "which diseases count" — whenever you see any single number, don't take it as a national verdict.
- **The list of things "taken for old age" is an example, not an exhaustive one.** The hypothyroidism, anaemia, heart failure, depression, and weight loss picked in 1.6 are the ones most often mistaken for aging; in geriatric medicine this list is open-ended, and there is no "one number" to check it against. The doctor's judgement, made with your specific situation, is what counts.

One honest thing: some changes really can't be turned back. Everyone comes at last to Stage Four, and no one can stop that. What this book can do is make the first three stages slower and steadier.
